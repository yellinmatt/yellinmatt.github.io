The Great Vowel Shift

English spelling was fixed in print while the vowels underneath it were still travelling, and the two have never lined up since.

Shakespeare closes Sonnet 116 on a couplet: If this be error and upon me proved, / I never writ, nor no man ever loved. Read it aloud today and the last two words sit beside each other without touching. That is a strange thing to find in a poet with a hundred and fifty-four of these in print, every one of them locked shut by a rhyming couplet. Proved and loved rhymed in 1609 and later stopped, and what happened in between is the largest single event in the history of spoken English.

Between roughly 1400 and 1700, with the heavy work done in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, every long stressed vowel in English changed the place in the mouth where it was made. Philologists call it the Great Vowel Shift. The consonants came through largely intact; Chaucer rolled his r’s and sounded both letters of kn, but a consonant stayed where it was. The short vowels came through nearly untouched, which is why bit and cut and hand would be recognisable to a Londoner of 1380. The long vowels went, and they went together, in one connected rearrangement of the whole system.

The mechanism is easier to feel than to read. A vowel is defined by where the tongue sits: say beet, then boot, then boat, then bought, and you can feel the tongue drop and draw back through four positions. Every long vowel in English stepped up one rung of that ladder. The two already at the ceiling, the ee of beet and the oo of boot, had nothing above them and split into two sounds each. So Chaucer’s myne, which had sounded like a modern meen, became mine; his hous, which had sounded like hoose, became house; his sheep had sounded like shape, and his name was two syllables with the vowel of father.

Nobody recorded any of this while it happened. The evidence comes instead from the people who found the result intolerable. In 1569 John Hart published An Orthographie, which proposed a new phonetic alphabet for English on the grounds that the letters no longer told a reader what to say. Hart and the spelling reformers who followed him set down in obsessive detail exactly what the writing was failing to capture, and their complaints are now the best surviving record of how the language actually sounded. Rhyme supplies the rest. A couplet that no longer closes is a dated measurement, and there are thousands of them.

Why the spelling never followed the sounds comes down to a date. William Caxton set up England’s first printing press in the Almonry at Westminster in 1476, squarely in the middle of the shift, and put out the Canterbury Tales twice, in 1476 and again in 1483. He worked largely from Chancery Standard, the London forms the government clerks already used, and print did what print does to a variable thing: it picked one and made it permanent. Two further centuries of change went on beneath a spelling that had already been set. English orthography is, near enough, a photograph of the language as it was said around 1450, and the whole English-speaking world has been reading off it ever since.

· · ·

All of which is easiest to hear in Chaucer, who wrote before any of it began. Give the long vowels their Italian or Spanish values, sound the final e, and the opening of the Canterbury Tales comes back into rhythm.

Watch David and Ben Crystal, The Open University, 2011

Ten minutes on the reconstruction the Globe used for its 1604-style Romeo and Juliet in June 2004, the first full Shakespeare production in original pronunciation for half a century. Listen for the moment Ben Crystal runs a couplet twice, once in a modern accent and once in the older one, and the rhyme snaps shut on the second pass. The accent that comes out is not the plummy stage voice anyone expects, which is a good measure of how far the language has gone.

The shift never finished cleanly. One vowel, the open e, set off with the others and stopped partway, which is why great and meat and threat carry the same two letters over three different sounds, and why ea is the least trustworthy pair in the language. Hart wanted that tidied up in 1569, and every reformer since has wanted the same thing. None of them could offer a replacement for the accident that came with the mess: a spelling old enough to predate the split between Edinburgh and Boston and Sydney, which is the reason all three can still read the same page.
