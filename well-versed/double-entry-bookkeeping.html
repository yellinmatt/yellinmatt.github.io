Finance · The Venetian Method

Double-Entry Bookkeeping

Enter every transaction twice, on opposite sides of the page, and the book will tell you when it is wrong.

At the Campo San Giacomo di Rialto, where Venice conducted its wholesale trade, remarkably little money changed hands. The galley fleets arrived and departed on a schedule the state set, prices moved with them and with whatever news came in by letter, and the men standing in that square bought and sold against each other on credit for months at a stretch. A merchant’s working capital was largely other merchants’ promises. Counting coin was the easy part. The hard thing was knowing, on any given morning, precisely who owed him what, and being able to show it when the other man remembered the figure differently.

The answer Venice settled on was a rule about writing. Every transaction enters the ledger twice, in equal amounts and opposite directions: the account that receives is made debtor, on the left, and the account that gives is made creditor, on the right. Selling a consignment of ginger against a promise moves goods out of the warehouse and a debt into the world, and the book records both halves as a matter of form. Do that without exception and a second property falls out for nothing. Every debit has its matching credit, so the two columns must come to the same total, and a book that will not balance is telling the man who keeps it that something in it is wrong before anyone he trades with finds out. A record that tests itself is a different instrument from a list.

The first printed account of the method sits inside a mathematics textbook. Luca Pacioli, a Franciscan friar out of Sansepolcro in Tuscany, came to Venice around 1464 to tutor a merchant’s three sons and took the first chair of mathematics at Perugia in 1477. Between the tenth and the twentieth of November 1494 the press of Paganino de’ Paganini brought out his Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalità, six hundred and sixteen pages of commercial mathematics. The ninth of its ten parts deals with business, and inside that part sits a short treatise, Particularis de computis et scripturis, running to some two dozen leaves. Pacioli claimed nothing as his own invention and said so; he called what he was describing the method of Venice. What he had that the Venetians did not was a press, and his treatise became the root of the nearly eight hundred editions of bookkeeping manuals Europe published between 1500 and 1800.

Nearly everyone quotes the treatise’s opening, and nearly everyone quotes it wrongly. Pacioli lists three things a man requires in order to be a true merchant, and in John Geijsbeek’s 1914 English, the version most of the world has read, the second is that he be a good bookkeeper. Alan Sangster and Fabio Santini went back to the 1494 Italian in 2022 and reported that all ten translations they examined, across five languages, are unreliable. Pacioli’s word is ragioneri, which in fifteenth-century usage meant a reckoner, what we would call a mathematician; its companion computista meant a calculator, someone fast at sums. Geijsbeek made the first of those a bookkeeper and three later translators made it an accountant, which is how a passage about commercial competence came to read as advice about accounting.

The third quality suffers worse. Che con bello ordine tutte sue facende debitamente disponga asks that the merchant keep his affairs in good order, meaning all of them: the letters, the bills of exchange, the promissory notes, the agreements, the record of what he has lent and borrowed. The word scripturis in the treatise’s own title referred precisely to the written records that are not the account books. Pacioli never says the merchant should keep the ledger himself, and most men trading at the Rialto employed somebody who could. What he could not hire out was the arithmetic: two moneys of account, several currencies in circulation, rates that moved with the fleets, weights that differed by commodity, freight and insurance and duty, all of it resolved into one number while a counterparty stood waiting in the square.

For close to three centuries the ledger did the work Venice had built it to do, and then an English potter turned it on a question nobody had asked it. By the end of 1769 Josiah Wedgwood was selling pottery across Europe and running out of money, with stock piling up and bills going uncollected. In 1772 he went through his own accounts to find out why. The pricing was haphazard, the production runs too short to be economical, and the cash was coming back too slowly to pay for the next batch. He also found something without a name. Modelling and moulds, rent, fuel and wages did not rise and fall with the number of pots. “Consider that these expenses move like clockwork,” he wrote to his partner Thomas Bentley, “and are much the same, whether the quantity of goods made be large or small.” Spread across a longer run they cost less per pot. The economic argument for mass production came out of a bookkeeping exercise undertaken to avoid bankruptcy.

The rest followed the money. Railways had to be built with capital raised from strangers, joint-stock companies had to show those strangers what had become of it, and by the 1860s British law wanted an accountant present at every stage of a company’s life. Financial statements, an incidental by-product of the books in 1800, had become the reason for keeping them a century later. Underneath all of it the Rialto’s smaller idea is still running: a record nobody can check is not evidence, and the cheapest check yet devised is a second copy of the same fact, entered where it will fail to agree if either copy is wrong.
