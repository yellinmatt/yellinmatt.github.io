The Metamorphosis

The word Kafka wrote for what Gregor Samsa wakes up as names no species, and he wrote to his publisher to keep the thing off the cover.

The emptiness in that doorway is the most deliberate thing on the cover. Kurt Wolff’s Leipzig house had written to Prague to say that Ottomar Starke, a stage designer who had drawn many of the jackets in the series, would do the title page, and Kafka wrote back on 25 October 1915 to report a small fright. Starke actually illustrates, he pointed out, so it had occurred to him that Starke might want to draw the insect itself. Das nicht, bitte das nicht! Not that, please not that. He was not trying to restrict the artist, he said, only to ask on the strength of his naturally better knowledge of the story. Then the sentence that governs everything after it: the insect itself cannot be drawn, and it cannot be shown even from a distance.

He did not stop at a prohibition. Having forbidden the one image he offered two others, and the second is the one Starke used: the parents and the sister in the lighted room while the door to the wholly dark adjoining room stands open. There is also a small tell in the letter. Writing privately to his publisher, Kafka twice uses the word Insekt, a plain taxonomic noun of the sort a man might use about a moth on the ceiling, and it is a word he had kept out of the story entirely.

The nineteenth word of the story is Ungeziefer, and the word standing in front of it is ungeheueren. Take the two apart. Ungeheuer is the negation of geheuer, which describes a place with nothing wrong in it, safe and familiar and quiet; unbuild that and you get something ranging across monstrous, uncanny and enormous without settling on any of them. Ungeziefer descends through Middle High German ungezibere from a root meaning a sacrificial animal, and it named the beast that could not be offered: unclean, unfit for the altar. The word grades a creature’s standing. It says nothing whatever about legs or wings or shell, and what it actually names is the thing in the house that has no business being in the house, which is why modern German still uses it for whatever you call the exterminator about.

IAls Gregor Samsa eines Morgens aus unruhigen Träumen erwachte, fand er sich in seinem Bett zu einem verwandelt. Er lag auf seinem harten Rücken und sah, wenn er den Kopf ein wenig hob, seinen gewölbten, braunen, von bogenförmigen geteilten Bauch, auf dessen Höhe sich die Bettdecke, zum gänzlichen Niedergleiten bereit, kaum noch erhalten konnte. Seine vielen, im Vergleich zu seinem sonstigen Umfang dünnen Beine flimmerten ihm hilflos vor den Augen.

One morning, when Gregor Samsa woke from troubled dreams, he found himself transformed in his bed into a horrible vermin. He lay on his armour-like back, and if he lifted his head a little he could see his brown belly, slightly domed and divided by arches into stiff sections. The bedding was hardly able to cover it and seemed ready to slide off any moment. His many legs, pitifully thin compared with the size of the rest of him, waved about helplessly as he looked.

German: Die Verwandlung, Kurt Wolff Verlag, Leipzig, as printed in the first edition. English: David Wyllie’s public-domain translation.

Every English translator has had to shut the door Kafka left standing open. Willa and Edwin Muir got there first, in 1933, and the phrase they chose, a gigantic insect, taught the story to the English-speaking world and put a bug in the mind of every reader who came after. Since then the two words have been rendered at least sixteen ways. Stanley Corngold in 1972 went for monstrous vermin, J. A. Underwood for giant bug, M. A. Roberts for gargantuan pest, and Michael Hofmann in 2007 for monstrous cockroach, which settles the question outright. Susan Bernofsky, translating for Norton in 2014, chose some sort of monstrous insect, and explains in her afterword that she put those three vague words in on purpose: she wanted the reader to meet Gregor’s body as blurredly as Gregor meets it.

· · ·

The most famous dissent belongs to Nabokov, who taught the story at Cornell and read it as a professional lepidopterist. He worked from details in the text and identified Gregor as a beetle, which disposes of the cockroach everybody pictures, and moreover a beetle carrying wings folded beneath that hard convex back, wings that could have taken him out of the flat at any point had he ever thought to try them. On the flyleaf of his teaching copy he drew the creature and wrote “just over three feet long.” The charwoman’s cheerful Mistkäfer, dung beetle, he set aside as her rough affection and not a classification. The reading is wonderfully attentive and it is answering a question the story has declined to accept.

It was written fast. Kafka began on 17 November 1912 and finished on 6 December, under three weeks of night work at the end of office days, and he reported his progress letter by letter to Felice Bauer in Berlin, whom he had met in August and would spend the next five years failing to marry. He was twenty-nine. On the night of 5 December he wrote to her: weep, dearest, weep, now the time for weeping has come; the hero of my little story died a while ago. The story ran in the October 1915 number of Die weißen Blätter and came out as a book that December, the longest thing Kafka ever considered finished and let out of the house. The manuscript is now in the Bodleian.

Consider what the refusal buys him. A creature you can see is a creature you can name, and a creature with a name has a size and a species and a set of instructions for getting rid of it, at which point the horror of the story belongs to the exterminator. Withhold the picture and the reader is left where Gregor is, inside a room whose door keeps being opened by people who look in and cannot say what they have seen. That is the arrangement Starke drew. His man in the dressing gown stands in the lighted half of it, and everything the book is about is behind him, in the dark, looking out.
