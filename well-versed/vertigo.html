Vertigo

Hitchcock’s 1958 disappointment gives its secret away to the audience long before it tells its hero, and fifty-four years later the critics voted it the greatest film ever made.

Saul Bass drew the poster in two flat colours: a black silhouette of a man pitching into a white spiral, the outline of a woman tumbling with him, the whole thing set on a field of orange-red. Across the top Paramount printed “Alfred Hitchcock’s Masterpiece,” and in 1958 almost nobody agreed. The film opened at the Stage Door Theater on Mason Street in San Francisco on 9 May, drew mixed notices and earned a good deal less than the studio had hoped; the New York Times found the plot “devilishly farfetched.” In 2012, 846 critics polled by Sight & Sound ranked it above Citizen Kane as the greatest film ever made. What carried it there is a decision Hitchcock very nearly lost: he lets the audience in on the truth long before the hero.

James Stewart plays Scottie Ferguson, a San Francisco detective who quits the force after a rooftop chase leaves him with a crippling fear of heights. A college acquaintance, Gavin Elster, hires him to trail his wife Madeleine (Kim Novak), who seems possessed by a great-grandmother who killed herself at twenty-six. Scottie follows her through the city, pulls her out of the bay beneath the Golden Gate Bridge and falls in love with her, then freezes on the stairs of a mission bell tower while she climbs on and falls to her death. Months later he meets a Kansas shopgirl named Judy Barton who looks like Madeleine, and he sets about dyeing her hair blonde and buying her the dead woman’s clothes until Madeleine stands in front of him again.

The source novel, Pierre Boileau and Thomas Narcejac’s D’entre les morts (Paris, 1954), saves its twist for the last pages: the woman the hero remakes turns out to be the woman he lost. Hitchcock moved it. Soon after Judy appears, she writes Scottie a letter, and a flashback shows us what it says: Elster hired her to play Madeleine, and the body that fell from the tower was his real wife. “If we don’t let them know, they will speculate,” Hitchcock explained on the tapes of his long interview with François Truffaut, adding that “one of the fatal things” in suspense “is to have a mind that is confused.” From that moment every scene of the makeover plays twice, as his obsession and as her dread, and when Stewart arrives to collect her, “the audience says, ‘Little does he know.’”

The scene almost did not survive. The American Film Institute’s catalogue records that it was cut after the first preview and went back in only because Barney Balaban at Paramount insisted. The case for it is the one Hitchcock made for his whole method with a bomb under a table: let the audience see the bomb and the clock, and a dull conversation becomes unbearable, so that, in his words, “they’re working for ten minutes, instead of being surprised for fifteen seconds.”

The other thing everybody remembers is a camera move. To put Scottie’s fear on screen at the tower, the crew built a model of the stairwell at one-tenth scale and pulled the camera back from it while zooming in, so the stairs hold their size in the frame as the space around them warps. It has been known as the Vertigo effect ever since.

Watch for the stair shot: the steps stay put while the drop beneath them seems to lengthen, which is the whole trick in a few seconds.

· · ·

Hitchcock owned the picture outright, and in the early 1970s he pulled it from circulation along with four others he owned: Rope, Rear Window, The Trouble with Harry and The Man Who Knew Too Much. For about a decade nobody could see it. It came back in 1983, after his death, and in 1996 Robert A. Harris and James C. Katz restored it in 70mm for more than a million dollars. The poll tracked its return. A runner-up in 1972, it tied for seventh in 1982, rose to fourth in 1992 and missed first by four votes in 2002; in 2012 it finished with 191 votes, thirty-four ahead of Kane, which had led every poll since 1962. In 2022, among 1,639 voters, it fell to second, seven votes behind Chantal Akerman’s Jeanne Dielman.

Now make the shot yourself. Stand a friend at one end of a long hallway, start a few paces from them with your phone camera at its widest, and walk slowly backward while you pinch to zoom in, keeping their face the same size on the screen. The face holds still; the hallway behind it crowds forward and bends. That queasy slide is what Hitchcock wanted the stairs to do to Scottie.

Keep the bomb test; it works on any story you tell, from a toast to a closing argument. Before you reach the turn, ask whether your listener knows something your hero does not. If not, you are buying fifteen seconds of surprise; let them in earlier and you buy ten minutes of suspense. My own view is that the letter is the best thing in the film, because it turns a mystery into a tragedy we watch with our hands over our eyes.

When Vertigo comes up, here is your line: Hitchcock tells you the truth before he tells James Stewart, the studio had to put that scene back after a preview, and it is the reason the critics finally ranked the film above Citizen Kane.
