Ulysses

For eleven years the book could not lawfully cross the American border, and the case that changed that turned on whether it made anyone want to go to bed.

In August 1920 John Sumner of the New York Society for the Suppression of Vice swore out a warrant against two women who edited a small magazine. Margaret Anderson and Jane Heap had been running a novel in The Little Review an instalment at a time since March 1918, and the July–August number carried the closing pages of a scene in which a man on a Dublin beach watches a girl lean back to see the fireworks. The Post Office had already stopped three earlier issues and burned one of them. This one went to court.

The trial came on in February 1921 before three judges. John Quinn appeared for the defence, and when the prosecution rose to read the passage aloud one of the judges proposed that the two defendants leave the room, to spare them the shock. Quinn objected that they had a right to hear the case against them. His own argument was that the prose was too difficult to corrupt anybody. It made people angry, he told the court; they want to break something. The judges convicted, fined the editors fifty dollars each, and ordered the serialisation stopped. Heap wrote afterwards that they limped from the field. The novel was two-thirds written.

No house in London or New York would take it after that, so the book was finished and printed in Paris by a bookseller. Sylvia Beach ran Shakespeare and Company, an English-language shop on the Left Bank, and in April 1921 she agreed to publish a novel she had no experience whatever of publishing. She funded it by subscription and sent it to Maurice Darantiere in Dijon, whose typesetters handled a quarter of a million words of English none of them could read. Joyce wanted the covers in the blue and white of the Greek flag and the book out on his fortieth birthday. Darantiere put the first copies on a train, and they reached Paris on the morning of 2 February 1922.

The edition ran to a thousand numbered copies on three grades of paper: a hundred signed by Joyce on Dutch handmade at 350 francs, a hundred and fifty on vergé d’Arches at 250, and seven hundred and fifty on handmade paper at 150, making a book of 732 pages. Jack Dalton later counted more than two thousand errors in it, and separating Darantiere’s mistakes from the ones Joyce meant has kept editors quarrelling ever since.

What is inside takes one day. Leopold Bloom, an advertising canvasser, leaves his house on Eccles Street on the morning of 16 June 1904, goes about his business in Dublin, and comes home late; Stephen Dedalus crosses the same city on the same day; Molly Bloom lies awake at the end of it. The eighteen episodes run in parallel to the Odyssey, so a newspaper office answers to Aeolus and a cabmen’s shelter at one in the morning to Eumaeus. Those Homeric names appear nowhere in the printed book. They come from two schemas Joyce drew up privately, one sent to Carlo Linati in 1920 and one that Stuart Gilbert published, so a reader who refers to the Circe episode is quoting the author’s notes.

· · ·

The date is the one thing in the book that was never invented. On 16 June 1904 Joyce and Nora Barnacle walked out together to Ringsend, the first time the two of them had gone anywhere alone, and he gave his novel the afternoon his life turned. He knew people were keeping it by June 1924, when he mentioned the day in a letter. Dublin has marked it every year since the fiftieth anniversary in 1954, when John Ryan and Brian O’Nolan set off in two horse-drawn cabs to follow the route and gave up before the end.

Random House bought the American rights on the bet that the ban could be broken, and its lawyer Morris Ernst built the case he wanted. He had a copy shipped from Paris and made certain customs seized it, which put the object itself in the dock under the Tariff Act of 1930 and gave the file its title, United States v. One Book Called “Ulysses.” Before the copy travelled he had reviews pasted inside the covers, so the evidence of the novel’s standing arrived as part of the exhibit.

Judge John M. Woolsey read all 732 pages and gave his decision on 6 December 1933. He put aside the rule then in force, under which a book could be condemned on its worst page and on its effect upon the most susceptible reader, and asked what the whole of it did to a person of average sexual instincts. In spite of its unusual frankness, he wrote, he did not detect anywhere the leer of the sensualist. Then the sentence everybody quotes: whilst in many places the effect of the book on the reader undoubtedly is somewhat emetic, nowhere does it tend to be an aphrodisiac. The Second Circuit affirmed the next year, Augustus Hand writing, and Random House published it in New York.

The useful thing to know about the novel is that nobody is required to take it whole. He wrote it an episode at a time and they come apart cleanly, which is why most of the people who talk about it with any pleasure have read four or five. Start with Hades, in which Bloom rides out to a funeral at Glasnevin in a carriage of men who do not much want him there and thinks about his father and his son, both dead. It runs about twenty pages and it is as good as anything written in English in the last century.
