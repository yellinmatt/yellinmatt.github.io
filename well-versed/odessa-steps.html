What a director did with a staircase, and why the record of 1905 does not match it

The Odessa Steps

The killing in Odessa in June 1905 was real and enormous, and it did not happen on the stairs; what Eisenstein built there instead is the reason film is edited the way it is.

A flight of granite in Odessa falls a hundred and forty-two metres from the boulevard to the harbour, dropping twenty-seven metres across ten landings. Francesco Boffo and two architects from St Petersburg built it between 1837 and 1841, and they built a deception into it. The stair is twelve and a half metres wide at the top and nearly twenty-two at the bottom, so anyone looking up from the port sees edges that appear parallel and a climb that appears to have no end. Two hundred steps were cut originally; the port took eight of them in 1933 and a hundred and ninety-two remain. Long before a camera was pointed at it, this was an instrument for making a short distance feel enormous.

On 14 June 1905 the crew of the Black Sea battleship Potemkin refused meat they said was crawling with maggots. An officer shot the sailor who had spoken for them, Grigory Vakulinchuk, and the crew answered by killing the officer, the captain and several others, and raising a red flag. The ship reached Odessa at six the next morning, and Vakulinchuk’s body was laid out on the mole under a handwritten note saying he had been killed for telling the truth. People came down with food and flowers all day, and by evening the gathering had turned into speeches and then into looting and fire. Around midnight the troops opened fire on the crowd penned in the port. The Times reported the dead in the hundreds; later accounts ran to two thousand. The staircase stood a short walk above all of this and carried none of it.

Twenty years later the All-Russian Central Executive Committee wanted a jubilee picture for the anniversary. Nina Agadzhanova wrote a script in eight episodes meant to cover the whole of 1905. The committee approved it on 4 June 1925 and the film had to be finished by December, so Sergei Eisenstein, who was twenty-seven and had made one feature, threw away seven episodes, took his unit to Odessa and shot the mutiny alone. It opened at the Bolshoi Theatre on 21 December 1925, runs seventy-four minutes, and is cut into five parts, the fourth of which carries a title card reading The Odessa Steps.

His theory of editing is the thing to carry away. Montage, in his formulation, is an idea that arises from the collision of independent shots, in which each element is perceived “not next to the other, but on top of the other.” A rifle and a face are two facts; set them against each other and the viewer supplies a third that is in neither. The staircase handed him a ruler for exactly that. His soldiers come down as one rigid horizontal line, in step, rifles level, faces never shown. The crowd below is scatter: a mother, a boy, an old woman in pince-nez, each of them an individual in a separate frame. He cuts between the order above and the disorder beneath it, and lets the descent run far longer than walking it would take, because the screen time is measuring pressure rather than distance.

One moment has travelled further than the film. A woman wheeling a pram is hit, her hands come off the handle, and the carriage starts down the treads by itself while the rifles keep coming. Brian De Palma restaged it in Union Station in The Untouchables in 1987 and assumed, correctly, that the audience would know what he was quoting; Francis Bacon took the open screaming mouth of the woman in pince-nez into his paintings. The pram is the clearest case of the whole method, because a baby in danger says nothing about tsarism until the cut makes it say so.

Governments understood what they were looking at. Joseph Goebbels called it “a marvellous film without equal” and warned that anyone without firm convictions could come out of it a Bolshevik; Heinrich Himmler forbade the SS to see it. The British Board of Film Censors refused it a certificate, so it reached Britain in 1929 only as a private society screening and was not passed for public exhibition until 1954, the longest ban any film has carried there.

Its standing has moved more than most. The first Sight and Sound critics’ poll, in 1952, placed it fourth with sixteen votes, behind Bicycle Thieves. Six years later a jury at the Brussels World’s Fair called it the best film ever made. In the 2022 poll, with more than sixteen hundred voters, it came fifty-fourth, which measures how completely the rest of cinema absorbed the technique. In 1955, for the fiftieth anniversary of the mutiny, Odessa renamed the Richelieu Steps the Potemkin Stairs. Ukraine restored the older name of Primorsky after independence in 1991, and visitors still ask for the Potemkin Stairs, which is the ordinary fate of a place a film has described better than its own history managed to.
