CPR

Pressing on a stranger’s breastbone a hundred times a minute keeps blood moving to the brain until a shock or an ambulance arrives, and the method began with a laboratory dog at Johns Hopkins in 1958.

In 1958 a dog went into cardiac arrest in a laboratory at the Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, and the defibrillator that might bring it back was seven floors away. Guy Knickerbocker, a doctoral student in electrical engineering, went to fetch it. While he was gone his colleagues kept pressing on the animal’s chest, for the reason he gave years later: “We started to pump the dog’s chest because it seemed to be the right thing to do.” He was away about twenty minutes. Two shocks revived the dog, which meant the pressing had kept enough blood moving through those twenty minutes to leave a living animal for the current to restart.

Knickerbocker had already seen a hint of it on the pressure trace. He worked for William Kouwenhoven, an electrical engineer and Hopkins professor who had been studying the fibrillating heart in dogs since the 1930s, and in their defibrillation experiments the weight of the electrodes on a dog’s chest raised its arterial pressure. With James Jude, a surgical resident, they found that two hands pushing down on the breastbone squeeze a stopped heart and force blood out of it, and that doing so about once a second could keep a circulation going until a shock or a hospital took over. The only cardiac massage medicine knew until then was the open kind, reported since 1901, in which a surgeon cut through the chest wall and squeezed the heart in his hand, a procedure that belonged in an operating theatre and nowhere else. The first person to get the closed version was a woman of thirty-five whose heart stopped under anaesthesia before a gallbladder operation. Within two minutes she had a beat again; she had her operation and recovered fully.

The three men published “Closed-Chest Cardiac Massage” in the Journal of the American Medical Association on 9 July 1960. Its conclusion handed the procedure from surgeons to everyone else.

Anyone, anywhere, can now initiate cardiac resuscitative procedures. All that is needed are two hands.

“Closed-Chest Cardiac Massage,” JAMA 173, no. 10 (9 July 1960).

Compression was half a method. Breathing had a separate history: in 1954 the anaesthesiologist James Elam confirmed that the air a rescuer breathes out can safely keep another person breathing, and Peter Safar, an Austrian-born anaesthesiologist, built mouth-to-mouth into a drill anyone could learn. When the Hopkins group and Elam spoke together to the Maryland Medical Society in 1960, they told the doctors that chest massage and mouth-to-mouth “cannot be considered any longer as separate units, but as parts of a whole and complete approach to resuscitation.” The name records the merger: cardio for the heart, pulmonary for the lungs, both worked by hand.

Teaching it raised a practical difficulty. The force that moves blood also breaks ribs, so live volunteers were out, and Safar went looking for a model. He found Åsmund Laerdal, a Norwegian toymaker who knew soft plastics from his toys and model cars, and who a few years earlier had pulled his small son from the water and pressed on the boy’s ribcage until the water came out. Laerdal wanted the mannequin to look calm, nothing a trainee would recoil from, and he took its face from a plaster mask sold in reproduction since the early twentieth century as the Inconnue de la Seine, the unknown woman of the Seine. Legend made her a girl pulled drowned from the river in Paris; no record in the police archives bears it out. Resusci Anne went on sale in 1960, and Laerdal Medical now estimates that she and her successors have trained more than 500 million people. The phrase trainees are taught to say as they shake her shoulder, “Annie, are you OK?”, is said to have reached Michael Jackson in a first-aid class and come out in “Smooth Criminal.”

What a bystander is asked to do now is simpler than what Safar taught. On 31 March 2008 the American Heart Association advised in Circulation that anyone who sees an adult suddenly collapse should call 911 and press on the chest without stopping, leaving out the breaths. Michael Sayre, who chaired the statement, gave the reason: people nearby so often hold back “because they’re afraid that they will hurt the victim.” The instructions fit in a breath. Place both hands, one on top of the other, on the centre of the chest and push hard and fast, 100 to 120 times a minute and at least two inches deep (5 centimetres, though no more than 2.4 inches), letting the chest come all the way back up between pushes. Keep on until a defibrillator arrives or a paramedic takes over. Breaths still matter for children and infants, and for anyone whose heart stopped for lack of oxygen, as in a drowning or an overdose.

Holding that pace without a metronome is the hard part, and the usual remedy is a disco record. David Matlock and colleagues at the University of Illinois had volunteers practise to the Bee Gees’ “Stayin’ Alive,” which runs at about 103 beats a minute, and reported to the American College of Emergency Physicians that weeks afterwards they could keep the rhythm by thinking of the tune.

Watch American Heart Association, with Ken Jeong

The Heart Association’s own short demonstration of the two steps. Watch for how little it asks: a call for help and a steady push in the middle of the chest, with no pulse check and no breaths.

The numbers explain why the drill is aimed at ordinary people. More than 350,000 Americans a year have a cardiac arrest outside hospital, and 73.4 per cent of those arrests happen at home, which makes the likeliest person to need these hands someone the rescuer lives with. In the latest figures the Heart Association reports, 40.2 per cent received CPR from a bystander, and among adults treated by paramedics, 9.1 per cent left hospital alive. Started at once, the Association says, CPR can double or triple the chance of surviving. Knickerbocker, who died in 2022 at eighty-nine, put the whole discovery in a sentence: “We had found a way to slow down the dying process and give people time to receive defibrillation.”
