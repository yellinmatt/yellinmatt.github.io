The Bespoke Suit

A trade body and an advertising regulator define the same word two different ways, and the gap between them is about fifty hours of hand-sewing.

Nine men sit along wooden benches beneath the windows of a first-floor room off Savile Row, a coat across each pair of knees, a needle in each hand. The photograph was taken in 1944, at Henry Poole & Co., in the fifth year of a war. There is not a machine in the picture. That absence is the whole of the argument about what the word bespoke means, and it is an argument the Row has been losing for about twenty years.

The street had been laid out two centuries earlier with no thought of tailoring in it. Savile Row went up between 1731 and 1735 as part of the Burlington Estate, and the 3rd Earl of Burlington named it for his wife, Lady Dorothy Savile. It filled with military officers and politicians, William Pitt the Younger among them, and stayed residential for the better part of a hundred years. The tailors followed the customers, settling first in the streets around Mayfair and then on the Row itself, and the house usually credited with arriving first is Henry Poole & Co., which in 1846 opened a proper shopfront onto the Row out of the workshops behind its premises in Old Burlington Street.

Poole is called the founder of Savile Row and is better remembered for a single commission. In 1865 the Prince of Wales, later Edward VII, asked for a short coat to wear at informal dinners at Sandringham, and Poole cut down the tailcoat and delivered the result in celestial blue. The house ledgers run back to 1846 and record nothing like it earlier. The founding members of the Tuxedo Club outside New York, William Waldorf Astor, Robert Goelet, Ogden Mills and Pierre Lorillard, were all Poole customers in the 1860s, and the garment crossed the Atlantic with them and took the club’s name. The livelier version, in which one James Potter is sent to Poole to ask what a man wears to dine with the Prince, has a problem: no client of that name appears anywhere in the ledgers.

What Poole sold, and what the houses of the Savile Row Bespoke Association sell now, is defined by process. A bespoke suit begins as a paper pattern drafted for one body by a master cutter, refined across the fittings, and kept afterwards for the next commission. The association’s own standard requires the forepart of the jacket to be hand-canvassed, the buttonholes sewn and the sleeves set and the linings felled by hand, the garment made within a hundred yards of the Row, and a minimum of fifty hours of hand work spread across a series of fittings. Made-to-measure starts from a standard block pattern, adjusts it, and skips the handwork. Ready-to-wear picks the nearest of a dozen sizes and hopes.

Simon Crompton, who writes Permanent Style, once had two jackets made in the same cloth, one bespoke and one made-to-measure, and photographed them side by side. His warning about the exercise is the useful part of it. The advantages he cares about do not photograph. An armhole cut tight with a generous sleeve set into it is what lets an arm rise without dragging the whole jacket up with it, and a collar shaped to the neck holds its place when the wearer turns. The hand-padded chest and lapel show up only in the way the lapel rolls, long and smooth, down to the waist button. Crompton is also candid that the collar on his own bespoke jacket stood away a little, and that is the finding worth carrying: cutting by hand creates the possibility of a perfect fit and guarantees nothing, and a tailor’s second jacket for a customer is usually better than his first.

In 2008 a tailor on Bond Street called Sartoriani advertised its suits as bespoke. It took a customer’s measurements in London and sent them to a factory in Germany, where the suit was made partly by machine and could be altered once on its return. Somebody complained to the Advertising Standards Authority. The Row expected the complaint to be upheld.

ReadTwo definitions of one word

The tradeBespoke – a suit made on or around Savile Row, bespoken to the customer’s specifications. A bespoke suit is cut by an individual and made by highly skilled individual craftsmen. The pattern is made specifically for the customer and the finished suit will take a minimum of 50 hours of hand work and require a series of fittings.

Made-to-measure – garment made to a customer’s individual requirements, to some extent, but not necessarily by hand.

The regulatorWe considered that customers would expect a bespoke suit to be tailored to their measurements and specifications. We considered that the majority of people, however, would not expect that suit to be fully handmade with the pattern cut from scratch.

Savile Row Bespoke Association, “Tailoring Terms”; Advertising Standards Authority adjudication on Sartoriani London, June 2008, as quoted in Forbes.

The reasoning turned on usage. The Oxford English Dictionary gives bespoke as made to order, which is exactly what Sartoriani was doing, and the ASA took the public’s understanding of the word as the standard an advertisement has to meet. Mark Henderson, then chairman of the association, called the decision shoddy and said that a perfectly good word was being undermined. He had no route of appeal, because under the ASA’s rules only the original complainant may appeal and nobody knew who had complained. The word itself is older than the quarrel, and what it originally described was an act of the customer’s: bespoke as an adjective dates to about 1755, from the verb bespeak, to speak for a thing in advance.

The trade kept its own vocabulary regardless. An armhole is a scye, from arm’s eye. A pattern draft carrying so many alterations that the lines cross is a Clapham Junction. An unclaimed garment is a pig. Cutting by instinct built up over years rather than by a measured system is working by rock of eye, and that phrase gets closest to what was actually at stake in 2008, since the thing being defended was a judgement no pattern block holds.

The Row’s answer since has been to go on quoting the process and the price. A two-piece suit at Huntsman, at number 11 since 1919, starts at £7,500 and runs to a minimum of three fittings. When it is done the cutter’s name goes inside the jacket, on the assumption that the customer will be back and that somebody will need to know whose pattern it was.
