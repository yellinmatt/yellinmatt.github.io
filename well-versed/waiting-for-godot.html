Waiting for Godot

Beckett’s play about two men waiting for someone who never comes emptied a Miami theater billed as a comedy, then found its truest audience inside San Quentin.

On 3 January 1956 the Coconut Grove Playhouse in Miami gave the American premiere of a French play that its producer, Michael Myerberg, had advertised as “the laugh sensation of two continents.” The house had come to see Bert Lahr, the Cowardly Lion of The Wizard of Oz, alongside Tom Ewell, under the direction of a young Alan Schneider. What it got was two shabby men beside a bare tree on a country road, waiting for someone called Godot, who sends a boy at nightfall to say he will surely come tomorrow. More than half the audience left at the interval. The next morning, Lahr’s son John later wrote, people were lined up at the box office to give their tickets back. The run lasted two weeks, and the New York booking was cancelled.

Myerberg had a half-excuse in the title page. Samuel Beckett, a Dubliner who had settled in Paris, wrote En attendant Godot in French between 9 October 1948 and 29 January 1949, as a relief from the novels he was stuck in, and subtitled it “a tragicomedy in two acts.” Four years passed before anyone would stage it. Roger Blin finally opened it on 5 January 1953 at the Théâtre de Babylone, a small house on the Left Bank, and took the part of Pozzo himself after the actor he had cast walked out.

Its shape is simple to tell and hard to sit through. Vladimir and Estragon, two tramps and old companions, pass an evening by the tree. They squabble over boots and turnips and consider hanging themselves. A landowner named Pozzo crosses the stage driving his servant Lucky on a rope, and Lucky, ordered to think aloud, pours out a speech of learned-sounding phrases that collapses into nonsense. Then the boy arrives with his message. The second act begins “Next day. Same time. Same place,” and almost everything recurs, a little worse: Pozzo has gone blind, Lucky has lost his voice, and the boy says he never saw them before. The only change in the landscape is that the tree has put out four or five leaves. Reviewing the play in the Irish Times on 18 February 1956, the critic Vivian Mercier summed it up in five words that have followed it ever since: a play in which “nothing happens, twice.”

Mercier’s joke holds a real observation about how the play is built. Each act ends on the same four beats, and Beckett hands the lines to the other man the second time round.

End of Act I
ESTRAGON: Well, shall we go?
VLADIMIR: Yes, let’s go.
They do not move.

End of Act II
VLADIMIR: Well? Shall we go?
ESTRAGON: Yes, let’s go.
They do not move.

Samuel Beckett, Waiting for Godot: A Tragicomedy in Two Acts, Beckett’s own English translation (New York: Grove Press, 1954). Quoted briefly for study.

Each closing exchange is cancelled by its own stage direction. The men agree to leave and stay put, and the audience, which has spent two hours watching them wait for a promise to be kept, is left doing the same thing in the dark. Beckett refused every invitation to say what the waiting meant. When Ralph Richardson, weighing a part in the London production, pressed him about Pozzo’s past, Beckett replied that all he knew about Pozzo “was in the text, that if I had known more I would have put it in the text.” Asked whether Godot stood for God, he said that had he meant God he would have written God.

London got the English version first, in August 1955, at the small Arts Theatre, directed by a twenty-four-year-old Peter Hall. Philip Hope-Wallace of the Guardian called it “the sort of rubbish we used to see in small cellars in Berlin in the 1920s,” and the run would have ended after four performances had Hall not persuaded the management to hold on until the Sunday papers came out. Harold Hobson in the Sunday Times and Kenneth Tynan in the Observer both came out for it. Tynan wrote that the evening had forced him “to re-examine the rules which have hitherto governed the drama; and, having done so, to pronounce them not elastic enough.” The production moved to the Criterion in the West End and stayed eight months.

After Miami the production was recast and reached Broadway on 19 April 1956 at the John Golden Theatre, with Lahr still as Estragon, E. G. Marshall as Vladimir and Herbert Berghof directing; that cast recorded the whole play. Listen to how Lahr plays the opening minutes, as a vaudeville double act working an empty stage, and to how long the silences are allowed to run.

The audience that understood it best came the following year. On Tuesday, 19 November 1957, the Actor’s Workshop of San Francisco, directed by Herbert Blau, played Godot for about 1,400 inmates in the North Dining Hall of San Quentin State Prison. The choice was partly practical, since the prison allowed no women performers and the play needs four men, a boy and a couple of rocks. Blau went out before the curtain and told the men to take it the way they would take jazz and listen for whatever they found. They stayed to the end. The San Quentin News reported that the visiting company “had its audience of captives in its collective hand,” and Beckett counted that notice among his favourites. Martin Esslin opened The Theatre of the Absurd (1961), the book that gave the movement its name, with the story of that night: a play that had baffled sophisticated audiences, he wrote, “was immediately grasped by an audience of convicts.” The assistant director that evening, Alan Mandell, went on to play Estragon himself; he died in Los Angeles this month, at ninety-eight.

In 1998 a poll run by London’s Royal National Theatre named Godot the most significant English-language play of the twentieth century, a striking result for a work Beckett wrote first in French. My own view is that Miami and San Quentin saw the same play and the prisoners read it correctly. A man serving a sentence knows that one day can repeat the last almost exactly, that a promised visitor can be put off until tomorrow, and that the companion beside you is how the hours get passed. The jokes in the script are real, and Myerberg’s poster sat nearer the truth than his opening night suggested; Lahr’s clowning lands for any audience willing to sit through the evening alongside the two men on stage.
