The P-Trap

Every sink in the house holds back the sewer with a few inches of standing water, and the pipe that keeps it there comes apart by hand.

Open the cabinet under a kitchen sink and follow the drain with your finger. It drops from the basin, dips into a U, climbs back up and turns sideways into the wall. Water wants to fall, and here the pipe makes it rise, which looks like poor design until you notice what stays behind. After every use two to four inches of water sit in the bottom of the curve, and that plug is what separates your kitchen from the sewer on the far side of the wall.

The idea came from a watchmaker. Alexander Cumming, a Scot working in London, had already built George III a clock that recorded air pressure against time when, on 11 November 1775, he took out patent number 1105 for a water closet. Drains until then gave smells an open road back into the house. His specification staked its claim on three things.

ReadAlexander Cumming, patent 1105, 1775 · tap the dotted words for a gloss

…the shape of the pan or , the manner of admitting the water into it, and on having the so constructed that its contents shall or may be totally emptied every time the is used.

As quoted by The National Archives (UK), catalogue C 54/6441

That last clause is the clever part. A bend full of stale water would soon stink on its own, so each flush had to push the old contents out and leave clean water resting in the curve. Cumming drew his bend as an S, down, up and over, then down again.

Britain did not require traps until after the Great Stink of 1858, when the Thames in a hot summer made the argument for them. The next change of shape is usually credited to Thomas Crapper, who around 1880 reworked the S into the U-bend. Give him that and no more, since the Science Museum says he clearly did not invent the toilet, which in its words had “no single ‘inventor’ who could claim the title.” Add an elbow and a level run to the far side of a U and the fitting looks like a P lying on its back, which is where today’s name comes from.

The level run decides whether the seal survives. Send a full sink down an S and the falling column of water pulls the last of the seal along with it, the same siphon that empties a gas tank through a hose, and the bend is left dry. A P-trap carries the water sideways to the point where the drain turns down, and a vent joins there, a pipe that climbs through the roof. Air enters at that junction and breaks the siphon. S-traps are prohibited in new construction for this reason, though the codes often tolerate old ones that give no trouble. You can diagnose the problem by ear, because a gurgle as the sink finishes draining usually means a poorly vented fixture.

Two other failures are yours to handle. A sewer smell from a bathroom nobody uses generally means the water has evaporated, and a little running water restores the seal. The second is the clog, because the low point that holds water also collects hair and grease. Clearing it takes a bucket and, at most, a pair of pliers.

  1. Plunge first. If the sink stays stopped after a few rounds, open the cabinet.
  2. Set a bucket under the trap. Unscrew the two slip nuts by hand, and use slip-joint pliers only if they refuse.
  3. Empty and scrub the trap. A wire or bottle brush does the job. Rinse it at a different sink, since the one above you now drains straight into your lap.
  4. Check the washers. They should be soft and supple. Replace any that have gone hard. If the trap was clean, feed a small drain snake into the pipe in the wall.
  5. Refit it. Thread each nut by hand so it starts true, seat the washers straight, and stop at hand-tight.
  6. Test it. Fill the sink, lay a paper towel under the trap and pull the plug. If the towel gets wet, tighten a little at a time until the leak stops. A persistent drip usually means grit under a washer or a twisted one, so take the joint apart again.
Watch Richard Trethewey, This Old House

This is the bigger version of the job, replacing a whole kitchen trap, with the same bucket underneath and the same slip joints at the sink.

Know where the job stops being yours. Nuts that crumble when you touch them call for a new trap, and This Old House prices a plastic replacement at $15 to $20 in parts. If every drain in the house runs slowly, the trouble is in the main line, and that is a call to a plumber. So is turning an S-trap into a proper P-trap, because the new run has to reach a vent, often through a wall. An air admittance valve can sometimes stand in for the vent, where local code allows one. The next time a sink smells, then, you have three questions to ask before anyone sends an invoice: is there water in the bend, does the drain gurgle, and what letter is the pipe?
