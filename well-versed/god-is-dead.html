God Is Dead

The most quoted sentence in modern philosophy is given to a lunatic carrying a lit lantern in daylight, and the phrase itself came to Nietzsche out of a Lutheran funeral hymn.

A man lights a lantern on a bright morning, carries it into the market-place, and calls out that he is looking for God. The crowd standing there does not believe in God, so the search strikes them as funny. Has he lost his way like a child? Has he gone to sea? The laughter is the design of the scene. Nietzsche hands the most famous sentence in modern philosophy to a lunatic with a lamp lit at the wrong hour, in front of unbelievers who cannot think what he is shouting about, and titles the section Der tolle Mensch, the madman. It is number 125 of Die fröhliche Wissenschaft, published in the late summer of 1882.

The madman aims the announcement at the unbelievers. He skips the argument for atheism entirely and tells the people who already agree with him that they have done something: we have killed him, you and I, and we are all his murderers. The event he reports is the collapse of a load-bearing assumption. Five years later, adding a fifth book to the second edition, Nietzsche said it without the theatre: the most important of recent events is “that the belief in the Christian God has become unworthy of belief,” and so much of European morality had been built on that belief, and had grown into one thing with it, that the demolition would run longer than anyone could yet imagine.

The insane man jumped into their midst and transfixed them with his glances. “Where is God gone?” he called out. “I mean to tell you! We have killed him,—you and I! We are all his murderers! But how have we done it? How were we able to drink up the sea? Who gave us the ? What did we do when we loosened this earth from its sun? Whither does it now move? Whither do we move? Away from all suns? Do we not dash on unceasingly? Backwards, sideways, forwards, in all directions? Is there still an above and below?”

“Do we not stray, as through infinite nothingness? Does not empty space breathe upon us? Has it not become colder? Does not night come on continually, darker and darker? Shall we not have to light lanterns in the morning? Do we not hear the noise of the grave-diggers who are burying God? Do we not smell the divine ?—for even Gods putrefy! God is dead! God remains dead! And we have killed him! How shall we console ourselves, the most murderous of all murderers?”

“The holiest and the mightiest that the world has hitherto possessed, has bled to death under our knife,—who will wipe the blood from us? With what water could we cleanse ourselves? What , what sacred games shall we have to devise? Is not the magnitude of this deed too great for us? Shall we not ourselves have to become Gods, merely to seem worthy of it?”

Here the madman was silent and looked again at his hearers; they also were silent and looked at him in surprise. At last he threw his lantern on the ground, so that it broke in pieces and was extinguished. “I come too early,” he then said. “This prodigious event is still on its way, and is travelling,—it has not yet reached men’s ears. Lightning and thunder need time, the light of the stars needs time, deeds need time, even after they are done, to be seen and heard. This deed is as yet further from them than the furthest star,—and yet they have done it!” It is further stated that the madman made his way into different churches on the same day, and there intoned his . When led out and called to account, he always gave the reply: “What are these churches now, if they are not the tombs and monuments of God?”

The sentence itself has a pedigree Nietzsche never claimed. Jean Paul had published a dream-vision in 1797, inside the novel Siebenkäs, under the chapter heading “The Dead Christ Proclaims That There Is No God.” Gérard de Nerval put Dieu est mort into a sonnet in 1854, and Victor Hugo quoted him saying it aloud in Les Misérables eight years after that. Behind all three stands a seventeenth-century Lutheran funeral hymn by Johann Rist in which God himself lies dead, Gott selbst liegt tot, a line about Good Friday that any Christian can sing without discomfort. Hegel quoted that hymn in his lectures on the philosophy of religion and made it a proposition about the divine nature. The words reached German through the liturgy. Nietzsche’s addition was the consequence.

He had sketched the consequence seventeen sections earlier, in five lines. After the Buddha was dead, people went on showing his shadow in a cave for centuries, and given the way the human race is built there will be caves for millennia yet in which it is still displayed. We have still to overcome it, Nietzsche ends. What gets cast on that wall is the moral ranking: humility above pride, pity above hardness, truth above usefulness, every one of them derived from the belief and every one still in working order after the belief has gone. On this account a civilization that drops the theology and keeps the ethics untouched has not finished the job it thinks it finished.

Then there is the tone, which almost everyone reverses. The madman grieves. He asks who will wipe the blood off and what water there could be to wash in. Turn to the fifth book and the same event arrives as weather clearing: Nietzsche and his free spirits feel a new light fall on them, the horizon open once more, every hazard permitted again to whoever wants to look, and the sea, in his phrase our sea, lying open as perhaps it never has. Both passages sit in one volume. Quote either alone and half the thought is missing.

· · ·

Nietzsche wrote nothing after 6 January 1889. His sister Elisabeth took charge of the estate, installed it in Weimar, and with Heinrich Köselitz began arranging fragments from the notebooks into a book under a title her brother had once considered: Der Wille zur Macht, 483 sections in 1901, then 1,067 in the remodelled edition of 1906, presented to readers as the masterwork he had not lived to publish. He had in fact dropped that title at the end of August 1888 for a different plan. When Giorgio Colli and Mazzino Montinari went to the Leipzig archives in the 1960s and worked from the originals, they found fragments cut apart and stitched together with nothing on the page to mark the seams, and called the book a historic forgery. The standard edition of Nietzsche has been theirs ever since.

What survives all that handling is the timing. Nietzsche dated the death to the past and its arrival to the future, and he put the interval at something like centuries. The lines about lightning and starlight go to a man nobody in the market is willing to hear out. Reach for the sentence as a slogan and you have taken the death and left the shadow.
