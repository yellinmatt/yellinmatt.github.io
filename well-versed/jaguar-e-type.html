The Jaguar E-Type

The most admired shape in motoring was worked out on logarithm tables, by a man who refused the word stylist.

At half past four on the afternoon of 15 March 1961, in the Parc des Eaux-Vives above Lake Geneva, William Lyons pulled the covers off a car and the room stopped behaving like a press conference. Jaguar had brought one demonstrator, the fixed-head coupé registered 9600 HP, and the queue of journalists wanting a run up the hill climb outgrew it inside the hour. Lyons telephoned Coventry and asked for the second car and the man who could get it there. Norman Dewis, the company’s test and development engineer, drove the first production roadster across France through the night and arrived seventeen hours later. That car still exists, chassis 850003, registered 77 RW.

WatchNorman Dewis, filmed by the Jaguar Daimler Heritage Trust

Dewis telling the story himself, decades on. Listen for what he was actually worried about on the drive, which was not the police and not the speed.

Looks were only half of it. The roadster cost £2,098 including tax, which the Jaguar Daimler Heritage Trust reckons at a hundred and ninety-four weeks of an average wage. It would do close to 150 miles an hour. Cars that could live with it on the second number cost roughly twice as much on the first, and The Motor had 77 RW out for a road test that ran on 22 March, a week after the show.

Malcolm Sayer drew the shape, and would not let anyone call him a designer. He was born in Cromer in 1916 to a schoolmaster who taught the unusual pairing of mathematics and art, took a first-class diploma in automotive engineering at Loughborough, and spent the war at the Bristol Aeroplane Company on the Blenheim and the Beaufighter. He reached Jaguar in 1951 by way of a university post in Baghdad that turned out to exist only on paper. What he brought was a method: a slide rule, seven-figure logarithm tables, and formulae he had devised himself for describing a curve in three dimensions, the work now done by modelling software. “I’m not a hairdresser,” he said when people reached for the word stylist.

He proved the method on racing cars first. The C-Type won Le Mans at its first attempt in 1951, and the D-Type won it in 1955, 1956 and 1957, taking monocoque construction from aircraft practice and reaching 192 mph down the Mulsanne straight on an engine smaller than its rivals’. Aerodynamic reasoning of that kind had belonged to racing cars and aeroplanes, and the E-Type was among the first cars built in volume to come out of it, which is the ground the Museum of Modern Art gives for keeping one. Sayer worked the outside first. He fixed the silhouette, fitted the bumpers, lamps and wheels inside it as ellipses his formulae described, and had scale models put in a wind tunnel to be read from every angle rather than only front to back, which is all the finned American saloons of the period asked of themselves.

Underneath sat a monocoque with a front subframe, independent suspension at both ends, rack-and-pinion steering and disc brakes at all four corners, most of it inherited from the racers. Some of it was poor. The discs could not keep stopping a car driven hard at 140, the seats were shallow buckets with no room for a tall driver, and the Moss gearbox was slow and obstructive, as it had been in the XK150 before it. Jaguar replaced the box, the seats and the brakes in 1964 when the 4.2-litre engine came in, which is the single most useful thing to know about buying one.

· · ·

Enzo Ferrari is supposed to have called it the most beautiful car ever made. Nobody has produced a record of him saying so. The line rests on secondhand accounts and has been repeated until it reads as documentation, which is worth knowing before you repeat it. A harder piece of evidence sits in New York. In 1996 the Museum of Modern Art accepted a 1963 roadster in opalescent dark blue, given by Jaguar, as only the third car in its collection after the Cisitalia 202 of 1972. Terence Riley, then chief curator of architecture and design, set out the grounds: beauty and sculptural quality, functionality, and a seminal effect on the cars that followed. The museum had drawn up a wish list of ten or twelve cars, and the E-Type was at the top of it.

The four cars people mean by “E-Type”

SeriesYearsWhat it is
Series 11961–19673.8-litre six, 265 bhp, Moss gearbox. From Oct 1964 a 4.2 with Jaguar’s own all-synchromesh box and better seats
Series 1.51967–1968The transitional car, updated ahead of the Series 2 proper
Series 21968–1971Detail and cosmetic changes made to satisfy American regulations
Series 31971–1974A 5.3-litre V12 of 276 bhp on the longer wheelbase. Faster than any earlier E-Type and least loved

A 2+2 on a stretched wheelbase joined the range in March 1966. Roughly 85 per cent of all production was exported, so a right-hand-drive car is rarer than it sounds and many have been converted.

Production ran to September 1974 and about 72,000 cars. Sayer did not see the end of it, dying in 1970 a month short of fifty-four, by then Jaguar’s director of design. His arithmetic is still visible on the car. The long bonnet and the haunches over the rear wheels are the residue of an argument about air, and the cars collectors pay several times the going rate for, the earliest ones with their bonnet locks on the outside, are prized for having arrived first.
