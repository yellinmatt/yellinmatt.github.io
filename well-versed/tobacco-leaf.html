The Tobacco Leaf

Everything worth tasting in a cigar or a pipe was settled in a barn, by heat applied to a leaf that had to be kept alive to receive it.

In 1839, on a farm in Caswell County, North Carolina, a young enslaved man named Stephen sat up through the night with a barn of curing tobacco and fell asleep in the warmth of it. He woke to find the fire almost out. He ran to the charcoal pit he worked the rest of the year as the farm’s blacksmith, carried back an armful of charred log ends and threw them on the dying embers, and the heat came up hard and fast. What came down off the sticks afterward was six hundred pounds, in the phrase the trade press collected decades later from men who had been there, “of the brightest yellow tobacco ever seen.” Abisha Slade, who owned the farm and owned Stephen, spent a dozen years working out how to do it deliberately, and by 1857 he was selling twenty thousand pounds a year at the best prices in the state.

The accident reads clearly once you know what a curing barn is for. A tobacco leaf cut in August is still alive, and the barn’s first job is to keep it that way. Held near a hundred degrees in humid air, the leaf goes on respiring, breaks its own chlorophyll down, and converts its stored starch to sugar; the green drains out and the yellow underneath comes up. When the colour is right the heat is raised to kill the tissue and stop the change where it stands, then raised again to dry first the blade and then the thick stem. Virginia Cooperative Extension still teaches the sequence as three temperatures, a hundred, a hundred and thirty, a hundred and sixty, across five or six days, in a barn called flue-cured after the metal pipes that run the heat in from a furnace outside, so the fire never lays smoke on the leaf. Kill the leaf too early, by drying it too fast or firing it too hot, and the green sets and stays. Stephen’s charred logs happened to land at the hour the leaf was yellow and wanted stopping.

For a generation everyone assumed the charcoal was the secret. The credit belonged to the dirt. Thin, sandy, half-exhausted piedmont soil starves a tobacco plant of nitrogen, a starved plant lays down less protein and stores more starch, and that is precisely the leaf that cures pale and mild. Virginia had already learned this once and let it go. Around 1650 Edward Digges planted ordinary Orinoco seed on a poor sandy neck of the York River and got a light, aromatic leaf that London decided was the finest in the world; it sold on his initials, as E. Dees. Two centuries apart, in two states, the same finding: a great leaf is mostly the ground it had to struggle in.

· · ·

The plant itself is American, unknown in Europe until Columbus’s men watched it burned and inhaled on Cuba in 1492. Two species matter. Nicotiana rustica is what the Virginia Indians grew, and the English could not abide it; William Strachey found it “poore and weake, and of a byting tast.” Nicotiana tabacum, the milder Spanish plant of the Caribbean, was what London wanted, and in 1611 John Rolfe, an ardent smoker three years in the colony, got seed from Trinidad and Caracas off a shipmaster and had it growing by July 1612. Samples reached England aboard the Elizabeth in July 1613, twenty thousand pounds followed in 1617 and twice that in 1618, and within a decade Virginia was settling its taxes, its debts and its passages in leaf. The genus carries the name of a Frenchman who never crossed the Atlantic: Jean Nicot, ambassador in Lisbon, sent tobacco to Catherine de’ Medici in 1560 as a cure for headaches at the French court, and Linnaeus honoured him two centuries later. The alkaloid took his name too.

England’s king thought the whole business a disgrace and said so in print. James I published A Counterblaste to Tobacco in 1604, the same year he authorised a duty of six shillings and eightpence on every imported pound, and by 1624 he had given the argument up and made the tobacco trade a royal monopoly instead. His last sentence is the most quoted line ever written against smoking, though the one before it, reporting what had been found inside the chests of heavy smokers opened after death, is the one that should stop you.

Flue-curing is one of four ways to take the life out of a leaf, and those four are the whole vocabulary. Flue-cured runs four to eight days of clean piped heat and locks the sugars in, which gives the bright mild Virginia leaf that fills most of the world’s cigarettes. Air-curing hangs the leaf in a ventilated barn for one to two months with no fire at all, long enough to spend its sugars entirely; that is Burley, low in sugar, high in nicotine, and neutral enough that blenders use it as the carrier when a blend needs a flavour added to it. Fire-curing smoulders hardwood on the barn floor beneath the hanging leaf for three to ten weeks, laying creosote into the tissue; that is Kentucky dark, and in its Levantine version, Latakia. Sun-curing lays small aromatic leaf on racks in the open for a fortnight, which is how Turkey and Greece make Oriental.

Watch NC State University Libraries, “Pistney and Flowers barn bulk tobacco curing”

Two North Carolina growers load and fire a bulk barn in an extension film from the university’s tobacco archive. Watch how much of the work is packing the racks evenly, because air that finds a gap cures that part of the barn faster than the rest.

None of this is obscure once you are standing at a counter. A cigar is air-cured leaf that is then fermented in bulk, which is where its earth and leather come from and why no part of it tastes sweet. A cigarette is mostly flue-cured with air-cured blended in for strength. An English pipe blend is Virginia with fire-cured Latakia laid over the top, and that smoky note came off a hardwood fire smouldering in a barn in the eastern Mediterranean. Ask a cigar man where his wrapper was grown and he will answer you with a soil, which is the answer Abisha Slade and Edward Digges reached from opposite ends of two hundred years.
