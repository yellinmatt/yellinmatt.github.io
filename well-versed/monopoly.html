Monopoly

The best-selling board game in the world began as an argument against itself, and the man whose name went on the box was a latecomer.

The panel at the middle of that board says PUBLIC TREASURY. One corner pays a hundred dollars for labour upon Mother Earth; the corner diagonally opposite is a poor house. A third corner is Lord Blueblood’s Estate, the owner given as resident in London, England, and the sign laid across it reads NO TRESPASSING — GO TO JAIL. Around the outside run forty spaces in coloured groups, with a railroad at each quarter, a water franchise and a lighting franchise, a Luxury at seventy-five dollars and a Chance. Anyone who has thrown dice on a kitchen table knows the shape of this. It was printed in 1906, it is called The Landlord’s Game, and the woman who patented it built it to show that playing this way ends badly for everyone at the table.

Elizabeth Magie was working as a stenographer in Brentwood, Maryland, when she filed. She wrote verse, acted, held a patent on a device that fed paper through a typewriter, and had once bought advertising space offering herself for sale as a “young woman American slave” to embarrass employers over what they paid women. The conviction under all of it came from her father, an abolitionist newspaperman who had been a friend of Abraham Lincoln’s, and from Henry George, whose Progress and Poverty of 1879 sold into the hundreds of thousands. George’s claim was that the community around a plot creates the rise in its value while the deed-holder merely collects it, so the rent of land belongs to the public and should be taken as the only tax, with every other tax abolished. His followers called it the single tax, and Magie thought the argument would land harder if people could handle it.

The specification reads like any other patent until you notice what each rule is standing in for. The blue spaces are marked bread, coal, shelter and clothing, and landing on one costs five dollars into the Public treasury, with the drafting itself supplying the translation in a parenthesis: “This represents indirect taxation.” The spaces marked “No trespassing” represent, in her words, property held out of use, and the penalty for stepping on land a speculator is sitting on is jail. A player who runs out of money and has nothing left to borrow against goes to the poorhouse. Each circuit of the board is a stint of labour, and the hundred dollars collected at the start is wages.

Magie also wrote two sets of rules. Under one, players build up holdings and squeeze each other until a single owner holds the board. Under the other, later published as Prosperity, the rent collected runs into the treasury and back out to everybody, and the game is won when the poorest player at the table has doubled the stake they started with. She meant the first set to be played and disliked, and she tested both at Arden, Delaware, a colony of single-taxers living on ninety-six acres of former farmland. Another such settlement supplied a property name still legible on the 1906 board, Fairhope Avenue, after the community founded on Mobile Bay in November 1894 to make a practical test of the same doctrines.

“It is a practical demonstration of the present system of with all its usual outcomes and consequences, says Miss Magie. It might well have been called the ‘Game of Life,’ as it contains all the elements of success and failure in the real world, and the object is the same as the human race in general seem to have, i.e., the accumulation of wealth. … There are two franchises: the water and the lighting; and the first player whose throw brings him upon one of these receive a giving him the privilege of taxing all others who must use his light and water.

There are two tracts of land on the board that are — are neither for rent nor for sale — and on each of these appear the forbidding sign: ‘No Trespassing. Go to Jail.’ One of these tracts of land (the largest on the board) is owned by Lord Blueblood, of London, England, and represents foreign ownership of American soil. …

Should any one be so unlucky, or so reckless and extravagant, as to become ‘broke,’ there is a nice little poor house off in one corner where he may tarry until he makes a lucky throw or until some friend takes pity on him and lends him enough to set him on his feet again. And here is where he generally gets for the other players, taking advantage of the unfortunate one’s necessities, demand an enormous rate of interest which the individual must pay before he can complete his round and get his wages. …

Let the children once see clearly the gross injustice of our present land system and when they grow up, if they are allowed to develop naturally, the evil will soon be remedied.”

Lizzie J. Magie, “The Landlord’s Game,” The Single Tax Review, Autumn 1902.

Parker Brothers looked at the game in 1909 and turned it down as too complicated. It spread by hand instead. Scott Nearing, teaching economics at the Wharton School, lived at Arden in 1910, learned the game there and played it with his students until Pennsylvania dismissed him in 1915 over his politics; his students drew their own boards and carried it away with them. From a fraternity house in Indianapolis it reached a teacher at the Friends school in Atlantic City, whose circle of Quakers replaced the property names with their own streets and, wanting a quieter evening, dropped the auction at the front of the game so that a player simply bought whatever he landed on. Every board was homemade and no two were quite alike, which is how one copyist came to write Marvin Gardens for the Atlantic City neighbourhood actually spelled Marven.

In 1933 Charles Todd taught that version to a childhood friend of his wife’s and her husband, an out-of-work heater salesman from Germantown named Charles Darrow. Darrow copied the board, asked for the rules to be typed out for him, and reproduced them exactly, misspelling included. He sold the game to Parker Brothers in 1935, took a patent on it on the last day of that year, and for decades every box carried the story of the unemployed man who had worked it out alone in his basement during the Depression.

That November the company went to Magie and bought her patent for five hundred dollars with no royalties, as part of a sweep that also took in the rival games Finance and Inflation. The document it acquired was her second patent, granted 23 September 1924, because the 1904 one had run out in 1921 and the original game had been in the public domain ever since. Parker Brothers printed The Landlord’s Game in 1939, quietly, and let it die. Magie was then seventy and gave two interviews in 1936, to the Washington Post and the Evening Star, photographed with her boards in front of her, saying plainly that Darrow had not invented the thing. Nobody followed it up, and she died in 1948 with no public credit for any of it.

What reopened the file was a threat letter. Ralph Anspach, an economist at San Francisco State, had concluded while lecturing on Adam Smith that a game teaching children to admire monopoly was teaching them the opposite of how competition works, and in 1973 he published his own, first as Bust the Trust and then as Anti-Monopoly. Parker Brothers’ lawyers wrote to him in 1974. Rather than withdraw, he set out to show that the game had existed long before Darrow patented it, advertised in the Christian Science Monitor for anyone who had played it early, and followed the answers back through the Quakers and the fraternity boards to Arden and to Magie. He lost at trial anyway. To make the point, the company had journalists brought out to watch forty thousand copies of Anti-Monopoly bulldozed into a landfill, and the land was later sold and built on.

On 26 August 1982 the Ninth Circuit held that as applied to a board game the word Monopoly had become generic, meaning the public now heard it as the name of the thing itself and no longer as a badge of who made it, and that the registration was therefore invalid. The Supreme Court declined to hear it. The decision alarmed every company holding a famous mark, and within two years Congress had amended the Lanham Act for the express purpose of overturning the test the court had used, which had turned on why purchasers bought the product. The trademark came back with the statute. Anti-Monopoly stayed in the shops, where it still is.

Watch Ruthless: Monopoly’s Secret History, American Experience, PBS, 2023

Watch for the homemade boards. The film puts several of them on screen, oilcloth and hand-lettering and local street names, and they do more to settle the question of who invented what than any of the testimony does.

· · ·

Hasbro puts cumulative sales past 275 million copies in 114 countries and 47 languages. Every one of them descends from the grabbing half of a game written with two rulebooks, and the half that was supposed to follow, in which the rent comes back to the table and the poorest player wins by doubling a stake, fell away somewhere between Arden and Atlantic City. The demonstration still works exactly as designed, since an evening of it ends with one player holding everything and the rest sitting out, watching. The part that dropped away in transit was the second rulebook, which told you what to make of that.
