The Waltz

The dance The Times warned every English parent against in 1816 had already taken something else out of the ballroom: any way to tell, from the floor, who outranked whom.

Nine couples stand across a single engraved plate, numbered one to nine, and at the far left a box of musicians watches them work. The plate is the frontispiece to A Description of the Correct Method of Waltzing, published in London in 1816 by a dancing master named Thomas Wilson, and its purpose was entirely practical. An Englishman who had never in his life put an arm around a woman in a public room needed to be shown where the arm went. At position one the couple barely touch, her hand resting on his shoulder at the full stretch of her arm. By position eight the engraver has drawn them as a single silhouette. Wilson had a demand to answer, and the demand was four years old.

Picture the room the dance walked into. For a century English balls had run on country dances, in which men and women faced each other down two long lines and worked through named figures: lead down the middle, hands four round, turn partners. The lines were ordered by rank, a habit carried over from court dancing, so that every ball had a head and a foot and everybody present knew which end was theirs. Jane Austen's nephew, recalling her ballroom years in an 1870 memoir, wrote that “much heart-burning and discontent sometimes arose as to who should stand above whom.” The minuet that opened a formal ball was stricter still. One couple performed it alone while the room watched, and its figures had to be oriented toward the two highest-ranking people there, who were called the presence. Every dance in an English ballroom was addressed to somebody.

The waltz arrived in 1812, carried back from Paris by travelled aristocrats and first seen at Almack's, the subscription assembly rooms on King Street in St James's where a committee of patronesses decided each season who was fashionable. It had no lines and no figures. A couple took hold of each other, turned, and worked their way around the floor for as long as the music lasted. A room full of revolving couples has neither a head nor a foot. Rank went unreadable on the dance floor, and the historian Ruth Katz gave the consequence a name, the egalitarian waltz: once everyone is turning, a spectator can no longer tell from where a dancer stands what that dancer is. Queen Victoria felt the same change from the other side. Her dancing master's journal records that she never waltzed as sovereign, since it was held undignified for the monarch to dance in the arms of a subject, while reels and Highland flings, where she need only take a hand, raised no objection at all.

Four years as an aristocratic novelty ended in a single evening. On Friday 12 July 1816 the Prince Regent gave a fête at Carlton House where the dancing was waltzes and cotillions, and though the royal family themselves sat it out, as The Times reported on the Monday, the waltz had now been danced at the English court. On the Tuesday the same paper ran a column about it.

We remark with pain that the indecent foreign dance called the Waltz was introduced (we believe, for the first time) at on Friday last. This is a circumstance which ought not to be passed over in silence. National morals depend on national habits: and it is quite sufficient to cast one's eyes on the intertwining of the limbs, and close of the bodies, in this dance, to see that it is far indeed removed from the modest reserve which has hitherto been considered distinctive of English females. So long as this obscene display was confined to prostitutes and adulteresses, we did not think it deserving of notice; but now that it is attempted to be forced on the respectable classes of society by , we feel it a duty to warn every parent against exposing his daughter to so foul a contagion.

“Dance Called the Waltz,” The Times (London), 16 July 1816, p. 2, column d.

Quoted for its subject, the paragraph is about women's bodies, and that is how it is almost always quoted. Its argument runs somewhere else. The offence is dated precisely to the moment the dance stopped being confined to people the paper had already written off and began descending from above, and the writer goes on to grant “a due deference to our superiors in rank” while claiming a higher duty to morality. That is a newspaper telling the court it has failed at ordering the room. Byron had filed the same charge in verse three years earlier, anonymously, through a country gentleman called Horace Hornem who turns up at a ball to find his wife's arms half round the waist of a stranger; the poem's real quarry is the German dynasty sitting on the English throne. The caricaturists went for the bodies, which draw better than hierarchies do.

The mechanics have barely moved since, and they are learnable in an afternoon. Waltz music is in three-four: three beats to the bar, the weight of the phrase on the first. You take one step per beat, so the count is simply the music. In the closed hold the lead's right hand sits flat on the follow's left shoulder blade, the follow's left hand rests on the lead's upper arm, and the free hands join at about the follow's eye level; the connection travels through the back, and the arms only hold the shape. The box step runs six steps across two bars, forward, side, close, back, side, close, and the turn is that box rotated a quarter at a time, so four of them carry a couple the whole way round. Rise and fall is what separates waltzing from walking in squares: lower into the first beat, rise through the second and third onto the balls of the feet, lower again into the next bar. The floor travels counterclockwise, which is called the line of dance and is the traffic rule that keeps a crowded room from colliding.

The objection lost completely. Within a generation the waltz opened balls from London to St Petersburg, and the dances that followed took its two innovations as settled, the closed hold and the travelling circuit: the polka in 1844, then the mazurka, then the tango and the foxtrot. The warning to parents reads now as a period curiosity. The sentence about deference to superiors in rank was the accurate one. Something went out of the English ballroom in July 1816, and nothing since has put it back.
