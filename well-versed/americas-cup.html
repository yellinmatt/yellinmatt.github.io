The America’s Cup

A New York schooner won a silver ewer round the Isle of Wight in 1851, and her owners gave it away under a deed that kept it in American hands for 132 years.

Late on the afternoon of Friday 22 August 1851, a black-hulled American schooner came through the Needles, the chalk stacks off the western tip of the Isle of Wight, with the rest of the fleet miles astern. The story told ever since puts Queen Victoria close by, asking a signal-master which yacht lay second, and being told, “Ah, Your Majesty, there is no second.” Historians attach the word “alleged” to the exchange, and the finish sheet gives them cause. The wind died in the Solent, the schooner America needed nearly three hours to cover the last miles to Cowes, and the little cutter Aurora crossed the line at 8.45 pm, eight minutes after her. The phrase survived the arithmetic, and it describes the trophy that race created better than any result sheet: for most of its life there has been one holder and a queue of yachts trying to take its place.

America had been built that spring in New York by George Steers for a syndicate of New York Yacht Club members led by the club’s commodore, John Cox Stevens. She measured 101 feet and spread more than 5,000 square feet of sail, and she crossed to the Isle of Wight in July looking for a race. The Royal Yacht Squadron gave her one at its August regatta: open to yachts of every nation, once round the island, for a silver ewer that Lord Anglesey had presented to the Squadron. Fourteen British cutters and schooners started against her, the largest of them the 393-ton Brilliant. America broke her jibboom early and was fifth at the first mark, yet by about four o’clock she was leading round the southern tip of the island. Within weeks the syndicate had sold the yacht for $25,000, five thousand more than they had paid for her, and carried the ewer home. The trophy takes its name from her.

For six years the cup stayed with the owners. Then on 8 July 1857 five of them, in a letter drafted by George Schuyler, presented it to the New York Yacht Club on conditions that still govern the sport’s oldest trophy.

Any organized Yacht Club of any foreign country shall always be entitled, through any one or more of its members, to claim the right of sailing a for this Cup with any yacht or other vessel of not less than 30 or more than 300 tons, measured by the of the country to which the vessel belongs.

It is to be distinctly understood that the Cup is to be the property of the Club, and not of the members thereof, or owners of the vessels winning it in a match; and that the condition of keeping it open to be sailed for by Yacht Clubs of all foreign countries, upon the terms above laid down, shall forever attach to it, thus making it perpetually a for friendly competition between foreign countries.

J. C. Stevens, Edwin A. Stevens, Hamilton Wilkes, J. Beekman Finley and George L. Schuyler to the Secretary of the New York Yacht Club, New York, 8 July 1857. The club rewrote the deed in 1887, adding among other conditions that a challenging club must hold an annual regatta on the sea.

Those terms turned a regatta into a duel with the odds set by the holder, who chose the waters and sailed under its own rules. The first challenger came from Britain in 1870 and lost, and so did every challenger after him for the next 113 years. Inside the New York Yacht Club the ewer sat bolted to a glass-enclosed table. Counted from 1851, the American run lasted 132 years, and it is usually called the longest winning streak in sport.

It ended off Newport, Rhode Island, in September 1983, against a challenger from the Royal Perth Yacht Club financed by the businessman Alan Bond and designed by Ben Lexcen. Whenever Australia II was lifted in or out of the water, her crew hung tarpaulin “modesty skirts” over her sides to hide the keel, and the New York club protested that whatever lay underneath made her an illegal 12 Metre. She was cleared to race. Against Dennis Conner’s Liberty she fell three races to one behind in a best-of-seven, then won the next two. On 26 September Liberty led the deciding race by 57 seconds after four of the six legs. Halfway down the fifth, a downwind run in a light southwesterly, Australia II sailed past, and John Bertrand’s crew held her lead up the final beat by tacking again and again, crossing at 5.21 pm with 41 seconds in hand. The next day Prime Minister Bob Hawke told Australians, “Any boss who sacks anyone for not turning up today is a bum.”

The deed has outlasted every boat that raced under it. New York law treats it as a charitable trust, with the cup as the trust’s property and the holding club as sole trustee until someone wins it away, which is why the sport’s fiercest fights have sometimes been settled by judges. When a Spanish club formed in June 2007 lodged a challenge on the same day the Swiss holder, Société Nautique de Genève, won its defence, the Court of Appeals ruled in April 2009 that a challenger must already have held an annual regatta on the sea before it challenges, and threw the Spanish entry out. The judges added that the right to hold the cup should be decided “on the water and not in a courtroom.” The thirty-eighth match will be sailed off Naples from 10 to 18 July 2027, Emirates Team New Zealand defending against a field led by Britain’s GB1, the challenger of record, all of them racing for a jug that five New Yorkers handed over on condition that whoever holds it must keep putting it up for challenge.
