The Seven Groups

A wolf’s hunt has eight moves in it, and almost every breed of dog is that sequence with some of the moves bred out.

At a sheepdog trial the dog is sent four hundred yards up the field, runs out around the far side of the sheep in a wide arc, and then, at the top, stops. It drops almost flat, carries its head level with its shoulders and creeps forward a few feet at a time with its eyes locked on the flock. Shepherds call that stare eye, and a dog with a great deal of it is a strong-eyed dog. What the spectators along the fence are watching is a wolf hunting sheep with the last four moves taken out.

Raymond Coppinger, a founding biology professor at Hampshire College who ran a livestock guarding dog project there from 1976 and spent decades among working dogs in Europe and the American West, set the wolf’s hunt out as a fixed chain of motor patterns, meaning movements an animal performs the same way every time without having been taught them: orient, eye, stalk, chase, grab-bite, kill-bite, dissect, consume. A wolf that drops a link goes hungry, so the chain runs through to the end. A dog that has to work livestock must be stopped somewhere in the middle, and the stopping is done by breeding rather than by training. Select for the animals that hold the stare and never close, generation after generation, and the chain breaks where you want it broken.

Every gun dog and stock dog is that chain cut at a different link. A pointer stops early, at the eye and the beginning of the stalk, and holds there; the hold is the point. Take out the kill-bite while leaving the chase and the grab and the result is a retriever with what the field calls a soft mouth, an animal running the front half of a hunt with no ending available to it. Border collies keep eye, stalk and chase, and exaggerate the first two past anything a wolf bothers to do. At the far end are the livestock guardians, in which the chain is suppressed from the first link and the dog stays behaviourally juvenile well into adulthood, which is why a Great Pyrenees can lie down in the middle of forty ewes and be ignored by every one of them.

Watch the moment the dog reaches the top of its outrun and changes gear. The running stops, the body drops, and everything after that is pressure applied by posture rather than by movement. The sheep respond to a predator that has committed to a stalk, which is the only reason a forty-pound animal can steer half a ton of livestock across a field.

The seven groups a dog show walks through are the same map, drawn by committee. The American Kennel Club was founded in 1884 and spent its first forty years unable to sort its own entries, so that a dog might be shown as Sporting at one fixture and as a Terrier at the next, and several breeds carried names that lied about them: the Congo Terrier now called the Basenji, the Lhassa Terrier now called the Lhasa Apso. The modern system dates from 1924, when the mass of Non-Sporting breeds was cut into Working, Terrier, Toy and a Non-Sporting remainder. Hounds separated from Sporting in 1930. Herding separated from Working in 1983, which is the last time the map moved. The Dachshund holds the record for indecision, filed under four different groups before it settled among the hounds.

SportingBirds, worked alongside the gun. Pointers, setters, spaniels and retrievers, plus versatile breeds that do more than one of those jobs.
HoundMammals, often hunted without a handler in reach. Divided again into sighthounds and scenthounds, which is the oldest split in the dog world.
WorkingSize and nerve put to human use: property guardians, sled and cart dogs, water-rescue and fishermen’s dogs.
TerrierVermin, usually underground and out of earshot. Bred to decide alone, which is why the group has a reputation for not taking direction.
ToyCompany. The job is the person, and the miniaturisation is the specification.
Non-SportingThe remainder. Breeds whose work is long obsolete, or that never sorted cleanly anywhere else.
HerdingMoving and holding livestock. Split out of Working in 1983 once the group had grown too large to judge as one.

In 2017 Heidi Parker and Elaine Ostrander of the National Human Genome Research Institute published the largest breed phylogeny then assembled, 1,346 dogs across 161 breeds, which sorted into twenty-three clades. The tree recovers the jobs. Gun dogs cluster with gun dogs and spaniels with spaniels, and the groupings hold across coat, colour and size, which are the traits a show ring actually judges. “First, there was selection for a type, like herders or pointers,” Parker said when the paper appeared, “and then there was admixture to get certain physical traits.” Herding proved the least tidy branch of all: rather than one founding population there were three, one out of Britain, one out of northern Europe and one out of the south, meaning shepherds solved the same problem separately over thousands of years instead of copying a single solution over a few hundred.

The registry now holds 205 breeds, three of them admitted in December 2025. Nearly all are closed populations barely a century and a half old, hardened out of older working types in the Victorian decades when shows and stud books arrived and appearance became the thing being selected. The work most of them were sorted by has been obsolete for generations, and that is worth carrying when the group is used as a forecast. A group tells you what the dog was edited for, which is real and does show up in the animal. Whether that history has anything left to act on is a question about the household it lives in.
