The Trial of Socrates

Convicted at seventy of impiety and of corrupting the young, Socrates proposed that Athens reward him with meals at public expense, and the jury answered with hemlock.

The jury had found him guilty, and under Athenian law it was now his turn to name a penalty. The prosecutor had asked for death. The court would choose between the two proposals by a second vote, with no third option, so a defendant who hoped to live offered something the jurors could accept: exile, a heavy fine, his weeping children brought before the bench. Socrates, son of Sophroniscus, seventy years old and in a courtroom for the first time in his life, told them that what he deserved was dinner in the prytaneum, the town hall where Athens fed its Olympic champions at the city’s cost. The year was 399 BC, the month probably early summer, and the court had perhaps five hundred citizens on its benches.

He wrote nothing, so everything known about him reaches us second-hand. Plato, his most devoted follower, made him the speaker of nearly every dialogue; the soldier and author Xenophon left memoirs of their conversations; and the comic playwright Aristophanes put him on the stage in the Clouds of 423 BC, hanging in a basket to study the sky. Friends teased him about his looks, which the surviving portraits preserve: the snub nose, the wide bulging eyes, the heavy lips of a satyr. He spent his days in the agora, the city’s market square, asking men with a reputation for wisdom what courage or piety or justice was, and then showing them, one question at a time, that their answers fell apart.

The indictment survived for centuries in the city archive, and Diogenes Laertius copied its wording: Socrates was guilty of refusing to recognize the gods the state recognized and of introducing other new divinities, and guilty of corrupting the youth, and the penalty demanded was death. Three men brought it. Meletus, a young poet, swore the charge; Anytus, a leading democratic politician who had gone into exile under the oligarchy, backed it; Lycon spoke for the orators. Politics sat underneath the religion. After Sparta defeated Athens in 404, a junta known as the Thirty ruled the city by terror for most of a year, and their fiercest leader, Critias, had been one of the young men around Socrates, as had Alcibiades, who once went over to Sparta. An amnesty in 403 forbade prosecutions for anything done under the Thirty, so whatever the jurors held against those friendships could reach the court only as impiety.

Plato’s Apology, from the Greek apologia, a defence, is his version of the speech, and it explains the habit as a religious duty. Socrates’s friend Chaerephon had once asked the oracle at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, and the priestess answered that no one was. Baffled, Socrates went looking for a wiser man among the politicians, the poets and the craftsmen, and found each of them sure of things he did not understand; his own small advantage, he decided, was that he knew he did not know. He compared himself to a gadfly that the god had fastened to Athens, a large, well-bred horse made sluggish by its size.

The verdict went against him, and what surprised Socrates was how close it ran: “if only thirty votes had been cast the other way, I should have been acquitted.” Athenian courts of the period often sat 501 strong, which would put the count near 280 to 221. Then came the penalty speech.

Plato, Apology 36d–e and 38a, translated by Harold North Fowler, Loeb Classical Library, 1914. The first paragraph is the penalty proposal; the second comes from his answer to the suggestion that he go into exile and keep quiet.

The proposal cost him the room. His friends, Plato among them, pressed him into offering a fine of thirty minas with themselves as guarantors, but the second vote went for death, and Diogenes Laertius reports that eighty more jurors chose it than had voted to convict. Socrates closed by telling the court that the time had come to go away: “I go to die, and you to live; but which of us goes to the better lot, is known to none but God.”

· · ·

He should have died within a day. On the eve of the trial the Athenians had garlanded the stern of the ship they sent each year to Delos in memory of Theseus, and no execution could take place in the city until it came home, which Xenophon says took about a month. Toward the end of that month his old friend Crito arrived before dawn with an escape arranged, and in Plato’s Crito Socrates turns him down: a man who had lived seventy years under the city’s laws, and could have chosen exile lawfully at the trial, would not break them now to save himself.

The last day belongs to Plato’s Phaedo. Socrates spends it arguing for the immortality of the soul, sends the women away so his friends will not wail, drinks the poison without a pause, and walks about until his legs grow heavy. The jailer presses his foot and asks if he can feel it. His final words are to Crito: “we owe a cock to Aesculapius. Pay it and do not neglect it.” Asclepius was the god of healing, and a cock was the ordinary offering of thanks for a cure. Plato was not in the room; the dialogue says he was ill. David seated him at the foot of the bed anyway, an old man turned away from the cup, and gave Socrates a raised finger, still teaching, as his hand finds it without looking.

The trial leaves a real question about the jury. On one side, Socrates gave them almost nothing to work with: having been found guilty, he asked the court to honour the conduct it had just condemned. On the other side stands the sentence in the Read above, a claim that the only life worth having is one spent questioning, answered by a city that ended a life spent doing exactly that. Plato took the second side and spent the next half-century writing dialogues with his teacher at the centre, which is why every later philosopher measures himself against a man who never wrote a line.
