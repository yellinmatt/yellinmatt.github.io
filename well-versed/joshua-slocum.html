Sailing · Single-handed

Joshua Slocum

A captain on hard times rebuilt an old oyster sloop, left Boston alone in 1895, kept his place on three oceans by a tin clock and arithmetic, and came home the first man to sail round the world by himself.

At noon on 24 April 1895, with the twelve o’clock whistles sounding across Boston harbor, a fifty-one-year-old captain cast off a small sloop and stood out to sea with nobody else aboard. Joshua Slocum had been born on 20 February 1844 on North Mountain in Nova Scotia, “in a cold spot,” as he put it, and by the 1890s he had fallen on hard times in New England. A whaling captain at Fairhaven, Massachusetts, offered him an old oyster boat, warning that “she wants some repairs.” Slocum rebuilt her and kept the bill: “$553.62 for materials, and thirteen months of my own labor.” She was the Spray, 36 feet 9 inches long. Three years, two months and two days later, on 27 June 1898, she anchored at Newport, Rhode Island, after more than 46,000 miles, and Slocum was the first man in recorded history to have sailed around the world alone.

The record made the papers; what sailors still talk about is how he found his position. A navigator of the day was expected to carry a chronometer, a clock steady enough at sea to tell him the time at Greenwich, from which longitude, the east-west coordinate, can be worked out. Slocum owned an old one, and having it cleaned and rated would have cost fifteen dollars. “In our newfangled notions of navigation it is supposed that a mariner cannot find his way without one,” he wrote, and he left it at home. At Yarmouth he picked up “my famous tin clock, the only timepiece I carried on the whole voyage.”

What carried him instead was dead reckoning, the oldest method at sea. Bowditch’s American Practical Navigator, the standard reference of the trade, defines it as advancing a position “from a known fix” along the course steered and the speed made. You know where you began, which way you pointed and how fast you went for how long, and you plot where that leaves you. Crossing the Pacific from Juan Fernández toward the Marquesas, he finally tested it against the sky.

Five miles after six weeks alone on the largest ocean is a superb result, and the story has a better turn. Later in the same chapter Slocum admits that his first set of sights had put the sloop “many hundred miles west of my reckoning,” and that the fault lay in the book: the column “from which I had got an important logarithm was in error.” His sum of course, speed and time had been right all along, and the printed table wrong. He corrected the tables and sailed on, he wrote, “with my tin clock fast asleep.”

The Spray gave him one more advantage, and it made the voyage survivable for one man: she steered herself. From Thursday Island to Keeling Cocos in the Indian Ocean she ran “twenty-seven hundred miles as the crow flies” in twenty-three days, during which Slocum spent no more than three hours at the helm, including the beat into harbor. “I just lashed the helm and let her go,” he wrote, and whether the wind came from the side or from astern, “she always sailed on her course.”

You can run his method on foot this week. Next time you walk somewhere you half know, note the time and the spot where you start, hold one heading, and keep a steady pace. Distance is speed multiplied by time; with time in minutes, divide by sixty, so at three miles an hour, twenty minutes puts you one mile along your line. Before you look at your phone, mark on a map where you believe you are, then check. The gap is your error, and it will be larger the longer you went without a landmark.

That gap is the tool to keep, and Bowditch states it plainly: the error of a reckoning “is a function of time; it grows as the ship proceeds along the track without obtaining a fix.” An error of two miles an hour is two miles off after one hour and four after two. So the rule is speed times minutes over sixty for where you are, and a fresh fix, a landmark, a sight or a known buoy, whenever one is on offer, because every hour without one widens the circle you might be in.

His book came out in 1900. In 1909 he sailed from New England in the Spray, meaning to spend the winter in Grand Cayman, and neither he nor the sloop was seen again; in 1924 he was declared legally dead. When the voyage comes up, here is your line: the first man to sail alone around the world refused to pay fifteen dollars to service his chronometer, crossed the Pacific on dead reckoning, and when he finally checked against the moon he was within five miles.
