The Blues

Twelve bars, three chords and one repeated line, and the man who wrote them down.

The train was nine hours late. W. C. Handy, a cornetist and bandleader with a conservatory ear, had fallen asleep on a bench at the railway station in Tutwiler, Mississippi, sometime around 1903, and he woke to a guitar. A thin man in worn clothes had sat down beside him and was pressing a knife flat against the strings, sliding it along the neck the way Hawaiian players used a steel bar, singing a single line, Goin’ where the Southern cross’ the Dog. He sang it three times. In his 1941 autobiography Handy called it “the weirdest music I ever heard.” The words were ordinary railway talk: the Southern and the Yazoo Delta tracks crossed at Moorhead, forty-two miles south, and the Yazoo Delta was nicknamed the Dog. The man was singing about a junction. He was also, though no one in that station had a name for it, singing the blues.

That music had a structure, and it is the single most useful piece of musical literacy an American can carry. A blues runs twelve bars. In common time that is forty-eight beats in three phrases of four, moving through three chords: the ones built on the first, fourth and fifth notes of the key. Musicians write them as Roman numerals, so a bandleader can call a blues in any key and a stranger on the stand plays it without a chart. The V chord enters in bar nine at the point of highest tension, drops to IV, and settles home.

The words follow the same architecture. A blues stanza has three lines, and the second is the first said again. That repetition is doing real work. It hands the singer four bars in which the opening line is already sung and the closing line is not yet decided, which is precisely enough time to invent one. The form is an improviser’s form with a memory aid built into it, and it explains why so many blues punchlines land the way a good joke does. The trouble gets stated, held long enough to sit on the listener, and then answered.

ReadW. C. Handy, “The Saint Louis Blues,” 1914

AI hate to see de ev’nin’ sun go down,
AHate to see de ev’nin’ sun go down,
B’Cause ma baby, he done lef’ dis town.

The first stanza, as printed. Twelve bars exactly, three lines, the third arriving as the reason for the first two.

Then there is the sound, which no chord chart can hold. Blues singers and players bend certain degrees of the scale slightly flat, chiefly the third and the seventh, and later the fifth. These are the blue notes, and they fall in the cracks between the keys of a piano. A voice finds them without being told. A fretted guitar cannot, which is part of why the man at Tutwiler had a knife against his strings: a slide travels continuously along the wire and reaches every pitch between the frets. When a blues singer seems to grieve against the band rather than with it, that flattened interval is the reason.

Handy was a trained musician who could hear a folk idiom and set it in print, and print was where the money lived. He published “The Memphis Blues” in September 1912, a few months after Hart Wand’s “Dallas Blues” reached the presses in March; the bandleader James Reese Europe picked it up for Vernon and Irene Castle, who danced their new foxtrot to it. Two years later came “The Saint Louis Blues,” which opens with a straight twelve bars and then swerves into a habanera bridge Handy added to catch the dancers, a rhythm Jelly Roll Morton would call the Spanish tinge. That one never left the repertoire.

Sheet music sold the blues to people who read music. A record sold it to everyone else. On 10 August 1920 Mamie Smith walked into OKeh’s New York studio with a Black band called the Jazz Hounds and cut “Crazy Blues,” the first blues recording by a Black singer, and it moved seventy-five thousand copies in Harlem inside a month. An industry that had assumed no such market existed promptly invented one, called it race records, and sent scouts south with portable equipment to find whoever else was out there. That is the machinery by which Charley Patton, Son House, Bessie Smith and Robert Johnson arrived on shellac.

Listen Bessie Smith with Louis Armstrong, January 1925

Smith sings the stanza above at the top. Listen for what Armstrong, then twenty-four, does with the space at the end of each line: the cornet answers each line instead of merely backing it, which is the call-and-response the repeated line was built to make room for. Fred Longshaw is on pump organ, which is why it sounds like a church three doors down. Wait for the tempo to lean sideways when the habanera bridge arrives.

The music has argued with itself about Handy ever since. T-Bone Walker, who put the guitar through an amplifier in the 1940s, made the objection plainly: “You can’t dress up the blues... I’m not saying that ‘Saint Louis Blues’ isn’t fine music you understand. But it just isn’t blues.” A published, arranged number for a dance floor sits a long way from a man alone at a railway station with a pocket knife, and the distance is real. Some version of that complaint gets filed every decade against whoever has most recently carried the music into a larger room.

What survived the argument is the frame. Count twelve bars and you will hear it holding up “Hound Dog,” “Johnny B. Goode,” “Rock Around the Clock,” “Sweet Home Chicago” and most of what a bar band plays after eleven at night. It is the nearest thing American music has to a common language, plain enough that two strangers can start playing together without speaking first. The guitarist at the Tutwiler station is named nowhere in the record. What he was playing outlasted nearly everyone whose name got printed on it.
