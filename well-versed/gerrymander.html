The Gerrymander

A Massachusetts senate district of 1812 gave English its word for rigging a map, and the Supreme Court has since told federal judges to leave the partisan kind alone.

Look closely at the beast and you can read it. Salisbury sits in its jaws, Andover fills its chest, Marblehead curls into a talon, and Chelsea hangs off the bottom like a spur. On 26 March 1812 the Boston Gazette ran this woodcut under the headline “The Gerry-mander. A new species of Monster, which appeared in the Essex South District in January last.” The joke needed no explaining in Boston. The monster was a state senate district, long and thin, that ran from just outside the city up to the New Hampshire line and then hooked east along the Merrimack to the sea.

Its namesake had not drawn it. Elbridge Gerry had signed the Declaration of Independence and had refused, in Philadelphia in 1787, to sign the Constitution. By February 1812 he was the Democratic-Republican governor of Massachusetts, and his party’s legislators sent him a redistricting bill built to keep them in power. His son-in-law and first biographer, James T. Austin, says the governor found it “exceedingly disagreeable” and thought of sending it back. He signed it. He also said his name with a hard G, as in Gary, which the word has long since softened into the Jerry we hear today.

The map worked. Legislators squeezed the Federalist vote into a few districts and spread their own across many, and in the April election the Federalists won more votes statewide and took 11 senate seats to the Republicans’ 29. Essex, which had sent five Federalists to the senate, returned three Republicans. Gerry himself lost the governorship to Caleb Strong, joined James Madison’s ticket later that year, and died as vice president on 23 November 1814. Nobody knows who coined the word. One story has a Gazette editor looking at the map on his wall and saying “Salamander! Call it a Gerrymander,” another puts the poet Richard Alsop at a Federalist dinner party, and the Library of Congress declines to pick either.

The practice is older than its name, and the man who warned about it would become Gerry’s running mate. On 9 August 1787 the Convention took up who should control congressional elections, and Charles Pinckney and John Rutledge moved to leave it entirely to the states. Madison objected.

He won the point. Article I, section 4 lets each state legislature set the “Times, Places and Manner” of House elections and adds that Congress “may at any time by Law make or alter such Regulations.” Congress has used that power sparingly. An act of 1842 required single-member districts of contiguous territory, later statutes added compactness and equal population, and only the single-member rule survives.

The arithmetic has barely changed since Essex. Mapmakers call the two moves packing, which crams opponents into a few districts where their surplus votes are wasted, and cracking, which scatters them across many districts where they lose narrowly each time. Fifty precincts split 60–40 can yield a clean sweep for the majority or a 3–2 win for the minority, depending only on where the lines fall.

Software has made the old trick precise, and in North Carolina nobody bothered to hide it. After a court threw out the state’s map in 2016, Republican legislators told their mapmaker to produce ten Republicans and three Democrats, because they did not think eleven and two was possible. “I think electing Republicans is better than electing Democrats,” said Representative David Lewis. The challenge reached the Supreme Court alongside a Maryland case against a map drawn by Democrats, and on 27 June 2019, in Rucho v. Common Cause, the Court ruled 5–4: “We conclude that partisan gerrymandering claims present political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts.” Chief Justice Roberts granted that excessive partisanship “leads to results that reasonably seem unjust” and pointed to state constitutions and independent commissions. Justice Kagan opened her dissent: “For the first time ever, this Court refuses to remedy a constitutional violation because it thinks the task beyond judicial capabilities.”

Today’s House elections show what followed. Texas redrew its map in August 2025 to add five Republican-leaning seats, a rare break from the custom of redrawing once a decade. California’s voters answered that November with Proposition 50, at about 64 percent, aimed at five seats the other way. Ohio, North Carolina and Florida redrew too, and Virginia’s attempt was struck down by its own supreme court. In April 2026, Louisiana v. Callais narrowed the Voting Rights Act, the main federal check left on maps drawn along racial lines, and Louisiana and Alabama had new maps within weeks.

Here is the position worth holding. Gerrymandering is as old as the republic and belongs to whichever party holds the pen, as Massachusetts and Maryland both show. Since Rucho the live remedies are state courts, state commissions and Congress, whose power Madison fought for and which reaches House seats only, so a district like Essex South would still lie outside it. The race between Texas and California exposes the weakness of the state route: a state that disarms alone hands seats to its rivals, which makes Article I the lever that matters, and Congress has left it largely unpulled.
