The 1855 Classification

The list that still ranks Bordeaux was settled in thirteen days, out of a century of price records rather than a tasting.

On 5 April 1855 the Bordeaux Chamber of Commerce wrote to the city's wine brokers and asked for “the exact and complete list of all classified growths of the department, specifying which of the five classes each belongs to.” Napoleon III had called the provinces to a Universal Exposition in Paris, the Gironde was expected to send its wines, and the Chamber had discovered that it owned no official ranking to send with them. Thirteen days later the answer came back: fifty-seven red estates sorted into five ranks, twenty-one sweet whites sorted into two. That sheet of paper still sets the price of Bordeaux.

The men who drew it up were courtiers, the brokers who stood between the châteaux and the merchant houses along the Quai des Chartrons and took a commission on every cask that moved. They held no tasting. They had something they trusted further, a hundred years of their own transaction books, and they ranked the estates by what the wines had been fetching across decades of vintages, owners and weather. A single glorious year can be a fluke of August; a century of premiums is a fact about a place.

They also knew precisely what they were handing over, and they said so in the covering letter.

Gentlemen, We have had the honour of receiving your letter of the 5th of this month, by which you asked us for the complete list of the classed red wines of the Gironde… You know as well as we do, Gentlemen, how delicate a thing this classification is and what touchiness it arouses; we have therefore had no thought of drawing up an official register of our fine wines…

Syndicat des courtiers de commerce to the Chambre de Commerce de Bordeaux, 18 April 1855. Excerpted; the ellipses mark omitted passages. Text as reproduced in the French record of the classification and in Bruno Prats's account for the Académie du Vin de Bordeaux.

What keeps that disclaimer from being the whole story is that the prices the brokers trusted were tracking the ground. The Médoc is a low tongue of land between the Gironde estuary and the Atlantic pine forest, and the vineyards that matter sit on croupes, shallow mounds of gravel that the rivers and the ice ages dropped on top of the clay. It does two things a vine wants. It drains, which is the first problem of growing anything in a wet maritime climate, and, as Decanter's guide to the Left Bank puts it, “it stores heat during sunny days and then releases it slowly after sundown, encouraging the ripening process.” Cabernet Sauvignon ripens late and needs every degree it can find. Where the beds run deepest the prices ran highest, and the match is close enough that the 1855 list reads now as a rough survey of Médoc geology taken by men who believed they were doing accounts.

That exception is Château Haut-Brion, which stands in Pessac, now a suburb of the city, in the district called Graves after the local word for the gravel it sits on. The brokers could hardly have left it out. On 10 April 1663 Samuel Pepys recorded in his diary that he had drunk “a sort of French wine, called Ho Bryan, that hath a good and most particular taste that I never met with,” the first appearance of a named Bordeaux estate in English, a hundred and ninety-two years before anyone at the Chamber of Commerce thought to make a list. Its owner in 1855 put the quality down to “porous, gravelly soil and good slopes, light composting, and old vine stocks.”

In 1855 the head of the list held four names: Lafite and Latour in Pauillac, Margaux in its own commune, and Haut-Brion out in the Graves. Mouton, whose vines touch Lafite's, was placed first among the seconds, and its owners never accepted the verdict. Baron Philippe de Rothschild, who took the estate in hand in the 1920s and made it bottle its own harvest, petitioned formally in 1961, in 1969 and again in 1971. The decree promoting Mouton was signed on 22 June 1973 by the Minister of Agriculture, Jacques Chirac. Until then the estate had carried a motto reading Premier ne puis, second ne daigne, Mouton suis, first I cannot be, second I do not deign to be, Mouton I am. The Baron had it recut for the new label: Premier je suis, second je fus, Mouton ne change. First I am, second I was, Mouton does not change.

Everything else that has happened to the list is clerical. Cantemerle was added as a fifth growth on 16 September 1855, five months behind the rest, because it had been selling straight to Dutch buyers instead of through the Bordeaux trade and the brokers had never seen its prices. Estates have since been divided, sold and renamed, so the fifty-seven red châteaux of 1855 are sixty-one today. The sweet whites of Sauternes and Barsac number twenty-seven, headed by Château d'Yquem, which holds a rank invented for it alone and shared with nobody since, Premier Cru Supérieur.

The one thing a frozen list cannot do is stay accurate, and the arithmetic underneath this one has come apart. Bruno Prats, who ran Cos d'Estournel and went back through the price records for the Académie du Vin de Bordeaux, set the first growths at an index of 100 and tracked the rest against them. In 1824 the second growths stood at 88. By 2012 they stood at 23. The brokers were describing a hierarchy of near equals, and the step they recorded has since opened into a cliff. That gap is worth holding in mind whenever a wine list prints a classification next to a price.

The other bank has tried the alternative. Saint-Émilion, first classified in 1955, rewrites its ranking roughly every ten years; the 2006 edition was annulled in the French courts, and in 2021 and 2022 several of its grandest names withdrew rather than be judged again. The Médoc has moved one château in a hundred and seventy years and nobody has resigned from it. The honest sentence to have ready is that the list records the standing of fifty-seven estates in the spring of 1855, which turned out to be a durable guide to the ground they stand on and a poor one to what they now cost. For the drinking, the fifth growths are where the classification still earns its money, and Château Batailley in Pauillac is the reliable place to start.
