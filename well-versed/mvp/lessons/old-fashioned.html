The Old Fashioned

Why is it called an Old Fashioned? Who invented it? And what actually goes in one? The answers start in 1806, with one candidate’s bar tab.

The bar bill

A candidate loses an election. His bar tab makes history.

In early America, candidates bought drinks for the voters. This painting of an election day shows it. Find the tag: a voter is getting a free pour.

May 1806. A candidate near Hudson, New York, has lost his election. The town newspaper mocks him by printing his campaign as an account book, with one column for what he lost and one for what he gained.

  • 720rum grogs
  • 411glasses of bitters
  • 32gin slings
  • 17brandies
  • 25cock-tails

Nothing

Every drink he bought for the voters went under Loss. Under Gain the paper wrote one word.

“I have heard of a jorum, of phlegm-cutter and fog driver … but never in my life, though I have lived a good many years, did I hear of cock tail before.”

A Subscriber

A week later a reader wrote in about the bar bill. He knew the slang: a jorum, a phlegm-cutter and a fog driver were all names for a drink. But the last drink on the bill, “cock-tail”, was new to him. He asked the editor what it was.

The editor answered with a recipe. It had exactly four ingredients. Which four?

  • Spirits
  • Ice
  • Lemon juice
  • Sugar
  • An egg
  • Wine
  • Mint
  • Water
  • Honey
  • Soda water
  • Bitters
  • A cherry

Spirits, sugar, water and bitters. Spirits are strong liquor, such as whiskey, rum or gin. Bitters are a strong herbal flavoring, and there is more on them soon. Notice what is missing: ice, fruit and juice.

“Cock tail, then, is a stimulating liquor, composed of spirits of any kind, sugar, water, and bitters.”

The oldest definition of a cocktail ever found

The editor answered in the same paper, The Balance. Nobody knows who first mixed a cocktail. The written record starts here.

The editor

Harry Croswell

27 years old in 1806. The portrait shows him in old age.

  • His paperHe edited The Balance, the Hudson newspaper that printed both the bar bill and the answer.
  • His habitHe used his paper to attack politicians. One attack on President Thomas Jefferson got him convicted of libel, and Alexander Hamilton argued his appeal.
  • Why he matters hereThe bar bill joke ran in his paper. His reply to the confused reader became the oldest cocktail recipe we have.
“… a person having swallowed a glass of it, is ready to swallow any thing else.”

Read it twice. To swallow something is to drink it. It also means to believe it. Croswell is saying that a voter full of cocktails will believe whatever the candidate tells him.

So the first cocktail recipe in history was the setup for a political joke.

Here is a bartender making that 1806 mix now. Watch for sugar, bitters, a little water and whiskey. Today it is called an Old Fashioned, and it has two things the first version lacked: the ice and the name.

Spirits, sugar, water and bitters. In print since 1806.

The ice

A 22-year-old decides to sell ice to the tropics.

There were no freezers in 1806. If you wanted ice, you waited for winter. Workers sawed a frozen pond into blocks, like these, and stored the blocks packed in sawdust. In summer, and in hot places, ice was something most people simply did not have.

The merchant

Frederic Tudor

22 years old in 1806. Pictured in old age.

  • Who he wasThe son of a wealthy Boston family, with a pond on the family land.
  • His ideaCut the pond ice in winter. Ship it to hot places where water never freezes. Sell it there.
  • Why he matters hereHe started the ice trade. If your drink has ice in it, he is the person to thank.

In February 1806 he put about 130 tons of pond ice on a wooden sailing ship and sent it to Martinique, a hot island in the Caribbean. It sounded so unlikely that his hometown paper had to tell readers it was real. The report began: “No joke.”

The ice arrived. The plan still failed, because Martinique had no icehouse and the cargo had nowhere cold to wait. Tudor had to sell it straight off the ship before it melted. He lost $4,500, and later his debts put him in prison.

Tudor kept shipping ice for decades. In May 1833 the ship Tuscany left Boston with his ice, bound for Calcutta in India. The voyage was 16,000 miles. It took four months and crossed the equator twice.

180 tons of ice went into the hold. Four months, two equator crossings, no refrigeration. How much was still ice in India?

About 100 tons arrived frozen, kept cold under sawdust. Within three days, Calcutta was planning an icehouse.

Shipping ice was half the job. Tudor also had to make people want it. His salesmen went from saloon to saloon, gave ice away, and showed bartenders how to make cold drinks. It worked.

By the 1850s Boston was shipping about 160,000 tons of ice in a year, to the American South and overseas. A man once jailed for his debts was now called the Ice King.

It chills the drink. As it melts, it adds water too.

The bitters

The most famous bottle of bitters began as army medicine.

Bitters were already in the 1806 recipe. They are strong alcohol in which bitter herbs and spices have been soaked, and a few dashes do for a drink what salt and pepper do for food. This bottle, Angostura, came later, in 1824. It became the one bartenders reach for, and it started as medicine.

The doctor

Johann Siegert

A German army doctor

  • Where he wentTo Venezuela in 1820, as chief surgeon to the army of Simón Bolívar. Bolívar was the general fighting to free South America from Spain.
  • What he madeIn 1824, in the river town of Angostura, he finished a tonic for his soldiers’ stomach trouble.
  • Why he matters hereThat tonic is the bitters in your Old Fashioned. Recipes still name it today.

A bitter tree bark is also called angostura. Is it in the bottle?

  • Yes
  • No

No. The bitters are named after the town, which is now called Ciudad Bolívar. The bark is not in the recipe.

Now the drink had its ice and its best-known bitters. But no bartender had ever published his recipes.

A few dashes of an army doctor’s stomach tonic, named after a town.

The barman

America’s most famous bartender sets whiskey on fire.

This bartender is doing his signature trick, the Blue Blazer. He mixes whiskey with boiling water, sets it on fire, and pours it back and forth between two mugs. His own recipe calls it “a continued stream of liquid fire”.

The barman

Jerry Thomas

Born in 1830 in Sackets Harbor, New York

  • Before the barAt 18 he sailed to San Francisco and ran off to the gold fields. He said he left California with $16,000 in gold.
  • By 1858He ran the bar at the Metropolitan Hotel in New York. He was the most famous bartender in America.
  • Why he matters hereIn 1862 he published his recipes. No bartender had done that before, and the cocktail was in the book.

The first bartender’s guide ever published

This is his book, How to Mix Drinks. It came out in 1862 with more than 600 recipes.

“The ‘Cocktail’ is a modern invention, and is generally used on fishing and other sporting parties, although some patients insist that it is good in the morning as a tonic.”

That is how the book introduces the cocktail. Some people drank it in the morning, as medicine.

“3 or 4 dashes of gum syrup. 2 do. bitters. 1 wine-glass of whiskey, and a piece of lemon peel. Fill one-third full of fine ice.”

Gum syrup is sugar syrup, and “do.” is short for ditto, so two more dashes. Compare this with 1806. Sugar, bitters and spirit are all here. The spirit is now whiskey, and ice has joined them.

Thomas did not die rich.

  • 1872He opens a Broadway bar hung with life-size portraits of himself, and a statue.
  • 1885He dies in New York, having lost his money in the stock market.

After Thomas, bartenders started adding to the cocktail to improve it.

The 1806 recipe, written down by a bartender in 1862, now with whiskey and ice.

The name

Bartenders start improving the cocktail. Some drinkers hate it.

By the late 1800s the best American bars looked like palaces. This one is the Hoffman House in New York. Fancy bars wanted fancy drinks, so bartenders began adding new ingredients to the plain old cocktail.

Bartenders “improved” the whiskey cocktail with three new additions. Which three?

  • Egg white
  • Cherry liqueur
  • Soda water
  • Absinthe
  • Mint
  • Pineapple syrup
  • Orange liqueur
  • Red wine
  • Honey

Cherry liqueur, absinthe and orange liqueur. Absinthe is a strong green spirit that tastes of anise.

“2 dashes Maraschino. 1 dash Absinthe.”

Jerry Thomas’s own guide added “Improved” cocktails in 1876. This page says to make the whiskey one like the Improved Brandy Cocktail, and that recipe adds the two lines above. Maraschino is the cherry liqueur. Other bartenders used orange liqueur too.

Some drinkers did not want the improvements. They told the bartender to make their cocktail the old-fashioned way.

The order caught on. In 1880 the Chicago Tribune wrote about people drinking “old-fashioned cocktails”. It is the first time the name appears in print.

“Dissolve a small lump of sugar with a little water in a whiskey-glass; add two dashes Angostura bitters, a small piece ice, a piece lemon-peel, one jigger whiskey. Mix with small bar-spoon and serve, leaving spoon in the glass.”

By 1895 the order had its own printed recipe, from a New York hotel bartender named George Kappeler. Count them: sugar, water, bitters, whiskey. They are the four from 1806, plus ice and a strip of lemon peel.

So that is why it is called an Old Fashioned. The name started as an order. It meant: skip the new extras and make my cocktail the old way.

“Old-fashioned” was how drinkers ordered the original cocktail. It was in print by 1880.

The myth

A Kentucky club says it invented the drink.

Ask who invented the Old Fashioned and many people will point here, to the Pendennis Club. It is a private club in Louisville, Kentucky. The story says the club’s bartender created the drink for a member, a bourbon maker named Colonel James E. Pepper.

In the story, Pepper then brought the recipe to the bar of the Waldorf hotel in New York. It is a good story. It first appeared in 1931, in a book about that bar.

Test the story. The name “old-fashioned” was in print in 1880. Guess the year the Pendennis Club was founded.

1881. The name is a year older than the club, and the recipe is 75 years older.

Put the dates in order and the Pendennis story falls apart.

  • 1806The recipe is printed in Hudson.
  • 1862Jerry Thomas publishes it, with ice.
  • 1880“Old-fashioned cocktails” appear in the Chicago Tribune.
  • 1881The Pendennis Club is founded.
  • 1931The Pendennis story is first told in print.

When a story sounds neat, check the dates.

Nobody invented the Old Fashioned in Kentucky. The drink and its name are both older than the club.

The comeback

The drink gets banned, then ruined, then rescued.

In 1920 the United States banned alcohol. The ban was called Prohibition, and it lasted until 1933. In this photo, government agents pour a barrel of liquor into a sewer. Good whiskey became hard to find. Some say that is when bartenders began mashing orange and cherry into the drink, to hide the taste of bad liquor.

On the left is the Old Fashioned of the 1940s, with orange and cherry mashed into it. It was sweet and watery. On the right is the drink good bars make now. It is the 1895 recipe with orange peel in place of lemon.

What saved the drink was the old books. In the late 1990s, a new generation of bartenders reread guides like Jerry Thomas’s. They took the fruit back out.

The plain version won. For eight years running, the Old Fashioned was the best-selling classic cocktail in a yearly survey of 100 top bars. A gin drink, the Negroni, passed it in 2022.

Banned in 1920, buried in fruit by the 1940s, restored from the old books in the 1990s.

Your turn

You have every piece. Now build the drink.

Make one the way bars do today. Tap each ingredient in the order it goes in. Sugar is first and the peel is last.

  1. Sugar cubePut one sugar cube in the glass.
  2. BittersShake a few dashes of bitters onto the cube.
  3. WaterAdd a few dashes of water. Crush and stir until the sugar dissolves.
  4. IceFill the glass with ice.
  5. WhiskeyPour in 45 ml of bourbon or rye. Stir gently.
  6. Orange peelFinish with a strip of orange peel.

That is the official recipe of the International Bartenders Association. It is the 1806 recipe with ice and a strip of peel.

Here is a test you can keep. A drink is in the Old Fashioned family if it is only spirit, sugar and bitters, with ice for the water. Which one belongs?

  • Tequila, orange liqueur, lime juice
  • Rye whiskey, sweet vermouth, bitters
  • Tequila, mezcal, agave syrup, bitters

The third. It is called an Oaxaca Old Fashioned, and agave syrup is its sugar. The first is a Margarita, which adds lime juice. The second is a Manhattan, which uses vermouth, a flavored wine, in place of sugar.

Three questions everyone asks.

  • Bourbon or rye?Either one. The official recipe allows both. Bourbon makes a sweeter drink and rye a drier one.
  • Sugar cube or syrup?Either one. Syrup is sugar already dissolved in water, so it mixes faster.
  • Fruit or no fruit?Use a strip of orange peel. Mashed orange and cherry belong to the 1940s version.

The Old Fashioned is the cocktail as it was first written down in 1806. It got its name when drinkers had to ask for it the old-fashioned way.
