The Old Fashioned

Who invented the Old Fashioned? Nobody you can name. So why is it called that, and what goes in one? The answers start with a bar bill from 1806.

The bar bill

A losing candidate’s drinks tab is about to make history.

In early America you won votes with free drinks. Watch the left edge of this election day. One voter is getting his.

May 1806, Hudson, New York. A candidate has lost, and the town paper prints what he spent on drink for the voters.

  • 720rum grogs
  • 411glasses of bitters
  • 32gin slings
  • 17brandies
  • 25“cock-tails”

All of it sat under the heading Loss. Under Gain the paper printed one word.

“I have heard of a jorum, of phlegm-cutter and fog driver … but never in my life, though I have lived a good many years, did I hear of cock tail before.”

A week later a subscriber wrote in. He knew plenty of names for a drink. The last one on that bill was new to him.

The editor wrote back with a recipe. It had four ingredients. Three were spirits, sugar and bitters. What was the fourth?

  • Ice
  • Water
  • Lemon
  • An egg

Water. The first cocktail on record has no ice in it.

“Cock tail, then, is a stimulating liquor, composed of spirits of any kind, sugar, water, and bitters.”

No inventor’s name survives. The trail stops here, in The Balance, and Columbian Repository, at the oldest definition of a cocktail anyone has found.

The editor

Harry Croswell

27 in 1806 · painted here in old age

  • His jobHe edited small-town papers for the Federalists, the party out of power, and mocked the men in office.
  • Already known forA conviction for libelling President Thomas Jefferson. His lawyer on appeal was Alexander Hamilton, who had built the US Treasury.
  • Why he matters hereHis reply to one puzzled reader is the first cocktail recipe in print.
“… a person having swallowed a glass of it, is ready to swallow any thing else.”

Croswell could not resist a dig at the candidate and the voters he had treated. The first cocktail recipe reached print as the setup for a political joke.

Spirits, sugar, water, bitters. Order that tonight and you get an Old Fashioned. But two things in your glass were not there in 1806: the ice and the name.

Spirits, sugar, water and bitters. Four ingredients, set down in 1806, with no ice.

The ice

A 22-year-old decides to sell winter to the tropics.

That same winter, near Boston, a frozen pond was being cut into blocks. The man paying for it was 22, and his plan sounded like a joke.

The merchant

Frederic Tudor

22 in 1806 · pictured in old age

  • Where he came fromA wealthy Boston family with a pond on its land.
  • His ideaCut the pond ice in winter and ship it to places where water never freezes.
  • Why he matters hereThe 1806 cocktail had no ice. Tudor spent his life putting ice within reach of every bar.

He loaded about 130 tons onto a ship bound for Martinique, an island in the Caribbean. The Boston Gazette had to tell its readers: “No joke.”

It went badly. The island had no icehouse, so he sold what he could straight from the ship. He lost $4,500. Later debts put him in prison.

He kept going. In May 1833 his ice left Boston on the ship Tuscany, bound for Calcutta in India. That is 16,000 miles, four months at sea and two crossings of the equator.

The Tuscany carried 180 tons of pond ice. How much was still ice when it reached India?

  • None
  • About 20 tons
  • About 100 tons
  • Nearly all of it

About 100 tons, packed in sawdust. Within three days Calcutta was planning an icehouse.

At home he had to create the thirst. His salesmen went from saloon to saloon, gave ice away and showed barmen what to do with it. It worked. America came to call him the Ice King.

Ice makes the drink cold. Then it melts, and becomes the water in the 1806 recipe.

The bitters

The seasoning in your glass started out as army medicine.

What are bitters? Strong alcohol steeped with bitter herbs and spices. Think of them as the salt and pepper of a drink: a few dashes season the whole glass. The most famous bottle began as medicine.

The doctor

Johann Siegert

Born in Germany · army surgeon

  • Where he wentVenezuela, in 1820, to be surgeon general to Simón Bolívar, the general fighting to free South America from Spain.
  • What he madeIn 1824, in the river town of Angostura, he finished a tincture for his soldiers’ stomachs.
  • Why he matters hereThat stomach medicine is the bitters in your glass. His sons moved the business to Trinidad in 1875, and it is still made there.

So what is Angostura bitters named after?

  • A bitter bark
  • The town
  • The doctor’s wife
  • A ship

The town, now called Ciudad Bolívar. There is a bitter angostura bark too, and none of it is in the bottle.

Spirits, sugar, bitters, and ice for the water. Every part was behind the bar. No barman had ever published how he mixed one.

Angostura bitters began as an army surgeon’s stomach medicine. They are named for a town. No angostura bark goes in.

The barman

America’s most famous bartender sets whiskey on fire.

Meet the showman. His signature trick was the Blue Blazer: whiskey set alight and poured from mug to mug in an arc of flame.

The barman

Jerry Thomas

Born 1830 · Sackets Harbor, New York

  • Before the barIn 1849 he sailed round Cape Horn to San Francisco and jumped ship for the gold fields. He left with $16,000 in gold, by his own account.
  • By 1858He ran the bar at the Metropolitan Hotel in New York, the most famous barman in America.
  • Why he matters hereIn 1862 he did what no barman had done. He published his recipes.

His book, How to Mix Drinks, put more than 600 recipes on paper in 1862. It was the first bartender’s guide.

“The ‘Cocktail’ is a modern invention, and is generally used on fishing and other sporting parties, although some patients insist that it is good in the morning as a tonic.”

That is his note on the cocktail. Read the end again. Some people were drinking it in the morning, as medicine.

“3 or 4 dashes of gum syrup. 2 do. bitters. 1 wine-glass of whiskey, and a piece of lemon peel. Fill one-third full of fine ice.”

Look familiar? It is the 1806 recipe, with whiskey named and ice added.

Thomas did not die rich.

  • 1872Opens a Broadway bar hung with life-size portraits of himself, and a statue.
  • 1885Dies in New York, having lost his money in the stock market.
  • 1928A journalist dubs him “the Professor.” Nobody called him that while he lived.

After him, barmen began to improve on the cocktail.

The 1806 recipe was now in a book, with whiskey named and ice added.

The name

Barmen start improving the cocktail. Drinkers push back.

Then success spoiled it. Bars became palaces, like the Hoffman House here, and the drinks grew fancy to match.

“2 dashes Maraschino. 1 dash Absinthe.”

By 1876 Thomas’s own guide listed an Improved Whiskey Cocktail: the old drink plus these two. Other barmen reached for orange liqueur.

Some drinkers wanted none of it. They told the barman to make their cocktail the old-fashioned way.

The words “old-fashioned cocktails” first turn up in a newspaper in 1880. In which city?

  • Louisville
  • New York
  • Chicago
  • New Orleans

Chicago. The Tribune of 15 February 1880 names them among the drinks downed to mark a piece of political news.

“Dissolve a small lump of sugar with a little water in a whiskey-glass; add two dashes Angostura bitters, a small piece ice, a piece lemon-peel, one jigger whiskey. Mix with small bar-spoon and serve, leaving spoon in the glass.”

From George Kappeler, billed by his publisher as head barman of New York’s Holland House hotel. Count them: sugar, water, bitters, whiskey.

So that is why it is called an Old Fashioned. The name began as an order. It meant: make mine the way they used to.

“Old-fashioned” is what drinkers said to get the 1806 cocktail back. It was in print by 1880, in Chicago.

The myth

A famous club says it invented the drink. Check the dates.

Ask who invented the Old Fashioned and you will often hear about this place: the Pendennis Club, a private gentlemen’s club in Louisville, Kentucky. It says its barman made the first one for a member, the bourbon distiller Colonel James E. Pepper.

Pepper, the story goes, then carried the recipe to the bar of the Waldorf hotel. The tale first appears in a 1931 book about that bar.

You already know what sinks it. The Pendennis Club was founded in 1881. Why can’t the story be true?

  • Colonel Pepper never existed
  • The name was in print in 1880
  • Kentucky had banned whiskey
  • The Waldorf had no bar

The name was in a Chicago newspaper a year before the club existed, and the recipe was 75 years older than that.

Line the dates up and the story has nowhere to stand.

  • 1806The recipe is defined in Hudson.
  • 1862Jerry Thomas prints it, with ice.
  • 1880“Old-fashioned cocktails” in the Chicago Tribune.
  • 1881The Pendennis Club is founded.
  • 1931The Pendennis story first appears in print.

A date is the cheapest test of a good story.

The Kentucky story fails on dates. The name was in print a year before the club existed.

The comeback

The drink gets banned, then ruined, then rescued.

Then America banned alcohol. Prohibition ran from 1920 to 1933. Here its agents empty a barrel into the sewer. Good whiskey was hard to come by.

Left, the 1940s: orange and cherry crushed in, sweet and watery. Right, the 1895 recipe, as good bars make it again.

The rescue came from the library. In the late 1990s a new generation of bartenders went back to the old books, Jerry Thomas’s guide among them, and took the fruit out again.

For eight years running it was the best-selling classic in a yearly poll of 100 leading bars. The Negroni, a gin drink, ended the run in 2022.

Banned, then drowned in fruit, then rescued from the old books.

Your turn

You have every piece. Put them in the glass.

Build one. Tap what goes into the glass, in a barman’s order.

  1. Sugar cubeOne sugar cube in the bottom of the glass.
  2. BittersA few dashes of Angostura, straight onto the cube.
  3. WaterA few dashes of plain water. Crush and stir until the sugar is gone.
  4. IceFill the glass with ice.
  5. Whiskey45 ml of bourbon or rye. Stir gently.
  6. Orange peelA strip of orange zest over the top.

That is the International Bartenders Association’s recipe: the four ingredients of 1806, and ice.

A tool to keep, the 1806 test: spirit, sugar, water and bitters, and nothing else of weight. Which drink passes?

  • Margaritatequila, orange liqueur, lime juice
  • Manhattanrye, sweet vermouth, bitters
  • Oaxaca Old Fashionedtequila, mezcal, agave syrup, bitters

The Oaxaca Old Fashioned. Agave syrup is its sugar and the ice is its water. Lime juice or vermouth puts a drink in another family.

Four things people always ask.

  • Bourbon or rye?Either. The official recipe allows both. Bourbon tastes sweeter and rye drier.
  • Sugar cube or syrup?Both are sugar and water. The cube is the 1895 way. Syrup dissolves faster.
  • Fruit or no fruit?A strip of peel, yes. Crushed orange and cherry is the 1940s version.
  • Is a Manhattan the same drink?No. A Manhattan trades the sugar for sweet vermouth.

Five lines to take to the bar.

  • 1The first cocktail recipe, from 1806, has four ingredients and no ice.
  • 2The editor who wrote it had Alexander Hamilton for a lawyer.
  • 3In 1833 a Boston merchant shipped pond ice 16,000 miles to Calcutta. More than half arrived.
  • 4Angostura bitters began as army stomach medicine, and it holds no angostura bark.
  • 5“Old-fashioned cocktails” were in a Chicago paper a year before the Kentucky club that claims them was founded.

The Old Fashioned is the cocktail as first defined in 1806, and its name is what drinkers ordered when barmen tried to improve it.
