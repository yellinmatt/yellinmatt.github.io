The Scurvy Trial

How do you find out whether a medicine works? In 1747 a navy surgeon split twelve sick sailors into pairs, gave each pair a different cure and waited to see who got up. Every pill you take was tested the same basic way.

Scurvy was the great killer of the long voyage. It loosens the teeth, swells the joints, bleeds under the skin and breaks down wounds, and Anson’s numbers show what it could do to a fleet.

Cures were easy to find. John Woodall had recommended lemons to ship surgeons as far back as 1617, and cider, vinegar and sea water all had champions of their own. What nobody had was a way to tell which of them worked. In May 1747, below decks on a warship called the Salisbury, a surgeon of thirty decided to find one.

On 20 May 1747 Lind took twelve men whose cases were as alike as he could make them, with the same putrid gums, the same spots and the same weakness in the knees. He laid them in one place and fed them one diet, and then he gave each pair a single thing the others did not get.

The two men on citrus ate their oranges and lemons, Lind noted, with greediness, until the fruit ran out after six days. By then one of them was fit for duty and the other was well enough to nurse the rest, and no other pair improved as quickly or as visibly. Textbooks call this the first clinical trial, a generous title for twelve men, and the design still earns its fame, because every drug in your cabinet reached you through a descendant of it. Lind wrote it up himself, six years later.

The flawWho chose the sea water?

Read his account the way a referee would and one line should bother you. “Two of the worst patients” went on sea water, and Lind chose them himself. A doctor who decides who gets what will steer each remedy toward the patients he expects it to suit, however honest he is, and the remedy then takes the credit or the blame for his choice.

The fix took two centuries. The Medical Research Council’s streptomycin trial of 1948 let random numbers do the assigning and kept the sequence hidden from the doctors enrolling patients, so nobody’s hunch could pick the winners.

The waitForty-eight years

You might expect every ship to have carried lemons by the next spring. The Navy waited forty-eight years, and part of the blame is Lind’s. His Treatise of the Scurvy appeared at Edinburgh in 1753, dedicated to Anson, and the trial sits inside it as a few pages of a long argument about causes. He blamed moisture and a coarse diet that the body could not digest, which made fruit one good remedy among many.

To carry fruit to sea he proposed a rob, juice reduced for hours in nearly boiling water, and that heat destroyed much of what the juice held. The Navy’s Sick and Hurt Board judged the rob ineffective and too expensive. The case was finally made by Gilbert Blane, who had urged citrus since about 1780: in 1794 the Suffolk sailed twenty-three weeks non-stop to India with lemon juice in the grog and no serious outbreak, and in 1795 the Admiralty ordered a daily ration for every sailor, about three-quarters of an ounce.

The whyWhat was in the fruit

Nobody on board could have said why the juice worked. The answer arrived by accident in 1907, when two Norwegian physicians produced scurvy in guinea pigs, and in 1932 Charles Glen King’s laboratory proved that Albert Szent-Györgyi’s hexuronic acid was vitamin C, 185 years after the fore-hold.

Your body holds about 1,500 milligrams of vitamin C and cannot make its own. Cut off fresh food and that store drains, and once it falls below about 350 milligrams, usually within four to twelve weeks, gums bleed and spots appear. A long voyage on a coarse diet was the perfect way to empty it.

The amount involved is tiny: about 10 milligrams a day prevents the disease, and a medium orange holds 70. Small numbers made the next mistake easy. When the Navy later swapped lemons for West Indian limes, with roughly a quarter of the vitamin, scurvy returned on voyages to Antarctica into the twentieth century. A fair comparison can tell you that something works long before anyone can tell you why, and a wrong theory can still undo the finding.

Show what the review found

The review pools 29 trial comparisons and 11,306 people, against placebo, mostly randomised and double-blind. Taken daily by ordinary people, vitamin C barely changed the risk of catching a cold, a risk ratio of 0.97. It shortened adults’ colds by 8 percent, about half a day on a week-long cold, and starting it once symptoms appeared showed no consistent effect.
