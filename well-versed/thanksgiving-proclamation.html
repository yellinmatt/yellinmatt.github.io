The Thanksgiving Proclamation

A magazine editor asked for it, the secretary of state wrote it and Lincoln signed it in the middle of a war, and the Thursday on your calendar was settled seventy-eight years later.

The page opens in a clerk’s patient copperplate, “By the President of the United States of America. A Proclamation,” and its first sentence is about the weather: a year “filled with the blessings of fruitful fields and healthful skies.” It is dated 3 October 1863, three months after Gettysburg. Americans already gave thanks by proclamation, but state by state, mostly in New England and the North, on whatever day each governor named. This page asked the whole country, along with those “at sea” and those living abroad, to give thanks on one day, the last Thursday of November. The national holiday was a wartime invention, and the date it falls on today stayed open to argument until 1941.

The push came from Philadelphia. Sarah Josepha Hale, seventy-four years old, edited Godey’s Lady’s Book, whose reach no American magazine of the century matched, and she had used it to campaign for a single day since her first Thanksgiving editorial in 1837, which proposed “the last Thursday in the month.” She sent her case to the governors of every state and territory, to American ministers abroad and to commanders in the Navy. On 28 September 1863 she wrote to the president. Her request was “to have the day of our annual Thanksgiving made a National and fixed Union Festival,” and she added that she had asked her friend William Seward, the secretary of state, to raise it with him. Five days later the proclamation was signed.

Seward went further and wrote it himself. John Nicolay, one of Lincoln’s secretaries, recorded in April 1864 that the original was in Seward’s handwriting, and Gideon Welles noted in his diary on the day of signing that he had complimented Seward on the work. A year later that manuscript was sold to raise money for Union soldiers. Read the long second paragraph and you can see the diplomat at his desk: peace kept “with all nations,” the war’s “theatre” shrinking as the Union armies advanced, the plough, the shuttle and the ship still at work. Then the text turns to the part a president speaks.

No human counsel hath devised nor hath any mortal hand worked out these great things. They are the gracious gifts of the Most High God, who, while dealing with us in anger for our sins, hath nevertheless remembered mercy. It has seemed to me fit and proper that they should be solemnly, reverently and gratefully acknowledged as with one heart and one voice by the whole American People.

I do therefore invite my fellow citizens in every part of the United States, and also those who are at sea and those who are in foreign lands, to set apart and observe the last Thursday of November next, as a day of Thanksgiving and Praise to our beneficent Father who dwelleth in the Heavens. And I recommend to them that while offering up the justly due to Him for such singular deliverances and blessings, they do also, with humble penitence for our national perverseness and disobedience, commend to His tender care all those who have become widows, orphans, mourners or sufferers in the lamentable civil strife in which we are unavoidably engaged, and fervently implore the of the Almighty Hand to heal the wounds of the nation and to restore it as soon as may be consistent with the Divine purposes to the full enjoyment of peace, harmony, tranquillity and Union.

Proclamation of Thanksgiving, drafted by William H. Seward and signed by Abraham Lincoln. Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln, ed. Basler.

Read those two paragraphs aloud at the table before the carving. It takes a little over a minute. Listen for what the thanks leave out: no battle, no general and no enemy is named, and the sentence that invites gratitude goes on to ask for penitence and to name the widows, the orphans and the mourners. It is a thanksgiving written for a country that was burying its sons that year, which is why it still reads true at a table with an empty chair.

The date had a longer quarrel. Washington had proclaimed the first national thanksgiving under the Constitution, at Congress’s request, for Thursday 26 November 1789, and he signed it on 3 October, seventy-four years to the day before Lincoln. In 1870 Congress made the day a holiday in the District of Columbia and left the choice of date to the president, and from then on every proclamation named the last Thursday. Then November 1939 had five Thursdays, the last one the 30th, and retailers wanted their Christmas season back. Lew Hahn of the National Retail Dry Goods Association reckoned that twelve to fifteen per cent of a year’s retail trade fell between Thanksgiving and Christmas. On 14 August, at a press conference on Campobello Island, Roosevelt explained that the last Thursday was a post-Civil War habit, “so there is nothing sacred about it,” and moved the holiday to 23 November.

Plymouth answered first. On 15 August its selectmen wired the White House that the change sacrificed “the real significance of the day for the purpose of satisfying commercial interests.” Calendar makers protested that their calendars for 1940 were already printed, and football coaches saw their Thanksgiving fixtures stranded. A Gallup poll counted 62 per cent of Americans against the change, more than half the governors proclaimed a different day, and Texas, Mississippi and Colorado observed both. New York’s mayor, Fiorello La Guardia, then surveyed the city’s stores and found no gain in sales. On 20 May 1941 Roosevelt told reporters that the experiment “did not work.” The House voted that October for the last Thursday; the Senate amended it to the fourth Thursday, to settle the years when November has five; and Roosevelt signed it on 26 December 1941.

That gives you a rule to keep. Thanksgiving always falls between the 22nd and the 28th of November, and in a month with five Thursdays it lands on the second-to-last, which was exactly Roosevelt’s date in 1939. The next such year is 2028, when the holiday comes on 23 November. This year the calendar matches 1789 and 1863, and the day falls on the 26th. So here is your line for the table: the fourth Thursday is Roosevelt’s 1939 date written into law, and the proclamation that made the holiday national asked for the last one.
