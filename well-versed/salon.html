The Salon

Paris spent two centuries making conversation a technique, and the famous painting of the result shows an evening that never happened.

Fifty-four people are packed around a gilded room on the rue Saint-Honoré. A bust of Voltaire presides from a plinth, a man at a green baize table reads aloud from Voltaire’s new tragedy L’Orphelin de la Chine, and the keys that circulate with the picture name Diderot, d’Alembert, Montesquieu and most of the rest of the French Enlightenment among the listeners. It is the image on the cover of a great many books about the period. Anicet-Charles-Gabriel Lemonnier painted it in 1812 for the Empress Joséphine and showed it at the Salon of 1814.

In French Studies for July 1991, John Lough went through it figure by figure. Most of the fifty-four could not have been at one of Madame Geoffrin’s Wednesday dinners in 1755, because some were not in Paris that year, some were the wrong age, and some were dead. She did not own the bust. Lough’s verdict was that the painting has no documentary value whatever for the place of the writer in old-regime society, which is a hard thing to say about the most reproduced picture of it. Even the word is a later arrival, since Antoine Lilti has shown that nobody used salon in this sense before about 1800.

What they had instead was a set of practices, deliberately designed. Between 1618 and 1620 Catherine de Vivonne, marquise de Rambouillet, rebuilt the Hôtel Pisani in Paris to her own plans, and the plans are the argument. She moved the staircase off the centre so that the great room ran uninterrupted, and laid out a suite of small chambers into which a party could break apart and reassemble. She received in the chambre bleue, hung in blue brocade and sited away from the fire she disliked, where guests took their places in the ruelle, the alley of floor between bed and wall. She held it from 1620 to 1648, and required that guests of unequal rank treat one another by a standard she called politesse. The house was a machine for talk before anyone had a word for what it was doing.

The rules were written down inside a generation. Madeleine de Scudéry came up through the Rambouillet circle and made conversation her lifelong subject, first inside enormous novels and then directly, in the essay that opens her Conversations sur divers sujets of 1680. She begins by defining it negatively, which is the move nobody expects, and the definition has not aged at all.

As Conversation is the bond of all , the greatest pleasure of well-bred People, and the most ordinary means of introducing into the World, not only Politeness, but also the purest Morals, and the love of Glory and Virtue, I think the Company cannot entertain themselves more profitably, nor more agreeably, said Cilenia, than in examining what it is People call Conversation. For when Men only speak strictly according to the , it cannot be so termed.

The truth is, said Amilcar, a Lawyer pleading a Cause at the Bar, a Merchant negotiating with another, a General of an Army giving Orders, a King speaking of Affairs of State in His Council—all this is not what ought to be styled Conversation. All those People may discourse well of their Interests and Affairs and yet not have that agreeable talent of Conversation, which is the sweetest charm of Life, and perhaps more rare than is believed.

I conceive, replied she, that to speak in general, it ought oftener to be of common and things than of great Transactions… the secret is of speaking always nobly of things, very plainly of high things, and very gallantly of gallant things, without and affectation… Insomuch as to speak with reason, we may for certain affirm that there is nothing but may be said in Conversation, in case it be managed with Wit and Judgment, and the Party considers well where he is, to whom he speaks, and who he is himself.

Madeleine de Scudéry, “Of Conversation,” in Conversations Upon Several Subjects, trans. Ferrand Spence, London: H. Rhodes, 1683.

The circle’s other bequest is stranger. In her novel Clélie of 1654 Scudéry printed a map of the country of Tenderness, drawn first as a parlour game, and it is the clearest surviving evidence of what these people thought they were up to: they had taken the part of life least susceptible to method and given it a topography.

The woman who put a timetable to all of this had almost no schooling. Marie Thérèse Rodet was born in Paris in 1699 to a valet de chambre, raised by a grandmother who saw no reason to educate a girl, and married at fourteen to a widower of forty-nine who was general cashier of the Saint-Gobain mirror works. When Madame de Tencin died in December 1749 she inherited that hostess’s guests and made two changes that Dena Goodman argues invented the Enlightenment salon outright. She fixed a single day of the week. And she moved the sociable meal from the late-night supper to dinner at one o’clock, which opened the whole afternoon for talking. Mondays were for artists, Boucher and La Tour and Greuze among them; Wednesdays for men of letters.

Two subjects were barred at her table: religion and politics, the two on which her guests had most to say. Jean-François Marmontel, who ate there for years, remembered that her mild Voilà qui est bien kept the company, in his phrase, on a leash. Set the prohibitions beside the timetable and what emerges looks less like a debating chamber and more like a very well-run standing dinner, whose hostess had grasped that a fixed hour, a chosen guest list and a couple of forbidden topics will do more for an evening than any amount of brilliance around the table.

This is where the historians part company, and it is worth knowing which side you would take. Goodman’s Republic of Letters of 1994 made the salon the working institution of the Enlightenment, governed by women, and a schoolroom for clever women like Geoffrin whom nobody had troubled to teach. Lilti’s Le monde des salons of 2005 came back at that hard: the salons belonged to le monde, to worldly society, and the philosophers turned up because attending was how a man became a man of letters. His evidence is the money. Geoffrin sent silver to the Suards, two thousand gold écus to Thomas, and standing allowances to d’Alembert and the abbé Morellet, with nothing coming back the other way, which makes the relation patronage. Steven Kale put it colder still in 2002: the hostesses served the public needs of the men who set the limits of women’s public life.

Lilti’s own conclusion gives much of it back, granting that these were places of mixed sociability whose distinction owed most to the presence of women and to their conversation, which is very near Goodman. The sharpest test was staged inside Geoffrin’s own house anyway. Her daughter, the marquise de La Ferté-Imbault, founded a rival society in 1771, the Order of the Lanturelus, devoted to mocking the philosophers her mother fed, and ran it until about the time Geoffrin died on 6 October 1777. The battle for the mind of the eighteenth-century elite, in Goodman’s phrase, was for a few years fought out in one house on the rue Saint-Honoré.

What survives all this is portable and none of it is wit: a day settled far enough in advance that nobody has to be talked into coming, an hour early enough that the conversation has somewhere to go afterwards, a guest list somebody actually decided on, a subject or two ruled out because they turn a table into a courtroom, and a host whose job is the room itself. Geoffrin wrote badly and knew it, and she understood that last point better than any of the men she fed.
