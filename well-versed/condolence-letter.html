The Condolence Letter

The most quoted sympathy letter in American history names none of the dead. The one to copy went to a grieving daughter in Illinois.

The sheet above swears in small print that its original was on show at Huber’s Museum in New York, and the swearing is false. Michael F. Tobin copyrighted this page in 1891, the autograph dealer Charles Hamilton later judged it a forgery, and its three paragraphs have been run together into one. The real letter, dated 21 November 1864, reached Lydia Bixby of Boston on the 25th and ran in the Evening Transcript and the Evening Traveller that same afternoon. Whatever paper she held has not been seen since.

Its 139 words have been called the finest letter of condolence in English, and Saving Private Ryan has General Marshall read them aloud. As a note to a mother, they fail. A letter of sympathy has one job, which is to tell someone in grief that you know exactly whom they lost, and this one never names a son.

ReadTo Mrs. Bixby, 1864 · tap the dotted words for a gloss

Dear Madam,

I have been shown in the files of the War Department a statement of the of Massachusetts that you are the mother of five sons who have died gloriously on the field of battle.

I feel how weak and fruitless must be any word of mine which should attempt to you from the grief of a loss so overwhelming. But I cannot refrain from you the consolation that may be found in the thanks of the Republic they died to save.

I pray that our Heavenly Father may assuage the anguish of your bereavement, and leave you only the cherished memory of the loved and lost, and the solemn pride that must be yours to have laid so costly a sacrifice upon the .

Yours, very sincerely and respectfully,
A. Lincoln

Collected Works of Abraham Lincoln, ed. Basler, via Abraham Lincoln Online.

Hear the sound of it first. The writer concedes that words are weak, turns to the thanks of a nation, and closes on an altar, and that last clause could stand on a monument. Then check it against the record. William Schouler, the state’s adjutant general, had described Bixby to Governor John Andrew as “the best specimen of a true-hearted Union woman I have yet seen.” Two of her sons had died: Sergeant Charles at Fredericksburg on 3 May 1863 and Private Oliver near Petersburg on 30 July 1864. Of the other three, by the historian Michael Burlingame’s count, one had gone over to the enemy, another may have deserted and the third was honorably discharged. In 1925 a granddaughter told the Boston Herald of her “great sympathy for the South,” and a great-grandson said she destroyed the letter in anger.

The signature has its own trouble. John Hay, the president’s twenty-six-year-old assistant secretary, signed routine letters in Lincoln’s name, and a copy of this one sits in his scrapbooks. He is reported to have told the editor William Crary Brownell and the diplomat Walter Hines Page that he wrote it, and John Morley passed the same admission to Nicholas Murray Butler. His one written word on the subject, from 1904, calls the letter “genuine,” which leaves the question where it was. In 2019 Jack Grieve and colleagues traced its short word sequences through 500 samples of each man’s writing and named Hay, with nearly nine runs in ten pointing to him and the rest inconclusive. That finding explains the letter better than any scandal does. A young aide writing to a stranger had nobody to remember, so he wrote about the Republic.

Two years earlier Lincoln had written one himself, and that page survives in his hand. William McCullough had been the circuit clerk of McLean County, Illinois, when Lincoln rode that circuit as a lawyer and sometimes stayed with the family. He went to war past the usual age as lieutenant colonel of the 4th Illinois Cavalry and was killed near Coffeeville, Mississippi, on 5 December 1862. His daughter Fanny, twenty-two, locked herself in her room, and David Davis asked the president to write.

ReadTo Fanny McCullough, 23 December 1862

Dear Fanny

It is with deep grief that I learn of the death of your kind and brave Father; and, especially, that it is affecting your young heart beyond what is common in such cases. In this sad world of ours, sorrow comes to all; and, to the young, it comes with bitterest agony, because it takes them unawares. The older have learned to ever expect it. I am anxious to afford some alleviation of your present distress. Perfect relief is not possible, except with time. You can not now realize that you will ever feel better. Is not this so? And yet it is a mistake. You are sure to be happy again. To know this, which is certainly true, will make you some less miserable now. I have had experience enough to know what I say; and you need only to believe it, to feel better at once. The memory of your dear Father, instead of an agony, will yet be a sad sweet feeling in your heart, of a purer and holier sort than you have known before.

Please present my kind regards to your afflicted mother.

Your sincere friend
A. Lincoln

Collected Works, ed. Basler, via Abraham Lincoln Online. The manuscript is in the Shapell collection.

Watch what this page does. “Kind and brave” comes from someone who had sat at the man’s table. The letter has noticed one fact about her, that she is young and this is her first blow, and it speaks to that fact and no other. It asks her a question, “Is not this so?”, as you might across a room. Grief counsellors now warn against promising that time heals, and Lincoln promises it, on the authority of a friend. They also warn against “I know how you feel” unless you do. His mother had died when he was nine and his son Willie, eleven, had died that February, and he stops at “experience enough.”

You need much less than that. Emily Post’s standing advice is that “a single sincere line expressing the genuine feeling you had for the deceased is all you need to write,” and the note below can run to five.

  1. The name. “I was so sorry to hear about David,” never “your loss.”
  2. The sorrow, said plainly and once.
  3. One particular. Something the person said, did or taught you.
  4. One offer with a day on it. “I’ll bring dinner Thursday,” or “I’ll call next week,” if you will.
  5. Your name, and stop.

Then run the name test. Cross out the dead person’s name, write in somebody else’s, and read it again: if the note is still true, line three is not doing its work. “He was a wonderful man who will be missed by all” survives the swap, and “He taught me to read a balance sheet on a napkin” does not. Leave out “a better place,” “everything happens for a reason,” the manner of death, and “call if you need anything,” since the bereaved will probably not call. Write it by hand on a plain card. The first several weeks are best and late still counts, because the commonest mistake is sending nothing.

So do it tonight. Think of the last death near you that you meant to write about and did not, take a card and a pen, write the five lines, run the test, and post it tomorrow, even if the year has turned. If the subject comes up at dinner, you have a line ready: the Bixby letter is the most famous sympathy note in America and it names none of the dead, and the one worth copying went to a twenty-two-year-old in Bloomington.
