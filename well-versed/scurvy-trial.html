The Scurvy Trial

Twelve sick sailors, six remedies and a few oranges: how a naval surgeon in 1747 showed medicine the way to find out what works.

On 20 May 1747 two sailors in the fore-hold of HMS Salisbury were each handed two oranges and a lemon, and they ate them, their surgeon noted, with greediness. Ten other men lay near them with the same putrid gums, the same spots on the skin and the same weak knees, and those ten were dosed with cider, vinegar, sea water, a dilute acid or a paste of garlic and mustard seed. The fruit ran out after six days. By then one of the two was fit for duty, and the other was soon well enough to nurse the rest.

The surgeon was James Lind, thirty years old and from Edinburgh, and the disease was the great killer of the long voyage. Scurvy loosens the teeth, swells the joints, bleeds under the skin and breaks down wounds. When George Anson took eight ships round the world from 1740 he began with 1,854 men and came home with 188, and on one ship alone at least 380 of 510 died of it.

Lemons already had champions, since John Woodall had recommended them to ship surgeons in 1617. Lind added an arrangement: patients as alike as he could find, one room, one diet, and a single difference between each pair. Textbooks call this the first clinical trial, a generous title, and the design deserves its fame anyway, because every drug in your cabinet reached you through a descendant of it. His own account comes from the book he published six years later.

On the 20th of May 1747, I took twelve patients in the scurvy, on board the Salisbury at sea. Their cases were as similar as I could have them. They all in general had putrid gums, the spots and lassitude, with weakness of their knees. They lay together in one place, being a proper apartment for the sick in the ; and had one diet common to all…

Two of these were ordered each a quart of cyder a-day. Two others took twenty-five of three times a-day, upon an empty stomach… Two others took two spoonfuls of vinegar three times a-day… Two of the worst patients, with the tendons in the ham rigid, (a symptom none of the rest had), were put under a course of sea-water. Of this they drank half a pint every day, and sometimes more or less as it operated, by way of gentle . Two others had each two oranges and one lemon given them every day. These they eat with greediness, at different times, upon an empty stomach. They continued but six days under this course, having consumed the quantity that could be spared. The two remaining patients, took the bigness of a nutmeg three times a-day, of an recommended by a hospital-surgeon…

The consequence was, that the most sudden and visible good effects were perceived from the use of the oranges and lemons; one of those who had taken them, being at the end of the six days fit for duty.

A Treatise of the Scurvy, abridged. Transcriptions differ in small spellings; this one follows the American Revolution Institute’s text, corrected against the version quoted by Krehl where the first has a slip.

Read it as a referee would. The men were alike, they shared a room and a menu, and Lind wrote down what he gave and what followed. His flaw sits in one line: “two of the worst patients” went on sea water, because Lind himself decided who took what. A doctor who assigns treatments will steer them toward the patients he expects them to suit, however honest he is. The remedy took two centuries to arrive. The Medical Research Council’s streptomycin trial of 1948 let random numbers do the assigning and kept the sequence hidden from the doctors enrolling patients.

The result then went nowhere, and part of the blame is his. A Treatise of the Scurvy appeared at Edinburgh in 1753, dedicated to Anson, and the trial sits inside it as a few pages of a long argument about causes. Lind blamed moisture and a coarse diet that the body could not digest, which left fruit as one good remedy among many. To carry it to sea he proposed a rob, juice reduced for hours in nearly boiling water, and that heat destroyed much of what the juice held. The Navy’s Sick and Hurt Board later judged the rob ineffective and too expensive.

The Navy moved in 1795, forty-eight years after the oranges. Gilbert Blane had been urging citrus since about 1780, and in 1794 the Suffolk sailed twenty-three weeks non-stop to India with lemon juice in the grog and no serious outbreak. The next year the Admiralty ordered a daily ration for every sailor, about three-quarters of an ounce. Nobody on board could have said why it worked. Two Norwegian physicians produced scurvy in guinea pigs by accident in 1907, and in 1932 Charles Glen King’s laboratory proved that Albert Szent-Györgyi’s hexuronic acid was vitamin C, 185 years after the fore-hold. The amount involved is tiny: about 10 milligrams a day prevents the disease, and a medium orange holds 70. When the Navy later swapped lemons for West Indian limes, with roughly a quarter of the vitamin, scurvy returned on voyages to Antarctica into the twentieth century. So a fair comparison can tell you that something works long before anyone can tell you why, and a wrong theory can still undo the finding.

Vitamin C gives you a fair exercise of your own, since its reputation against the common cold has been argued over for decades. Find the review below, read only the abstract, and put these four questions to it before you read on.

Free to read. Five minutes, the abstract only, a written answer to each question.

You should have found this. The review pools 29 trial comparisons and 11,306 people, against placebo, mostly randomised and double-blind. Taken daily by ordinary people, vitamin C barely changed the risk of catching a cold, a risk ratio of 0.97. It shortened adults’ colds by 8 percent, about half a day on a week-long cold, and starting it once symptoms appeared showed no consistent effect. Next time a health headline crosses your phone, give it the same four questions.

When it comes up, here is your line: the most famous trial in medicine took twelve sailors and six days, and the Navy still needed forty-eight years to act on it.
