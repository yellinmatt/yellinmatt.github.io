Outdoor Pursuits · The Dog, the Gun, and the Bird

Upland Bird Hunting

The sport starts with a dog that stops its own hunt halfway and holds what it has found, and the gun, the manners, and the birds all follow from that refusal to close.

A pointer working a hillside in October covers ground at a gallop, swinging left and right across the wind with its head up, and then it does the thing no other working animal does. It stops. One foreleg stays lifted where the stride left it, the tail goes out straight, the body sets, and the dog holds that shape until the man behind walks up, which might take forty seconds or four minutes. Somewhere ahead of its nose a bird is crouched in the grass. The dog knows exactly where. It has decided not to go get it.

Everything else in upland bird hunting comes off that gesture. A dog’s predatory sequence runs search, stalk, chase, bite, and the point is the stalk held open indefinitely, the animal stopped one beat before the part it wants most. Breeders in Spain and then across western Europe found dogs that hesitated at that seam and bred hard for the hesitation from the middle of the seventeenth century, when birds were taken with nets and a frozen dog bought the seconds a man needed to throw one. Genetics has lately found the fingerprint of that work, a stretch of chromosome 22 present in pointing breeds and absent from breeds that do not point.

The net gave way to the gun, and the gun rewrote the manners. Through the matchlock centuries a bird was something you shot sitting, because ignition was too slow to swing on anything moving. The flintlock made hitting a bird in the air possible, a French fashion Charles II brought home from exile, and Richard Blome’s The Gentleman’s Recreation of 1686 was the first English book to show it done. Within two generations shooting flying had gone from a stunt to the only respectable method, and taking a bird on the ground had become the thing nobody admits to. The technology moved first, the manners caught up, and everyone forgot they had ever been optional.

The gun is plainer than its vocabulary suggests. A shotgun’s bore is measured in gauge, which counts how many round lead balls of that bore’s diameter together weigh a pound. Twelve to the pound make a 12 gauge, twenty make a 20, so the scale runs backwards and the smaller number means the larger hole. The 12 throws more shot and is standard for waterfowl and driven birds. The 20 throws a little less and weighs about a pound less, which is the difference between a gun you carry and a gun you feel, and it is the usual choice for a day of walking up quail or grouse.

The second number is choke, a slight constriction machined into the last inch or two of barrel, which squeezes the shot column on its way out and governs how fast the pellets spread. It is graded honestly, by firing at a large sheet of paper from forty yards and counting how many pellets landed inside a thirty-inch circle. Choosing one is a bet about distance. A ruffed grouse breaking at fifteen yards in thick woods wants the widest pattern you own, since a tight one arrives at that range as a pencil, and a November pheasant getting up at thirty-five yards over open stubble wants the opposite.

Three birds carry most of the conversation in North America. Serious people rank the ruffed grouse first, a solitary woodland bird of aspen and alder that flushes without warning in a noise like a small engine catching and puts a tree between itself and the gun in about a second and a half. Its spring display is the strangest sound in the northern woods. Standing on a log, the male rotates his wings forward and back fast enough to open a partial vacuum, and the air rushing in makes a low thump that accelerates over eight or ten seconds into a roll audible a quarter of a mile away. His wings hit nothing whatever, which Arthur Allen of Cornell proved in 1929 by filming a drumming bird and reading it frame by frame.

The bobwhite quail is the southern bird and the sociable one. A covey feeds together through the day and roosts at night in a tight ring on the ground, tails to the centre and every head facing out, which holds the birds’ heat and gives the group a full circle of watch. Walk into a covey that has chosen to sit tight and nothing happens until everything does, all of them coming up together in a burst of wingbeats loud enough to split a hawk’s attention four ways. The covey rise is what every quail hunter talks about first.

The third bird arrived by boat. In 1881 Owen Denny, the American consul general at Shanghai, shipped about sixty Chinese ring-necked pheasants home to Oregon aboard the Otago; most died on the crossing, and the birds that took hold in the Willamette Valley came from further shipments in 1882 and 1884. They settled into Midwestern farm country as though it had been laid out for them, and when Oregon opened its first season the reported bag ran to fifty thousand birds in seventy-five days. The state bird of South Dakota is an Asian import that arrived on a diplomat’s private enthusiasm.

Watch the dogs rather than the shooting. The pattern repeats: a dog quartering into the wind, the sudden lock-up, the handler walking in past the dog’s nose rather than around it, and only then the bird. Note how little of the day is spent shooting, and how much of it is spent reading ground.

Each of the three is made by the ground it lives on, which is where a day of walking becomes a question about land. Aldo Leopold, a forester and game manager before he was a writer and a grouse hunter throughout, put the problem in terms no wildlife agency has improved on.

“The physics of beauty is one department of natural science still in the Dark Ages. Not even the manipulators of bent space have tried to solve its equations. Everybody knows, for example, that the autumn landscape in the north woods is the land, plus a red maple, plus a ruffed grouse. In terms of conventional physics, the grouse represents only a millionth of either the mass or the energy of an acre. Yet subtract the grouse and the whole thing is dead. An enormous amount of some kind of motive power has been lost.

It is easy to say that the loss is all in our mind’s eye, but is there any sober ecologist who will agree? He knows full well that there has been an ecological death, the significance of which is inexpressible in terms of contemporary science. A philosopher has called this imponderable essence the of material things. It stands in to phenomenon, which is ponderable and predictable, even to the tossings and turnings of the remotest star.

The grouse is the numenon of the north woods, the blue jay of the hickory groves, the of the , the of the juniper foothills. Ornithological texts do not record these facts. I suppose they are new to science, however obvious to the discerning scientist.”

Aldo Leopold, “Guacamaja,” in A Sand County Almanac, Oxford University Press, 1949, p. 146.

Leopold was writing about a loss he could see coming, and it came. Counts of the northern bobwhite on the North American Breeding Bird Survey fell by roughly eighty-one percent between 1966 and 2019, almost none of it attributable to hunting. Fire suppression closed in the open pine woods the bird needs, farming was tidied out to the fencerow so that nothing weedy survived at the edges, and pasture went over to tall exotic grass a two-week-old chick cannot physically walk through. The cover went, and the bird went with it.

Against that runs a law the hunters asked for. The Federal Aid in Wildlife Restoration Act of 1937, called Pittman-Robertson after its Senate and House sponsors, laid an eleven percent federal excise tax on sporting arms and ammunition and sent the receipts back to the states for habitat work and hunter education. The gun industry and its customers lobbied for a tax on their own purchases, and the money has run into land every year since, in all fifty states and the territories.

· · ·

The vocabulary is worth carrying even if you never own a gun. Knowing that the 20 gauge is the smaller bore, that an open choke is the right answer at close range, that a pointer’s stillness is a bred interruption of its own appetite, and that the bobwhite went quiet because somebody mowed the fencerows, is knowing the shape of one long American argument about land, an argument conducted largely by people who spend October walking behind a dog.
