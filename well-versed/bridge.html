Games & Pastimes · Contract Bridge

Bridge

Before a card is played, the partners must describe their hands to each other out loud, in a vocabulary of thirty-eight legal calls, with both opponents listening.

In December 1931 the running score of a card game ran on the front pages of newspapers in thirty countries, and NBC gave it fifteen minutes of radio every night. Ely Culbertson had taken an apartment at the Chatham Hotel in New York, and later moved to the newly opened Waldorf-Astoria, to play a hundred and fifty rubbers against Sidney Lenz. A West Point instructor named Alfred Gruenther refereed, twenty-two years before he took command of NATO. Culbertson had staked five thousand dollars against Lenz’s thousand, both sums pledged to charity. The subject of the quarrel was whose bidding system was correct.

The card-playing half of bridge is old and easily learned. Four players sit in two partnerships, partners facing each other across the table, and thirteen tricks are played out from a standard deck. You follow the suit that was led if you hold it; the highest card of that suit wins the trick unless somebody out of the suit plays a trump, and then the highest trump wins. Whist had run on those rules since the seventeenth century, and a strong whist player was a good memory and good manners.

Contract bridge kept all of that and put an auction in front of it. Before the first card is played, the four players bid in turn for the right to name the trump suit and to promise how many tricks their side will take. A bid names a level and a denomination, and the six tricks that make up half the deal are assumed rather than counted, so 1♣ promises seven tricks with clubs as trumps and 3NT promises nine tricks with no trump suit at all. Each new bid must outrank the last, clubs lowest through no-trumps highest. Three passes in a row end the auction, and the final bid becomes the contract, which the side that won it now has to make or pay a penalty for missing.

Then comes the rule that has no counterpart anywhere else in cards. Whoever first named the winning denomination becomes the declarer, the player on the declarer’s left leads, and the declarer’s partner lays his entire hand face up on the table and does nothing for the rest of the deal. He is the dummy, and the declarer plays both hands against two opponents. Half of one partnership stops being a player at the moment the play starts.

Everything the two of them will ever tell each other, then, has to be said during the auction, in bids, aloud, while the opponents write it all down. Signals, hints, tone and grimaces are barred, and the entire permitted repertoire is thirty-five bids plus pass, double and redouble. So each call does two jobs at once. It proposes a contract, and it describes the hand that made it, according to a code the partnership settled on before sitting down. That code is what bridge means by a system, and it is what Culbertson and Lenz had rented the Waldorf to fight about.

None of that works without a unit of measure. Milton Work, the reigning card authority in Philadelphia, had by 1928 popularised a way of pricing a hand that is still the first thing anybody learns: an ace is four, a king three, a queen two, a jack one. That puts ten points in every suit and forty in the deck, so an average hand holds ten. Roughly twelve is enough to open the bidding at the one level, and roughly twenty-six between the two partnership hands is enough to bid a game.

Twenty-six is a fact about the scoreboard rather than about the cards. A game is a hundred contract points, and tricks are priced by denomination: forty for the first no-trump trick and thirty for each after it, thirty apiece in the major suits of spades and hearts, twenty apiece in the minor suits of diamonds and clubs. Three no-trumps therefore scores 40 and 30 and 30, which is exactly a hundred; four hearts or four spades scores 120; five clubs or five diamonds scores 100. Nothing below those reaches the line in a single deal, which is why the auction is really a negotiation about whether the partnership can afford one of them.

Work the diagram and the arithmetic does something useful. South holds the ace of spades, king-queen-ten of hearts with two more, and the ace and jack of clubs, which comes to fourteen points and a five-card heart suit, so South opens 1♥. North counts the diamond ace, two jacks and a queen for eight, and holds four hearts. Standard American gives North exactly one bid for that hand, a raise to 2♥, which means “I have your suit, and I have six to ten points.” South adds fourteen and eight, arrives at twenty-two, and passes. The partnership has found a nine-card trump fit and established that game is out of reach, and it has done so in three calls. The point count earns its keep by telling a partnership when to stop.

Culbertson priced hands differently. His Contract Bridge Blue Book of 1930 counted honour tricks, giving an ace one and a combined ace-king two, with a thicket of halves and quarters underneath, and it was fiddly enough that his rivals said so loudly. His durable contribution was structural. The Blue Book set down the approach-forcing principle, under which a new suit named by the responding partner obliges the opener to speak again, and a system that cannot force a second bid cannot describe anything complicated before the auction dies. Lenz belonged to the group calling itself Bridge Headquarters, which had answered with a rival Official System; Milton Work was among them. Culbertson, coming off the Mauretania, described their work to reporters as “eighty percent Culbertson, twelve percent Work and Lenz, and eight percent rubbish.”

Then he went and won. Lenz and his partner Oswald Jacoby led for forty-three rubbers before falling behind, and the partnership curdled: Jacoby, criticised once too often for defence he had thought through, replied that he had made a play “only twelve experts in the country would understand, and unfortunately Mr Lenz did not appear, at that particular moment, to be one of them.” He resigned after the 103rd rubber. The final margin was 8,980 points to Culbertson.

Honour tricks did not survive him, and the reason was a ghostwriter. A young hostess at McGill had laughed at how badly Charles Goren played, which sent him into the literature; thirteen years of Philadelphia law practice later, he was drafting Milton Work’s newspaper columns for him. Work died in 1934 and Goren began publishing on his own, refining the 4-3-2-1 count into the point-count method that carries his name. His first book, in 1936, went out under the subtitle “a simplified self-teaching method of contract bidding combining all the principles of the new Culbertson system,” which is the settlement of the whole feud printed on a title page. By 1958 Goren’s daily column was running in 194 American newspapers, and the count had become what everyone meant by bidding.

What survives at a modern table, then, is Culbertson’s architecture running on his opponents’ arithmetic, and it is the reason two strangers can sit down as partners and understand each other by the second call. They are speaking a published language in front of witnesses, and the whole art of it lies in hearing precisely how much a bid does and does not say.
