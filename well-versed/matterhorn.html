The last unclimbed Alp, and the hour that put the whole sport on trial

The Matterhorn

Seven men reached the summit on 14 July 1865 after a climb that turned out to be easy, and four of them died coming down, tied to a length of rope that had only been brought along as a spare.

Late on the afternoon of 14 July 1865 a boy ran into the Monte Rosa hotel in Zermatt to say he had watched an avalanche fall from the summit of the Matterhorn onto the glacier at its foot. Nobody believed him. What he had seen come off the north face was four men on a rope, dropping four thousand feet.

Nothing else around Zermatt looks like that mountain. Mont Blanc stands more than three hundred metres taller and Monte Rosa is next door, yet at 4,478 metres the Matterhorn is the one that holds the eye, because it rises alone, a four-sided pyramid with a face turned to each point of the compass and no neighbour close enough to lean on. The decade running from Alfred Wills on the Wetterhorn in 1854 to that July afternoon is called the golden age of alpinism. The Alpine Club was founded in London in 1857, the great summits fell one after another to English gentlemen and the Swiss and French guides they hired, and by 1865 the Matterhorn was the last of the prizes still standing. It was widely held to be unclimbable, and from Zermatt it plainly looked it.

Edward Whymper first came to the Alps in 1860, twenty years old, a London wood engraver sent out by a publisher to make drawings of mountain scenery. He went home a climber. He then spent five summers being beaten off the Matterhorn, almost always from the Italian side, and almost always in the company of Jean-Antoine Carrel of Valtournenche, a guide and army veteran who thought the mountain was Italian and should fall to an Italian.

In July 1865 Carrel proved the point by slipping away with a national expedition, quietly bankrolled to put an Italian on the summit first. Whymper, stranded at Breuil with no guides left in the valley, crossed the Théodul pass to Zermatt and built a party in one evening out of whoever happened to be in the hotel. Lord Francis Douglas, eighteen, lent him a porter and joined. The Reverend Charles Hudson, the strongest amateur climber in England, had arrived with the same intention and the same day in mind, bringing Michel Croz of Chamonix, the finest guide of the era, and a young friend named Douglas Hadow whose whole mountain career amounted to one ascent of Mont Blanc. Peter Taugwalder of Zermatt came on with his son. Seven men, most of whom had never climbed together, walked out of the hotel at half past five the next morning.

The east face they had all been told was impossible turned out to be, in Whymper’s phrase, a huge natural staircase rising for three thousand feet. They pitched a tent at eleven thousand feet on the 13th, climbed most of the following morning without bothering to rope up, and met real difficulty only in the last stretch, where verglas on the rock pushed them off the ridge onto the north face and Croz had to place Hadow’s feet for him one at a time. Then the angle eased. Croz and Whymper ran the last snow slope neck and neck, and at twenty minutes to two, as Whymper wrote, the world was at their feet and the Matterhorn was conquered. Looking over the Italian edge he spotted Carrel two hundred metres below, still working at the hard part. He shouted, got no response, and began rolling rocks off the cliff until the Italians looked up. They turned around and went down.

Whymper stayed behind on the summit for a few minutes to write the party’s names on a page for the bottle they had forgotten to leave, and caught the others just as the difficult section began. They were descending it properly, one man at a time, in the order Croz, Hadow, Hudson, Douglas, old Peter, Whymper, young Peter. About an hour below the top, Hadow slipped.

Michel Croz had laid aside his axe, and in order to give Mr. Hadow greater security was absolutely taking hold of his legs and putting his feet, one by one, into their proper positions. As far as I know, no one was actually descending… it is my belief, from the movements of their shoulders, that Croz, having done as I have said, was in the act of turning round to go down a step or two himself: at this moment Mr. Hadow slipped, fell against him and knocked him over. I heard one startled exclamation from Croz, then saw him and Mr. Hadow flying downward: in another moment Hudson was dragged from his steps, and Lord F. Douglas immediately after him.

All this was the work of a moment. Immediately we heard Croz’s exclamation, old Peter and I planted ourselves as firmly as the rocks would permit: the rope was taut between us, and the jerk came on us both as on one man. We held, but the rope broke midway between Taugwalder and Lord Francis Douglas. For a few seconds we saw our unfortunate companions sliding downward on their backs, and spreading out their hands, endeavoring to save themselves. They passed from our sight uninjured, disappeared one by one, and fell from precipice to precipice on to the Matterhorngletscher below, a distance of nearly four thousand feet in height. From the moment the rope broke it was impossible to help them.

Edward Whymper, Scrambles Amongst the Alps in the Years 1860–69 (John Murray, 1871), ch. XXI.

The survivors took two hours to get themselves down to easy ground, and somewhere in that stretch Whymper asked for the broken end and found the explanation of the disaster in his hand. Three ropes had gone up the mountain that morning. Two were sound Manila lines. The third was old and weak, carried only in reserve, in case they needed to cut lengths off it and leave them fixed to the rock behind them. The four men who fell had been tied to each other with good rope. The weak line was the piece running from Douglas back to Taugwalder, precisely where the shock arrived. It had parted in mid-air, with no sign of having been cut or chafed.

Croz, Hadow and Hudson were found on the glacier and buried beside the church in Zermatt. Of Lord Francis Douglas only a sleeve was ever recovered. Peter Taugwalder was charged before a court of inquiry in the Valais and acquitted, and spent the rest of his life being told to his face that he had cut the rope to save himself. Carrel reached the summit from the Italian side with Jean-Baptiste Bich on 17 July, three days too late. In Britain the deaths turned a private enthusiasm into a public quarrel about whether men had any business on mountains at all, and Queen Victoria is said to have asked whether climbing might be banned outright. The broken length now sits in a glass case in the Matterhorn Museum in Zermatt, which is the single most eloquent object in the sport.

The Hörnli ridge is the ordinary route today, graded AD, moderately difficult, well below what a competent alpinist trains for. Something like three thousand people a season start up it from the Hörnlihütte at 3,260 metres, and more than five hundred have died on the mountain since 1865, a toll no other Alpine peak comes near. Whymper closed his book six years after the accident with the passage every guide still quotes: “Climb if you will, but remember that courage and strength are naught without prudence, and that a momentary negligence may destroy the happiness of a lifetime. Do nothing in haste, look well to each step, and from the beginning think what may be the end.” The difficulty was never the point. Hadow slipped at an easy place, on the way down, an hour from the summit, roped to men he had met the previous evening.

A full ascent of the Hörnli ridge filmed by a certified guide. Watch how little of it is climbing in the vertical sense and how much is fast, exposed movement over loose broken rock, which is exactly why the route takes most of its lives on the way down. The difficulty stays low the whole climb. What never drops is the price of one careless step.
