The most reproduced photograph in America, and the thumb that was taken out of it

Migrant Mother

Five exposures in ten minutes at a California pea camp, and everything the most reproduced photograph of the Depression had to leave outside its edges.

The sign said PEA-PICKERS CAMP, and Dorothea Lange drove past it. She was heading north for Berkeley at the end of a long field trip, weeks of exposed film behind her, and argued with herself for twenty miles before turning the car around. What she found at Nipomo was a frozen pea crop, a camp of families with no work in it, and a woman of thirty-two sitting under a lean-to with her children. Lange stayed about ten minutes and made five exposures. She did not ask the woman her name.

The picture everyone knows is the closest of the five. A face fills the centre, a hand up at the jaw, the eyes fixed past the lens and well to the side of it. Two children press in from either side with their heads turned away into her shoulders, so there is nobody to look at but her, and the baby across her knees reads as a bundle before it reads as a child. Museums have been pointing at the Madonna and Child for decades, and they are right to: the triangle, the bowed heads, the infant in the lap. Lange had trained as a studio portraitist in New York before she ever photographed a breadline, and she knew what the arrangement was quoting.

The wider exposures show how much of the world she was removing. A sagging tent and its guy pole, a kerosene lantern and an empty pie plate on a trunk, an older daughter standing at the pole holding on, the California plain running off pale and flat behind: none of it survives into the famous one. Moving in from the same side, exposure after exposure, Lange stripped out the evidence that this was a particular family in a particular field, and left a woman who could be almost anywhere. She made the picture by taking things out of it.

ReadLange’s own account, twenty-four years later

I saw and approached the hungry and desperate mother, as if drawn by a magnet. I do not remember how I explained my presence or my camera to her, but I do remember she asked me no questions. I made five exposures, working closer and closer from the same direction. I did not ask her name or her history. She told me her age, that she was thirty-two. She said that they had been living on frozen vegetables from the surrounding fields, and birds that the children killed. She had just sold the tires from her car to buy food. There she sat in that lean-to tent with her children huddled around her, and seemed to know that my pictures might help her, and so she helped me. There was a sort of equality about it.

Dorothea Lange, Popular Photography, February 1960, as reproduced by the Library of Congress

She was on the federal payroll while she did it. The Resettlement Administration ran from 1935 to 1937, became the Farm Security Administration, and ended the war folded into the Office of War Information; through all three it kept a Historical Section under Roy Stryker, an economics instructor from Columbia who had never worked as a photographer. Stryker reviewed the contact prints and decided which negatives were printed at all. The archive he built reached the Library of Congress in 1944 with some 175,000 black-and-white negatives in it, and he built it by rejection. Of roughly 270,000 exposures his photographers made, close to 100,000 were killed, most punched through with a hole so no print could be pulled from them again. Stryker fired Lange three times.

Which brings the thumb. In the negative as Lange exposed it, a thumb hooks round the tent pole at the lower right, close to the lens and soft, and it tugs the eye out of the picture. Somebody took it out. The retouching is attributed to Lange, who seems to have read it as a flaw and simply removed it, and the Library of Congress holds the result both ways: LC-USF34-9058-C is the photograph the world knows, and LC-DIG-ppmsca-12883 is the same photograph with the thumb still in it. Both sit in the file, a few catalogue numbers apart.

The photographs did the job they were made for. On 10 March 1936 the San Francisco News ran two of them under the headline “Ragged, Hungry, Broke, Harvest Workers Live in Squaller,” the wire services carried the story, and the federal government shipped twenty thousand pounds of food to the camp at Nipomo. The family in the picture had already moved on looking for work, and got none of it.

Her name took forty-two years to reach print. In 1978 Emmett Corrigan of the Modesto Bee tracked her down: Florence Owens Thompson, born Florence Leona Christie on 1 September 1903 in Indian Territory, of Cherokee descent, widowed at twenty-eight with six children to feed. Lange’s caption had called them destitute pea pickers. They were driving to a lettuce job when the car’s timing chain broke near the camp, and Thompson was sitting with the children while her partner and her sons saw about the repair. “I wish she hadn’t taken my picture,” she told the paper. “I can’t get a penny out of it.” When she was dying in 1983, strangers who recognised the face sent thirty-five thousand dollars toward her care. The stone over her grave reads MIGRANT MOTHER — A LEGEND OF THE STRENGTH OF AMERICAN MOTHERHOOD.

None of this is a scandal, and calling it one misses what the photograph is for. Every picture is a set of choices about where to stand, when to press the shutter, and what to crop away, and the federal file is unusual mainly in having kept the receipts: the wider exposures, the unretouched negative, the hole-punched rejects, the caption that got the facts wrong. Migrant Mother is reliable about 1936 and unreliable about Florence Thompson, and the useful habit is holding both at once.
