Leviathan

The line everyone knows from it describes life with no government at all, and Hobbes’s evidence for it was that his reader locks the door at night.

The crowned figure on the title page rises behind the hills with a sword in his right hand and a bishop’s crosier in his left, and from a little distance he seems to be wearing chain mail. Closer in, the links turn out to be people. More than three hundred of them stand packed into his arms and chest, each with his back to the reader and his face lifted toward the head above. Abraham Bosse etched the plate in Paris after long discussion with the author, and along its top edge runs a line from the Book of Job about the sea monster: Non est potestas super terram quae comparetur ei, there is no power on earth to be compared to him. The book came out in London in 1651 under that monster’s name, and the picture holds its whole argument.

Its author was sixty-three and had been frightened for most of that time, by his own account. Thomas Hobbes was born early, on 5 April 1588 at Westport beside Malmesbury, when word reached his mother that the Spanish Armada was on its way; he later wrote in a Latin verse autobiography that she had given birth to twins, himself and fear. He took his Oxford degree in 1608 and went straight into the Cavendish household as a tutor, an attachment that lasted seventy years. Late in 1640, with Parliament turning on the king’s defenders, he left for Paris. He stayed eleven years, taught mathematics to the exiled Prince of Wales, and wrote while England fought its civil war and cut off its king’s head.

The argument begins from equality, which is the surprise. Chapter XIII opens by observing that nature has made people so nearly alike that “the weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest,” by plotting or by joining with others, and so nobody is ever strong enough to relax. From that Hobbes draws three causes of quarrel. Competition makes people attack for gain, glory for reputation, and the one in the middle, which he calls diffidence and means as distrust, makes them attack for safety. Distrust does the real damage. If I cannot be sure of you, the sensible course is to strike first, and you can work that out as easily as I can. He calls the result war and defines it the way one defines a climate: foul weather is a tendency over many days, and a few dry hours between showers do not end it.

Whatsoever therefore is consequent to a time of Warre, where every man is Enemy to every man; the same is consequent to the time, wherein men live without other security, than what their own strength, and their own invention shall furnish them withall. In such condition, there is no place for ; because the fruit thereof is uncertain; and consequently no ; no Navigation, nor use of the commodities that may be imported by Sea; no Building; no Instruments of moving, and removing such things as require much force; no Knowledge of the face of the Earth; no account of Time; no Arts; no ; no Society; and which is worst of all, continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short.

It may seem strange to some man, that has not well weighed these things; that Nature should thus dissociate, and render men apt to invade, and destroy one another: and he may therefore, not trusting to this Inference, made from the Passions, desire perhaps to have the same confirmed by Experience. Let him therefore consider with himselfe, when taking a journey, he armes himselfe, and seeks to go well accompanied; when going to sleep, he locks his dores; when even in his house he locks his chests; and this when he knows there bee Lawes, and publike Officers, armed, to shall bee done him; what opinion he has of his fellow subjects, when he rides armed; of his fellow Citizens, when he locks his dores; and of his children, and servants, when he locks his chests. Does he not there as much accuse mankind by his actions, as I do by my words? But neither of us accuse mans nature in it. The Desires, and other Passions of man, are in themselves no Sin.

Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, Part I, chapter XIII, “Of the Naturall Condition of Mankind,” in the spelling of the 1651 edition. Two consecutive paragraphs; the second stops four lines short of its end.

The five adjectives are usually quoted as a verdict on human beings, and the next paragraph refuses that reading. Nobody in Hobbes’s natural condition is wicked. They are ordinary, reasonable and afraid, and his proof is the reader’s own bedtime routine, performed inside a country that has laws and constables. He also conceded that there may never have been such a time over the whole world. He pointed instead to what peaceful people turn into during a civil war, and to kings, who face one another permanently “in the state and posture of Gladiators.”

The way out is a promise, made by every person to every other: “I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing my selfe, to this Man, or to this Assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy Right to him.” The subjects contract with one another. The sovereign signs nothing, so there is no clause he can be said to have broken, and the subject keeps a single liberty, which is to resist when his own life is being taken. Hobbes saw no gentler version that would hold, because “Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words.” The multitude joined in one person he named “that great LEVIATHAN, or rather (to speake more reverently) … that Mortall God.” Bosse drew exactly that: a ruler whose body is his people and who holds the church’s staff as well as the state’s blade.

The book satisfied nobody with power. Royalists found in it that any government able to protect its subjects was owed obedience, Cromwell’s included, and the clergy found their church folded into the state. Hobbes went home to London in the winter of 1651. On 17 October 1666, six weeks after the Great Fire, the House of Commons empowered a committee to receive information on books tending “to atheism, blasphemy and profaneness,” and named “the book of Mr. Hobbes called the Leviathan.” No prosecution followed, though he never again published in England on human conduct. Charles II, the old mathematics pupil, paid him a pension of a hundred pounds, and he died at Hardwick Hall on 4 December 1679, aged ninety-one, still under a Cavendish roof.

John Locke supplied the standard objection ten years later. To hand one man all the liberty of the natural condition and add armies to it, he wrote, is to suppose people so foolish “that they take care to avoid what mischiefs may be done them by pole-cats, or foxes; but are content, nay, think it safety, to be devoured by lions.” That objection has largely won. What the winners kept is Hobbes’s starting point: political authority has to be justified to the people under it, by an agreement equals would make, and nothing in birth or scripture can stand in for that.
