Johnson’s Dictionary

Samuel Johnson promised the English dictionary in three years, delivered it in nine with six copyists, and told the lord who claimed a share in it exactly what his help had been worth.

Early in the job, an Oxford friend named William Adams found Samuel Johnson at work and asked the obvious question. How could one man finish a dictionary of the English language in three years, when the French Academy, forty members strong, had needed forty years for theirs? Johnson did the sum aloud. “Let me see; forty times forty is sixteen hundred. As three to sixteen hundred, so is the proportion of an Englishman to a Frenchman.” James Boswell, who kept the exchange for his Life of Johnson, admired the ease of it. The forecast missed by six years, which on his own arithmetic still left him far ahead of the French.

Johnson was thirty-six in the summer of 1746, a bookseller’s son from Lichfield who had left Oxford without a degree and was scraping a living from journalism in London. On the morning of 18 June he signed with a group of London booksellers, Robert Dodsley, Andrew Millar and the Longmans among them, for 1,500 guineas, or £1,575. English had word-books already: lists of hard terms, and Nathan Bailey’s long, thinly defined compilation of 1721. It had nothing with authority. Johnson’s Plan of 1747 likened the rules of style to the rules of law, both arising from precedents often repeated, and he addressed it to Philip Stanhope, fourth Earl of Chesterfield, a Secretary of State and the natural patron for a work of national pride.

The method he worked out in a tall brick house at 17 Gough Square, off Fleet Street, was new to English. Johnson read through the writers he trusted, Shakespeare, Milton and Dryden above all, with the divines, the lawyers and the natural philosophers behind them, and marked sentences in books, many of them borrowed, that went home to their owners the worse for wear. Six amanuenses, five of them Scots, as Boswell noted with some relish, copied each marked sentence onto a slip of paper, and the slips were filed under the words they showed in use. The definitions came last, written from the evidence.

That order of work shows on every page. An entry opens on the headword in small capitals and its part of speech, gives an etymology in brackets, offers a definition, and then lets the quotations prove the sense, each one signed. There are about 114,000 of them spread across 42,773 entries, which makes the book a reader as much as a reference: open it anywhere and Shakespeare or Milton is using the word in front of you.

Three years became nine. The booksellers had paid in advance and grew restless. His wife Elizabeth, whom he called Tetty, died in March 1752. Chesterfield had given him a polite reception and a token sum in the early days and nothing after, and Johnson stopped calling. Late in 1754, with publication near and the book already talked about, the earl published two essays in a fashionable paper called The World recommending it to the public, and to anyone reading them it looked as though he had nursed the project all along. Johnson’s answer went to him in February 1755.

Seven years, My lord have now past since I waited in your or was from your Door, during which time I have been pushing on my work through difficulties of which it is useless to complain, and have brought it at last to the verge of Publication without one Act of assistance, one word of encouragement, or one smile of favour. Such treatment I did not expect, for I never had a Patron before.

The grew at last acquainted with Love, and found him a Native of the Rocks. Is not a Patron, My Lord, one who looks with unconcern on a Man struggling for Life in the water and when he has reached ground him with help. The notice which you have been pleased to take of my Labours, had it been early, kind; but it has been delayed till I am indifferent and cannot enjoy it, till I am solitary and cannot impart it, till I am known, and do not want it.

Samuel Johnson to Philip Stanhope, fourth Earl of Chesterfield, 7 February 1755, paragraphs three and four. Text from Jack Lynch’s edition; the original is lost and survives in eighteenth-century copies.

The letter turns on the drowning man, and the sentence after him is built from three matched clauses that come to rest on want, which in Johnson’s English still meant need. Chesterfield is said to have left it out on his table for visitors and praised its style, a gentleman’s way of conceding. Johnson kept his own score inside the book. The dictionary defines a patron as “Commonly a wretch who supports with insolence, and is paid with flattery,” and its Preface records that the work was done “with little assistance of the learned, and without any patronage of the great,” “amidst inconvenience and distraction, in sickness and in sorrow.” The letter has been called the declaration of independence of the modern author, and the label fits: the money for those nine years came from booksellers, who expected to recover it from readers.

The two folio volumes came out in April 1755, their pages nearly eighteen inches tall, priced at £4 10s. Oxford had sent Johnson a master’s degree weeks earlier so the title page could print the letters after his name. When the messenger who carried the last sheet of copy to Andrew Millar came back, Johnson asked what the bookseller had said. “Sir,” answered the messenger, “he said, thank God I have done with him.” “I am glad,” Johnson replied with a smile, “that he thanks God for any thing.”

The definitions everyone quotes are the least typical in the book, which is why they travel. Oats are “A grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people.” A lexicographer is “A writer of dictionaries; a harmless drudge, that busies himself in tracing the original, and detailing the signification of words.” A network is “Any thing reticulated or decussated, at equal distances, with interstices between the intersections,” which even Boswell admitted had been quoted “with sportive malignity.” A pension is “pay given to a state hireling for treason to his country,” a line his enemies threw back at him after he accepted £300 a year from George III in 1762. And when a lady asked why he had defined the pastern as the knee of a horse, he answered, “Ignorance, Madam, pure ignorance.”

The book held the field for more than a century. Noah Webster set his American Dictionary of 1828 against it and still borrowed its quotations, and the Oxford English Dictionary that finally displaced it reprinted a number of Johnson’s definitions over his initial. What the OED scaled up was the slip of paper: a sense established by quotation, with the writer’s name attached. The position worth holding is that the jokes are the least of his achievement. His Preface had already given up the hope of fixing English in place, conceding that “to enchain syllables, and to lash the wind, are equally the undertakings of pride,” and the dictionary he built instead records the language as its best writers had used it, which is the job every serious dictionary has taken on since.
