Blood Pressure

Both halves of the familiar reading come from sounds a Russian surgeon heard through a child’s stethoscope in 1905, and the line dividing a safe number from a dangerous one has moved within living memory.

In the autumn of 1905 a surgeon of thirty-one named Nikolai Korotkoff stood before the Imperial Military Medical Academy in St Petersburg and explained how to hear a man’s blood pressure. The report he published ran to 281 words, less than a page of the academy’s proceedings, and it introduced no new instrument. The arm cuff already existed. What Korotkoff brought to it was a child’s stethoscope and the patience to listen while the pressure came down.

Getting a number out of a living artery had taken two centuries before that. Stephen Hales, an English clergyman with a parish and a laboratory, tied a mare on her back in 1733, opened the crural artery of her left hind leg, fitted a brass pipe to a glass tube nine feet long, and watched the blood climb eight feet three inches up the glass, rising and falling with every beat of her heart. It is an honest measurement of arterial pressure, and the reason nobody tried it on a person. The cuff solved that in 1896, when Scipione Riva-Rocci wrapped an inflatable band around the upper arm, ran it to a mercury manometer, and pumped until the pulse at the wrist vanished. Easing the pressure down until the beat returned under his fingertips told him what force the heart needed to drive blood past the cuff. It told him one thing only, because a pulse coming back is a single event.

Korotkoff’s contribution was to stop feeling and start listening. Below a cuff squeezed shut, an artery is silent. Let the pressure down and the vessel opens for an instant at the crest of each beat; blood forced through that narrowed gap turns turbulent and makes a noise, so that the cuff becomes, in effect, a whistle. The tapping carries on for as long as the artery is being pinched at some point in the cycle. When the cuff finally sinks below the lowest pressure the vessel ever sees, flow runs smooth again and the sound stops. The first tap and the last one bracket the entire range the artery works across, which is why the reading has a top and a bottom.

N. S. Korotkoff, “To the question of methods of determining the blood pressure,” Reports of the Imperial Military Medical Academy 11 (1905), 365–367, in the standard English translation.

The first figure, the systolic, is the pressure at the crest of the heart’s contraction. The second, the diastolic, is the pressure still standing in the arteries between beats while the ventricle refills, held up by the elastic recoil of the great vessels; it is the floor the circulation never drops beneath. Both are quoted in millimetres of mercury because Riva-Rocci measured against a mercury column, and the unit has outlived the metal by a generation.

What has never settled is where along that scale the trouble begins. On 13 November 2017, at the American Heart Association’s Scientific Sessions in Anaheim, a panel of twenty-one experts chaired by Paul Whelton of Tulane published the first full American blood-pressure guideline since 2003 and moved the definition of hypertension from 140/90 down to 130/80. The effect was arithmetic and immediate: the share of American adults counted as hypertensive went from about 32 percent to about 46 percent overnight, with prevalence expected to triple among men under forty-five. Whelton was blunt about what the new stage-one band meant for anyone sitting inside it. “You’ve already doubled your risk of cardiovascular complications compared to those with a normal level of blood pressure,” he said. “It doesn’t mean you need medication, but it’s a yellow light.”

Underneath the move sat SPRINT, a trial of 9,361 adults at high cardiovascular risk and without diabetes, randomised to a systolic target below 120 or below 140 and halted early when the intensive arm pulled clearly ahead. Major cardiovascular events ran at 1.65 percent of patients a year against 2.19 percent on the standard target, a quarter less risk, bought at the price of more fainting and more acute kidney injury. The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline, the first revision since, held the line at 130/80 and added an encouragement to get most patients under 120/80.

All of which rests on a measurement easier to take badly than well. The cuff has to fit: in a randomised crossover trial published in JAMA Internal Medicine in 2023, adults who needed an extra-large cuff and were measured with a regular one read 19.5 mmHg high, enough to manufacture a diagnosis out of nothing. Talking during the reading adds as much as 19 mmHg, crossed legs 15, an unsupported arm 20, a full bladder 33. The guideline therefore asks for five minutes of rest beforehand, feet flat, back supported, arm at heart level, bare skin under the cuff, nobody speaking, and the average of two or three readings taken on at least two separate occasions.

That last instruction is the one worth carrying out of the examining room. A figure taken from a man who has parked badly and jogged up a flight of stairs is not a diagnosis of anything, and the reading a physician can act on is the dull one, taken twice, on two different days, by someone who let the room go quiet first.
