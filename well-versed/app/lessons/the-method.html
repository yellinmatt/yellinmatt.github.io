Theater · Acting

The Method

Stanislavski spent his last decade replacing the technique that had made his name in America, and the American who would not follow him wrote the encyclopedia article.

Rehearsals for Three Sisters had gone dead. The actors, Stanislavski wrote afterwards, “stopped in the middle of the play, ceased to act, seeing no sense in their work,” and then a man sitting on a bench began scratching his fingernails against the wood. The sound reached Stanislavski as a mouse, and behind the mouse came a run of memories he had not known he was carrying, and the scene in front of him opened up again. He was playing Vershinin. What stayed with him afterwards was the helplessness of the thing: the evening had been saved by an accident nobody in the room could have arranged.

Great actors had always had such nights and had never been able to order one up. Stanislavski’s ambition sounds modest and turned out not to be. He wanted to codify what the great ones did by luck so that an ordinary company could do it on a schedule, and he stated the requirement as a job description: the artist of the stage, he said, must be the master of his own inspiration and must know how to call it forth when it is announced on the poster of the theatre. The Moscow Art Theatre gave him four decades of laboratory. What came out of it he called the system, and at its centre sat perezhivanie, experiencing, the condition of an actor so far inside the imagined circumstances that the feeling in the scene is genuinely his.

ReadThree Sisters, Act III, 1901

IIRINA. We’ll go away too.

CHEBUTYKIN [drops the clock, which smashes]. To smithereens!

[Pause; everyone is upset and confused]

KULYGIN [picking up the pieces]. To smash such a valuable thing — oh, Ivan Romanitch, Ivan Romanitch! I’d give you minus zero for conduct!

IRINA. That was mother’s clock.

CHEBUTYKIN. Perhaps. . . . Well, if it was hers, it was. Perhaps I didn’t smash it, but it only seems as though I had. Perhaps it only seems to us that we exist, but really we aren’t here at all. I don’t know anything — nobody knows anything.

Four words do the work of the scene, and they are the four with nothing in them. An actress who arrives at “That was mother’s clock” with the right thing running underneath it has done the whole play; an actress who merely says the line correctly has said nothing at all. This is the problem the system was built to solve, and this is the play whose rehearsals produced the mouse.

Anton Chekhov, Three Sisters, Act III, in Constance Garnett’s translation, Macmillan, 1916. Chebutykin, the regimental doctor, is being addressed by his first name and patronymic, Ivan Romanitch; Kulygin, who reaches for a mark sheet, teaches Latin at the town school.

One route to perezhivanie ran through affective memory, a term Stanislavski took from the French psychologist Théodule Ribot, who had described it in the 1890s. Ribot meant the return of a past feeling with its charge intact, fetched back by something analogous in the present, the way a smell puts a grown man in a room he left at nine years old. Train that faculty deliberately, the argument goes, and an actor can produce real grief at eight o’clock on a Tuesday and again at eight o’clock on Wednesday. It is the most gripping idea in the system and the easiest to carry away on its own. That is roughly what happened.

The system crossed the Atlantic in 1922, when the Moscow Art Theatre played New York and two of its company stayed behind to teach: Richard Boleslavsky and Maria Ouspenskaya. Among the Americans who came to them were Lee Strasberg and Harold Clurman, who in 1931 founded the Group Theatre with Cheryl Crawford and spent a decade turning a Russian training into an American one. Strasberg ran the acting work, and the acting work ran on affective memory. Actors were asked to go back into their own griefs night after night and fetch the feeling out, and some of them were being damaged by it.

Stella Adler had sat in that room and had come to dislike what it did to people. In the summer of 1934 she got five weeks alone with Stanislavski in Paris, working through a script with him line by line, and found that he had moved. He now kept emotion memory in reserve and reached first for what the character physically does, on the reasoning that thought has a physical side and action has a mental one, so an actor who solves the doing will arrive at the reasoning from underneath. His instruction for rehearsal was blunt: “Start bravely, not to reason but to act. As soon as you begin to act you will immediately become aware of the necessity of justifying your actions.” Adler carried this back to the Group, where the actors rejoiced. Strasberg did not.

· · ·

Isaac Butler, whose The Method is the best account of the quarrel, adds a complication worth keeping. Stanislavski was by then working under Stalin, and a technique built on the private contents of one performer’s memory looked uncomfortably like the bourgeois individualism the cultural authorities were then rooting out. The turn towards action may have been partly a turn towards safety. It was also, on the evidence of his last productions, what he actually believed.

Strasberg kept his version. He took over the Actors Studio in 1948 and ran it until his death in 1982, and Marlon Brando, Rod Steiger, Geraldine Page and James Dean all passed through the building. What American audiences saw in the fifties was so unlike anything before it that the technique acquired the definite article. In 1959 he set the case out at length in the Encyclopædia Britannica, in an essay on acting where affective memory is, he wrote, “of prime importance.”

Britannica still runs that essay under his name, and the current article now carries the correction alongside it: the word Method fits Stanislavski’s work up to the early 1920s and largely ignores the method of physical action he arrived at afterwards. Which means that everything the phrase has come to mean in ordinary speech, the staying in character between takes and the interviews afterwards about what it cost, descends from one American branch of a Russian argument the Russian had already settled the other way. Butler puts the evidence on screen where it can be checked, setting Dean, taught by Strasberg, “pouty and withdrawn,” against Brando, taught by Adler, “alive with the character’s thoughts.” The gap between them comes from what each man had been told to think about while the camera ran.
