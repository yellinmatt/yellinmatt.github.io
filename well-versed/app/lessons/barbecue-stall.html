The Barbecue Stall

Six hours of a brisket cook are spent at one temperature, and every folk explanation for why is wrong.

A whole brisket goes on the pit before dawn. The cooker holds 225°F, the probe sits in the thickest part of the flat, and for two or three hours the reading climbs at an encouraging rate. Somewhere near 150°F it stops climbing, and may give back a degree. It will sit there for the next six hours while the fire is tended and the wood replaced and the cook changes the batteries in a thermometer that is working perfectly.

Pitmasters have had an explanation for this since long before anyone measured it, and it is a good one. Brisket is dense with collagen, the connective protein that makes a working muscle tough, and collagen starts converting to gelatin at around 160°F. That conversion consumes energy, and the numbers line up almost exactly, so the plateau got read as the meat spending its heat on the transformation that makes barbecue worth eating. Rival accounts blamed the fat rendering or the muscle proteins denaturing. All three are happening in there. All three take energy. The question nobody had put to a thermometer was whether any of them takes enough.

The arithmetic went first, and it was done by Greg Blonder, a Boston University physicist who had been chief technical advisor at AT&T's Bell Labs before he took his instruments to barbecue. A pork shoulder is roughly 65 percent water, 15 percent fat and 8 percent protein, and only about a quarter of that protein is collagen, which puts the collagen near 2 percent of the meat by weight. Melting 2 percent of a twelve-pound object will not hold the whole of it at one temperature through an afternoon. Having shown the leading suspect too small for the crime, he built an experiment to find the real one.

He placed two things in a thermostatically controlled smoker at 230°F, each with its own probe: a lump of pure beef fat from the refrigerator, and a large cellulose sponge soaked in water and shaken out. The fat rose on a smooth, unremarkable curve and never hesitated. The sponge kept pace to about 140°F, quit climbing, dropped a few degrees, and stayed down for more than four hours before it resumed. A kitchen sponge has no collagen in it, no fat to render, no muscle fibre to denature. What it has is water.

ReadProf. Greg Blonder, on the sponge and the fat

“Since there was a deep, glistening pool of melted fat in the smoker, the rendering fat hypothesis is busted. The barbecue stall is a simple consequence of evaporative cooling by the meat's own moisture slowly released over hours from within its pores and cells. As the temperature of cold meat rises, the evaporation rate increases until the cooling effect balances the heat input. Then it stalls, until the last drop of available moisture on the surface is gone.”

Greg Blonder, quoted in Meathead Goldwyn, “What Causes The BBQ Stall,” AmazingRibs.com.

Meat sweats, and that cools it, for the reason a person feels cold stepping out of a pool. Evaporating one gram of water takes more than five hundred calories, some five hundred times what it takes to warm that gram by a degree, and a twelve-pound cut has hours of water to give. The stall begins where the heat arriving from the fire equals the heat leaving as vapour, and it ends when the surface can no longer supply water fast enough to hold the balance. What the surface has by then become is bark, the dark spice-crusted exterior that is the whole reason for cooking this way. Those flat six hours are the hours in which the barbecue is made.

The stall temperature, it follows, belongs to the cooker. Blonder set bowls of water in an oven at rising settings and recorded where each levelled off: at 125°F the water stalled at 115°F, at 175°F it stalled at 140°F, at 225°F it stalled at 160°F, and as the oven approached 425°F the stall crept up toward the boiling point. Nothing in that series is meat. The level moves with the heat going in, the humidity of the chamber, and above all the draught across the surface, which is why a pellet smoker with a fan stalls sooner and for less time, and a tightly sealed electric smoker may not stall at all. It will not build much bark either.

That points at the fix, which competition cooks had found by trial long before anyone explained it to them. The Texas crutch is a wrap: heavy foil, sometimes a splash of apple juice or beer, applied once the meat has taken on the smoke it needs. Received wisdom held that the trapped moisture steams the meat tender. There is no steaming in there. A wrapped brisket at 225°F is closer to a braise, and it climbs simply because water cannot leave. Blonder's line for the meat inside the foil is the one worth keeping: it is like running a marathon in a raincoat, where you will sweat but it will not cool you off.

The proof was a six-pound pork butt. Blonder halved it, rubbed both pieces and set them in a 230°F cooker until the stall arrived, then wrapped one in foil with a tablespoon of water. The wrapped half reached 180°F in about six hours. Four hours after that, the bare half still had not got there. The effect reverses the instant the foil comes off, since a wet surface begins evaporating at once and can pull the interior back to 175°F, which is the argument for unwrapping only at the finishing temperature of about 203°F. Aaron Franklin's answer to the same physics is unwaxed butcher paper, which breathes enough to keep the bark crisp while cutting the evaporation down.

Watch what Franklin actually watches. He judges the wrap by the colour and firmness of the bark rather than by the clock, and he handles the fire to hold a temperature instead of chasing one, which is the whole of fire management in a sentence.

· · ·

The foil also disposes of the collagen theory for good. Collagen converts to gelatin inside a wrap exactly as it does outside one, and inside a wrap there is no stall. The two temperatures coincided and nothing more was ever true of them. Plateaus like this are ordinary once you know to look: ice sits at 32°F while it melts, water at 212°F while it boils, the heat arriving being spent on a change of state instead of a change of temperature. A reading that refuses to move is reporting where the energy went.

For the cook, then, a stalled thermometer is not a fault to be corrected. Opening the lid and stoking the fire both make it worse, since each moves more air across a wet surface. Three responses work. Wait, which costs hours and yields the best bark. Wrap at 150 to 160°F once the smoke is in, which halves the remaining time and softens the crust. Or run the pit up toward 300°F, where the heat outpaces the cooling and the stall shortens or never comes. Brisket is ready at about 203°F, when the probe slides in with no resistance, and the long flat stretch before that reading is where the bark came from.
