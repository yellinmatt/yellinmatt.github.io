Medicine · Surgical Anaesthesia

Ether Day

The most quoted sentence in the history of surgery was probably never spoken, and the man who paid to have it printed had every reason to want it in circulation.

Robert Liston could take a leg off at the thigh in under thirty seconds, and the students in his gallery at University College Hospital brought watches to time him. Speed was the whole of a surgeon’s mercy, because the patient was awake and had to be held. Operating theatres were built as steep bowls so that the men in the back could see over the shoulders of the dressers doing the holding. What surgery could attempt was therefore bounded by what a conscious person could endure having done to him: amputations, bladder stones, growths near the surface. The abdomen and the chest were closed countries.

On the morning of 16 October 1846, in the skylit amphitheatre at the Massachusetts General Hospital, John Collins Warren was to cut a vascular tumour from the left side of the jaw of Edward Gilbert Abbott, a Boston housepainter of twenty-one. Warren was sixty-eight and the most eminent surgeon in New England, and he had agreed to let a dentist try something first. William Thomas Green Morton had promised to render the patient insensible. Morton was late; he was still at the instrument maker’s, collecting a glass inhaler finished that morning. Warren had begun to explain the delay to the gallery when Morton came in. Abbott breathed from the globe for several minutes, and when Warren cut, the patient did not cry out.

Warren then turned to the doctors on the benches and said, “Gentlemen, this is no humbug.” That line is on plaques and in textbooks and in every telling of the story. Its only source is Trials of a Public Benefactor, a book Morton commissioned and paid for in order to argue his own claim to the discovery, and Harvard’s Countway Library, which holds the papers, marks the quotation as probably apocryphal. What Warren wrote in his own hand that evening survives at the Massachusetts Historical Society and reads: “Did an interesting operation at the Hospital this morning while the Patient was under the influence of Dr. Morton’s preparation to prevent pain – the Substance employed was Sulphuric Ether.” More of that day’s entry is given over to his efforts to reassemble the skeleton of a mastodon.

The word in the disputed sentence was well chosen even if the sentence was invented. Twenty-one months earlier, in that same hospital, Horace Wells of Hartford had tried to show a class what nitrous oxide could do for a tooth extraction. The gas ran short, the patient groaned, and the students hooted the dentist out of the room as a humbug. Wells never got past it. He took his own life in a New York jail cell in January 1848, at thirty-three. Anaesthesia reached the world on a second attempt made in a building where the first had been laughed at.

The state itself had no name. Five weeks after the operation Oliver Wendell Holmes, professor of anatomy at Harvard and the most quoted wit in Boston, wrote to Morton to supply one. Holmes is usually credited with coining the word, and his letter shows that he borrowed it. He points to Linnaeus and Cullen, who had used anaesthesia for a pathological absence of feeling, and proposes attaching it to a condition a doctor could now produce on purpose.

Morton meanwhile was trying to own the thing. He had tinted and scented the ether with oil of orange, named the preparation Letheon, and taken out a patent, granted on 12 November 1846, on a substance any apothecary could identify by its smell. The profession was disgusted, on the plain ground that a means of abolishing pain is not a thing to license. Then the claimants arrived. Charles Jackson, the chemist in whose house Morton had boarded and from whom he had taken the idea, wanted the credit. Crawford Long, a country doctor in Jefferson, Georgia, had etherised a patient in March 1842 and published nothing for seven years. In 1850 the French Academy of Sciences sidestepped the quarrel by dividing its Montyon prize between Morton and Jackson; Morton spent his half of the cash on a gold collar for the medal. He died in 1868 with nothing, and Jackson spent his last years in an asylum.

What none of them controlled was how fast it travelled. Henry Jacob Bigelow published the account in the Boston Medical and Surgical Journal on 18 November 1846; a letter carried it across the Atlantic, and on 21 December Robert Liston amputated Frederick Churchill’s thigh at University College Hospital and told the theatre, “This Yankee dodge beats mesmerism hollow.” Within the year army surgeons were etherising men in the field in Mexico. Boston raised a monument in the Public Garden in 1867 that names no discoverer at all and honours only the relief of suffering, which Holmes observed made it a monument to either.

What followed was not simply good. Antisepsis was twenty years behind, and surgeons who no longer had to hurry began working longer, deeper, and into cavities they had never dared open, still in their street coats and with unwashed instruments. Pain had been the limit on surgery and, without anyone intending it, also its guardrail. Once it was gone the mortality of the operating theatre climbed for two decades, until Joseph Lister’s carbolic acid brought it down again.
