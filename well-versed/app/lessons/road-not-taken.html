The Road Not Taken

Frost wrote the poem every graduation quotes as a tease for a friend who could never pick a path, and the friend missed the point.

In the spring of 1915 an envelope crossed the Atlantic from New Hampshire to Edward Thomas in England. It held a single poem, titled “Two Roads,” and no letter saying what it was for. Robert Frost had sailed home on 13 February after two and a half years in England, and Thomas had been the best thing those years gave him. In 1914 Frost rented a cottage called Little Iddens near Dymock in Gloucestershire, and the two men spent much of that summer walking its lanes. Thomas, a book reviewer who had written prose for money all his adult life, was only then starting to write poems, at thirty-six. He also had a habit Frost found irresistible: at every fork he chose a way, and by the end of the walk he was sorry he had not taken the other one.

Frost wrote the habit down. Here is the poem as he collected it the following year.

Two roads diverged in a yellow wood,
And sorry I could not travel both
And be one traveler, long I stood
And looked down one as far as I could
To where it bent in the undergrowth;

Then took the other, as just as fair,
And having perhaps the better claim,
Because it was grassy and ;
Though as for that the passing there
Had worn them really about the same,

And both that morning equally lay
In leaves no step had .
Oh, I kept the first for another day!
Yet knowing how way leads on to way,
I doubted if I should ever come back.

I shall be telling this with a sigh
Somewhere ages and ages :
Two roads diverged in a wood, and I—
I took the one less travelled by,
And that has made all the difference.

Robert Frost, “The Road Not Taken,” the opening poem of Mountain Interval (New York: Henry Holt, 1916). Public domain; text from Wikisource, checked against the 1915 Atlantic printing.

Read the second stanza slowly and the famous version falls apart. The speaker chooses the grassier road because it has “perhaps the better claim,” and two lines later he withdraws the reason: the passing had worn both “really about the same.” The third stanza seals it. Both roads lay under leaves nobody had stepped on that morning, so neither was less travelled than the other. The choice was a toss-up, and the speaker knows it.

The line everyone quotes arrives only after that, and it arrives in the future tense. In the last stanza the speaker imagines himself old, “ages and ages hence,” telling the story with a sigh, and in that retelling the arbitrary pick has become the road less travelled, the one that made all the difference. The poem shows you a man in the act of deciding what he will later claim about a choice he made on impulse. Its title points at the other road, the one regret will collect around.

Thomas did not see himself in it. He took the speaker for Frost and the poem for a grave meditation on choosing, and his letter of 13 June 1915 admitted that its “simple words and unemphatic rhythms” had thrown him: “It staggered me to think that perhaps I had always missed what made poetry poetry.” Frost answered on 26 June that the sigh in the last stanza was “a mock sigh, hypo-critical for the fun of the thing.” Thomas wrote back on 11 July, a little bruised. “You have got me again over the Path not taken & no mistake,” he conceded, and warned that hardly anyone would “see the fun of the thing without showing them.”

That same month Thomas enlisted in the Artists Rifles. He had also been weighing a move to New Hampshire to join Frost, and his biographer Matthew Hollis argues the poem helped push him toward the army. He was commissioned in the Royal Garrison Artillery, went to France, and was killed on 9 April 1917, the first day of the Battle of Arras. Almost all the poems he had begun writing in 1914 reached print only after his death.

Meanwhile the poem had found its public. The Atlantic Monthly printed it in August 1915 alongside “Birches,” and in that first printing the speaker “marked” the first road for another day; by the time it opened Mountain Interval in 1916 he “kept” it.

Frost spent the rest of his life watching readers take the poem at its word. Before reading it aloud he would warn an audience, by his biographer Lawrance Thompson’s account, “You have to be careful of that one; it’s a tricky poem—very tricky.” The warning never took. When Robert Pinsky, as Poet Laureate, asked Americans for their favourite poems, this was the one thousands of them sent in, and most people still misremember its title as “The Road Less Traveled.” The critic David Orr gave a whole book to the problem in 2015 and called it the poem everyone loves and almost everyone gets wrong.

The sincere reading survives because the poem allows it. Frost’s strict form (four stanzas of five lines rhyming abaab, loose four-beat lines) and its plain diction give the last stanza real weight, and the closing lines can be heard as regret as easily as self-mockery. The better reading keeps both. Everybody tells their life this way, turning coin-flips into convictions after the fact, and the poem catches the reflex gently enough that a graduating class can recite it as a creed. So the next time someone offers you “the road less travelled” as advice, you can point out that the poem says the two roads were the same, and that the man telling the story knew it.
