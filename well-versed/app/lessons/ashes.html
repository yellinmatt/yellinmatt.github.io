The Ashes

The most famous prize in cricket is a private keepsake ten centimetres tall that has never once been awarded to anybody.

It stands ten and a half centimetres high and weighs a hundred and twenty-five grams, which is roughly a pack of cards, and it lives in a case in the museum beside the pavilion at Lord’s. England and Australia have played for the Ashes since 1882, and whichever side wins, the urn stays where it is. Marylebone Cricket Club, which owns it, says so on its own page without any embarrassment at all: the urn “is not and never has been a trophy.” It is a present, handed to a young English captain in a country house outside Melbourne, and it sat in his study in Kent for the next forty-five years.

The thing it commemorates took two days. Rain had worked on the pitch at Kennington Oval before the start, and on 28 August 1882 the touring Australians were bowled out for 63, a feeble score. England managed 101 in reply, held to it by the fast bowler Fred Spofforth, who took seven wickets for 46 runs. Australia made 122 second time round, Hugh Massie hitting 55 of them, which left England needing 85 to win in the fourth and last innings of the match. Eighty-five is nothing. At 51 for two they were as good as home. Spofforth, whose nickname was the Demon, had told his side before they went out that this thing could be done, and he then took seven for 44, giving him fourteen wickets in the match for 90 runs. England were all out for 77. Australia won by seven runs, their first victory over a full-strength England side on English soil. The finish was close enough that one man in the crowd is said to have died of heart failure and another to have chewed his way through an umbrella handle.

A few days later a young London journalist called Reginald Shirley Brooks placed a mock death notice in the Sporting Times, mourning English cricket and announcing that the body would be cremated and the ashes taken to Australia. Half of the joke has worn away with time. MCC points out that cremation in Britain was then an unsettled public question, argued over in the papers and not yet plainly lawful, so a Victorian reader met the word with a small jolt that no modern reader gets.

Bligh had already agreed to take a side to Australia that winter, and he sailed three weeks after the defeat with a new and popular object: to bring home the ashes of English cricket. Billy Murdoch, who had just captained Australia at the Oval, promised to defend them. Australia won the first Test of the three in Melbourne; England took the second and third, and it was generally accepted that Bligh had done what he said he would.

Before the series began, Bligh and the amateurs of his team went to stay at Rupertswood, near Sunbury in Victoria, the house of Sir William Clarke, who was president of the Melbourne Cricket Club. On Christmas Eve they played a scratch match against a side made up of guests and estate staff, and won it. Playing along with the newspaper, Sir William’s wife, Janet, Lady Clarke, declared that Bligh had recovered the honour of English cricket and should have the ashes to prove it. A bail, one of the two small wooden crosspieces that rest on top of the stumps, was burned; the ash went into a small terracotta urn; the urn went to Bligh. MCC’s own guess is that the urn was an old perfume bottle or a cosmetic jar, taken off Lady Clarke’s dressing table with no more thought than that. Also in the house that Christmas was Florence Rose Morphy, employed by the Clarkes, whom Bligh married at St Mary’s in Sunbury on 9 February 1884.

I: Reginald Shirley Brooks in the Sporting Times, London, early September 1882. II: the fourth verse of a song in Melbourne Punch, 1 February 1883, cut out and pasted to the urn, where it remains.

The tidy Christmas Eve story has three loose threads, and the people who have gone looking are honest about them. The verse pasted to the urn did not appear in Melbourne Punch until 1 February 1883, five weeks after Christmas. Bligh and his wife both said in later life that the presentation came after the third Test, which finished in Sydney on 30 January. And the red velvet bag made for the urn by Anne Fletcher was not stitched until early 1883. Joy Munns’s reconstruction is that the same object was handed over twice: once at Christmas as a piece of household teasing, and again at Rupertswood at Easter, with the verse pasted on and the bag to carry it in. What is inside is no better settled: tradition says a burnt bail, in 1998 Lord Darnley’s eighty-two-year-old daughter-in-law said the ash was the remains of her mother-in-law’s veil, and other evidence points to a ball. MCC declines to choose, and will not claim to know where the urn came from either.

For about twenty years afterwards nobody used the phrase. It came back with Pelham Warner’s account of the tour he led in 1903–04, and by then the urn had been sitting privately in Kent for two decades. Bligh became the eighth Earl of Darnley in 1900 and kept it at Cobham Hall; an Australian journalist who called on him in 1921 found it in the den among the team photographs, next to a leather-bound account of the tour. It went on public display for the first time in 1926. Florence gave it to MCC after her husband died, and it has crossed to Australia three times since, for the Bicentenary Test at Sydney in 1988, a touring exhibition in 2006, and the State Library Victoria in 2019. Because the original is fragile and is not on offer, MCC had a larger copy cut in Waterford crystal from the 1998–99 series onward, and that is the object a winning captain lifts on the balcony.

The whole story in a hundred seconds, told in the room where the urn is kept, by the institution that owns it. Worth it for the shot of the thing at actual size, which is smaller than almost anyone expects.

What changes hands, then, is a claim. Whoever holds the Ashes keeps them until beaten in a series, and a drawn series leaves them exactly where they were, which is why Australia kept them after the 2–2 in England in 2023 and why they had them secured last winter after winning the first three Tests at home, before finishing 4–1. A side can therefore hold the Ashes for years without ever touching them, and can lose a series without anything physical moving from one country to the other.

MCC’s account of the object ends by calling the whole thing a love story, and the description holds. The urn records a defeat, a printer’s gag about cremation, a scratch game against the gardeners on Christmas Eve, and a marriage in a small church in Victoria; and two countries have spent a hundred and forty-four years playing for the memory of it.
