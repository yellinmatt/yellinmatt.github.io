The Front Crawl

London watched the fastest way through water in 1844, called it un-European, and kept swimming like a frog for another fifty years.

On Saturday 20 April 1844 the British Swimming Society put up a silver medal and invited two Ojibwe men from Canada to race for it in a bath in High Holborn, a tank some 130 feet long. Wenishkaweabee, billed as Flying Gull, beat Sahma, billed as Tobacco, and each crossed in under thirty seconds. Neither swam the way an Englishman did. Each man threw one arm and then the other over the surface and drove the water with his feet, and one paper could only compare the arms to the sails of a windmill. The Times called the style “totally un-European.”

A local swimmer, Harold Kenworthy, then got in and beat them with the breaststroke, the stroke an English gentleman was taught as the correct one: head up, arms and legs sweeping out and drawing back together like a frog’s. The visitors had already raced and were likely tired. The crowd went home having seen a curiosity, and British racing kept to breaststroke and sidestroke for about the next half century.

What the crowd missed is a matter of where the limbs travel when they are not working. In breaststroke both arms and both legs come forward underwater, shoving against the very water the swimmer is trying to pass through, so the body brakes twice in every cycle. A crawler always has one arm pulling while the other swings forward through air, which costs almost nothing, and so the drive never stops. Anyone at Holborn with a watch on one stroke against the other had the answer in front of him.

The stroke came back to English water by a side door. At the Lambeth Baths on 11 August 1873, John Arthur Trudgen won a 160-yard handicap with both arms coming over the surface, a method he said he had picked up abroad while living with his father, an engineer; the accounts disagree over whether that was southern Africa or South America. Under the arms he kept the breaststroke kick. The hybrid took his name, and for a generation the trudgen was the fast stroke of Britain and its colonies.

The kick that finished the job came from the Solomon Islands. Alick Wickham, born in 1886 on Roviana Lagoon and brought to Sydney as a boy, swam the Bronte sea baths face down with his legs beating up and down in the style of home, and the coach George Farmer is supposed to have watched him and said, “look at that kid crawling!” Dick Cavill, of a Sydney swimming family, rebuilt the trudgen along Wickham’s lines in the first years of the new century, and the world learned the result as the Australian crawl. In America Charles Daniels settled the legs at six beats to each full cycle of the arms, and his American crawl took over the sprints.

The swimmer who made the stroke look inevitable was a Chicago teenager coached by Bill Bachrach of the Illinois Athletic Club. On Sunday 9 July 1922, at Alameda in California, Johnny Weissmuller swam 100 metres in 58.6 seconds, the first man under a minute, breaking Duke Kahanamoku’s world record. Five Olympic golds followed, at Paris in 1924 and Amsterdam in 1928, along with sixty-seven world records and an amateur career without a lost race. Then came twelve films as Tarzan. Freestyle events allow any stroke a swimmer likes, and the crawl has won them so completely that “freestyle” now simply names it.

Pan Zhanle won in 46.40, a world record, twelve seconds inside Weissmuller. Coaches divide each arm into four movements: the catch, the pull, the push and the recovery. The catch is where a swimmer separates from a thrasher. The hand enters ahead of the shoulder, and before it pulls, the elbow stays high while the forearm tips down until it hangs close to vertical, so that everything from fingertips to elbow is pressing water backward. Coaches call this the high-elbow catch, or the early vertical forearm. A beginner drops the elbow and presses down instead, which lifts the shoulders and sinks the legs. Through all of it the body rolls along its long axis, raising the recovering shoulder clear of the surface and bringing the mouth round to air. The legs add little speed and spend their effort holding the hips up and the body straight.

Breathing is where most adults give up on the stroke, usually because they hold their breath with the face down and then try to empty and refill their lungs in the half second the mouth is clear. The standard pattern breathes on every third arm stroke, which alternates the side, and it makes a plain drill. Swim slow lengths taking air only on threes, letting the air out steadily into the water while the face is down, so that the turn to the side is for inhaling alone. Roll the head with the shoulders and keep one goggle in the water. After a few weeks the stroke stops feeling like an emergency, and both sides of the body pull evenly.

Every lap swimmer in a hotel pool now swims, near enough, what Flying Gull swam in Holborn, with a Solomon Islander’s kick beneath it and an American’s count on the legs.
