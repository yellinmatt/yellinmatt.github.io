The Rubicon

The best-known river crossing in history is missing from the memoir of the man who made it.

He spent the day where people could see him. At Ravenna, the last town of his province, Julius Caesar watched a public show, looked over the plans for a gladiatorial school he meant to build, and sat down to dinner with a large company. After sunset he slipped out with a few companions, and the biographer Suetonius records the transport: mules borrowed from a bakery next door, harnessed to a carriage. The lamps went out and the party lost the road. They wandered until a guide turned up at dawn and led them back to it on foot along narrow paths. So the conqueror of Gaul reached the frontier of Italy in January of 49 BC, late and on foot, and found his soldiers waiting for him at a stream.

The stream was called the Rubicon, and it carried a legal weight far beyond its size. North of it lay Cisalpine Gaul, which Caesar had governed since 58 BC. South of it lay Italy, where no governor held command at all. A Roman general’s authority over troops, his imperium, ended at the edge of the province he had been assigned, and a commander who led an army past that edge had made himself an enemy of the state. Caesar’s opponents in the Senate wanted him to lay the command down and come home a private citizen. He knew what waited there, because Cato had sworn on oath to prosecute him the moment his army was disbanded. On 7 January the Senate passed its emergency decree, and the tribunes who had been vetoing on his behalf, Mark Antony among them, fled the city in a hired cart dressed as slaves.

He had very little with him. Plutarch counts three hundred horsemen and five thousand infantry, a single legion, the Thirteenth, with the rest of the army still beyond the Alps. Caesar judged that surprise would do more than numbers and had already sent officers ahead, carrying swords only, to occupy the town of Ariminum without a fight. What remained was for him to follow them over the water.

Then, overtaking his at the river Rubicon, which was the boundary of his , he paused for a while, and realising what a step he was taking, he turned to those about him and said: “Even yet we may draw back; but once cross yon little bridge, and the whole issue is with the sword.”

As he stood in doubt, this sign was given him. On a sudden there appeared a being of wondrous stature and beauty, who sat and played upon a reed; and when not only the shepherds flocked to hear him, but many of the soldiers left their posts, and among them some of the trumpeters, the apparition snatched a trumpet from one of them, rushed to the river, and sounding the war-note with mighty blast, strode to the opposite bank. Then Caesar cried: “Take we the course which the signs of the gods and the false dealing of our foes point out. ,” said he.

Suetonius was writing about a hundred and seventy years later, and Plutarch, working in Greek in the same generation, tells it with one difference that matters. In his version there is no piper. Caesar halts, argues with himself in silence, talks the dangers over with his friends, and then calls out a phrase in Greek, “the phrase with which men usually prelude their plunge into desperate and daring fortunes”: anerriphtho kybos, let the die be cast. It was a stage proverb. A fragment of the comic playwright Menander gives the line to a man who has just decided to get married. The two wordings point in different directions. The Latin that everyone quotes announces something finished. The Greek is an order given to the dice, spoken by a player who has yet to see how they fall.

Plutarch also names a witness. Among the friends at the bank he lists Asinius Pollio, an officer of Caesar’s who went on to write a history of the civil wars. That history is lost, and it is probably where both biographers found the scene. The one account that survives from a man who was there is Caesar’s own, and it contains no river. In his Civil War he addresses the Thirteenth at Ravenna on the wrongs done to the tribunes, the soldiers shout that they will defend their general, and the next sentence reads: “Caesar set off with that legion to Ariminum, and there met the tribunes, who had fled to him for protection.” He gives the reader no boundary, no pause and no dice. To describe the crossing would have been to describe the offence, and the book exists to argue that the offence was committed against him.

Plutarch has him inside Ariminum before the next daybreak. Within days Pompey and the consuls had abandoned Rome, and Caesar chased them down the Adriatic coast to Brundisium, where they escaped by sea. He turned first on Pompey’s legions in Spain, telling his friends, “I go to meet an army without a leader, and I shall return to meet a leader without an army.” The return match came at Pharsalus in 48 BC. Pollio was on that field too, and reported what Caesar said as he looked at the Roman dead: “They would have it so. Even I, Gaius Caesar, after so many great deeds, should have been found guilty, if I had not turned to my army for help.” Of all the motives the ancient writers offer, Suetonius found that one the most believable. The man who crossed was a defendant avoiding his trial, and four years of war followed from it. He won, ruled alone, and was stabbed to death on 15 March 44 BC. The republic he had marched on was never put back together.

The frontier outlived him by about two years. Around 42 BC Cisalpine Gaul was merged into Italy, the Rubicon stopped dividing anything, and its name fell out of local use. Floods rearranged the streams of that coast in the Middle Ages, and when Renaissance scholars went looking they found three candidates, the Pisciatello, the Fiumicino and the Uso, and no way of settling it. In 1933 the Fiumicino was officially renamed the Rubicone, which made the question administrative without answering it. The surest thing known about the place is still the detail Suetonius left: it had a bridge, and the bridge was a small one.
