Bourbon

Congress made it a national spirit in 1964 with a page of rules, and almost everything in the glass follows from one of them: the barrel must be new.

On 4 May 1964 both houses of Congress agreed to a short resolution, S. Con. Res. 19, declaring bourbon whiskey “a distinctive product of the United States” and asking the federal agencies to keep foreign whisky sold under that name out of the country. Scotland and Cognac had long defended their spirits by geography. Bourbon’s claim covers the whole country, and since then its identity has lived in a set of federal rules, short enough to learn over one drink, most of which can be tasted.

The rules now sit in the federal standards of identity, at 27 CFR 5.143. Bourbon must be made in the United States from a mash of at least 51 percent corn. It comes off the still at no more than 160 proof, goes into charred new oak containers at no more than 125 proof, and reaches the bottle at 80 proof or above. Nothing may be added but water, so no colouring and no flavouring. Distillers fill out the rest of the grain bill with rye, which reads as spice, or wheat, which reads as soft bread, plus a little malted barley whose enzymes turn starch into sugar. The surprise is time. Plain bourbon has no minimum age at all; a bourbon called straight needs two years in the barrel, and a straight whisky younger than four must state its age on the label.

Of all of them, the new barrel does the most work. A cooperage fires the inside of each oak barrel until the surface blisters into charcoal, and the heat beneath cooks the wood’s sugars into a thin red layer that gives up vanilla and caramel to whatever is stored against it. Kentucky’s hot summers and cold winters push the spirit into that wood and draw it back out every year. A barrel gives its best only on the first filling, so American distillers sell their empties, and most Scotch whisky spends its years in casks that held bourbon first.

The ban on additives answers an older quarrel. Through the nineteenth century much of what sold as Kentucky whiskey was neutral spirit doctored by rectifiers, with colour and flavour from tea, tobacco and worse. Colonel Edmund Haynes Taylor Jr., a Frankfort distiller, led the straight-whiskey men against them, and his friend John G. Carlisle, a Kentuckian serving as Treasury Secretary, helped carry the case into law. The Bottled-in-Bond Act of 3 March 1897 put the government’s name behind a bottle: one distiller at one distillery in one distilling season, at least four years in a federally bonded warehouse, bottled at 100 proof. Distillers took the bargain because they could defer the excise tax until the whiskey left the warehouse.

Nothing in the 1964 resolution or the regulations mentions Kentucky. Bourbon can come from any state, and Tennessee whiskey such as Jack Daniel’s meets the same rules before being filtered through maple charcoal and sold under its own name. Kentucky simply dominates: about 95 percent of American bourbon comes from there, and at the start of 2025 its warehouses held about 16 million barrels, more than three times the stock of fifteen years before. That pile has become a problem. With tariffs and slower demand, Jim Beam announced it would pause bourbon production at Clermont for at least a year in 2026.

Even the name is in dispute. The familiar story credits Bourbon County, Kentucky, named for the French royal house. The whiskey historian Michael Veach notes that the county explanation does not appear in print until the 1870s, and he traces the word downriver instead: the Tarascon brothers, French merchants in Louisville, shipped charred-barrel whiskey to New Orleans, where customers came to ask for the whiskey from Bourbon Street, and the street’s name stuck to the drink.

Taylor’s own distillery survives, which makes its flagship a fair bottle to learn on. He bought the works in Frankfort in 1870 and named it O.F.C., sold it to George T. Stagg in 1878, and the Sazerac Company, owner since 1992, renamed it Buffalo Trace in 1999; it became a National Historic Landmark in 2013. Buffalo Trace Kentucky Straight Bourbon is bottled at 90 proof. Pour an ounce, let it rest a minute, and nose it with the mouth slightly open. The distillery lists vanilla, mint and molasses on the nose, then brown sugar and spice turning to oak, toffee and dark fruit. Sort what you find by its source: vanilla and toffee are the charred barrel, the spice is the rye in the mash, and a few drops of water will bring both forward.

Three phrases on a label hold most of this. Straight promises two years and nothing added. Bottled in bond is the 1897 guarantee, still in force. A straight bourbon with no age on the label is, by law, at least four years old. If only one rule stays with you, keep the new barrel, because it accounts for bourbon’s sweetness and for the casks Scotland fills with Kentucky’s cast-offs.
