The Milvian Bridge

Constantine took Rome at a crossing of the Tiber on 28 October 312, and every telling of the sign he fought under grew larger than the one before.

The battle fell on an anniversary. Maxentius had been proclaimed emperor in Rome on 28 October 306, and six years later to the day the city was celebrating him with chariot races in the Circus. A few miles to the north, where the Via Flaminia crosses the Tiber on a stone bridge called the Milvian, an invading army stood with a strange mark painted on its shields. Maxentius had twice before sat out an invasion behind the Aurelian Walls and watched the invader give up. This time he went out, crossed the river, and fought with the water at his back. By evening his army had broken, he had gone into the Tiber, and his head was later carried into Rome on a spear.

Putting it there had taken Constantine six years. The son of a junior emperor, he was acclaimed by his father’s troops at York in 306. Diocletian had divided the empire among four rulers, two senior Augusti and two junior Caesars, and when he retired in 305 the arrangement came apart into civil war. Constantine held Britain and Gaul. Maxentius, son of Diocletian’s old colleague Maximian, held Italy and Africa. In the spring of 312 Constantine crossed the Alps with the smaller army, beat Maxentius’s generals at Turin and at Verona, and came down the Flaminian Way toward the capital.

What was on the shields is the question the battle has carried for seventeen centuries. The earliest Christian answer comes from Lactantius, a teacher of rhetoric who would soon be tutoring Constantine’s eldest son, writing within a few years of the fight in a pamphlet about the deaths of the emperors who had persecuted the church.

Constantine was directed in a dream to cause the heavenly sign to be delineated on the shields of his soldiers, and so to proceed to battle. He did as he had been commanded, and he marked on their shields the letter Χ, with a perpendicular line drawn through it and turned round thus at the top, being the of CHRIST. Having this sign (☧), his troops stood to arms. The enemies advanced, but without their emperor, and they crossed the bridge. The armies met, and fought with the utmost exertions of valour, and firmly maintained their ground.

In the meantime a arose at Rome, and Maxentius was reviled as one who had abandoned all concern for the safety of the ; and suddenly, while he exhibited the on the anniversary of his reign, the people cried with one voice, “Constantine cannot be overcome!” Dismayed at this, Maxentius burst from the assembly, and having called some senators together, ordered the to be searched. In them it was found that: “On the same day the enemy of the Romans should perish.” Led by this response to the hopes of victory, he went to the field. The bridge in his rear was broken down. At sight of that the battle grew hotter. The hand of the Lord prevailed, and the forces of Maxentius were routed. He fled towards the broken bridge; but the multitude pressing on him, he was driven headlong into the Tiber.

Lactantius, Of the Manner in Which the Persecutors Died, chapter 44, translated by William Fletcher, Ante-Nicene Fathers, volume 7, 1886. The monogram the translation prints after “this sign” is set here as ☧.

Read it for what it leaves out. There is a dream, an order, and two Greek letters painted on wood. Nothing appears in the sky and no one else sees anything. The oldest account of the battle, a speech delivered before Constantine at Trier in 313, has no dream at all, only a divine mind guiding him, with the god left unnamed. Two years later the Senate dedicated a triumphal arch beside the Colosseum, and its inscription credits the victory to instinctu divinitatis, the prompting of a deity, again without saying which.

The famous version arrives a quarter of a century late. Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea, wrote his Life of Constantine after the emperor died in 337, and he says Constantine told him the story in person and swore to it. At about noon, on the march, the emperor and his whole army saw a cross of light above the sun bearing the words “Conquer by this,” which the Latin tradition renders In hoc signo vinces. That night Christ came to him in a dream, and at dawn he called in goldsmiths to make the sign as a jewelled standard, the labarum. Between Lactantius and Eusebius the vision has moved from the eve of battle to some unnamed day of the campaign, gained thousands of witnesses, and migrated from painted shields to a gold banner. Eusebius concedes that it would be hard to credit from anyone but the emperor himself.

Historians have a third text to set beside these. In 310 an orator in Gaul told Constantine that he had seen Apollo at a shrine, attended by Victory holding out laurel wreaths that each foretold thirty years of rule. The German epigraphist Peter Weiss argued that both stories describe one experience, a solar halo, the rings and crossed arcs of light that ice crystals can throw around the sun, read in 310 as a pagan god and later, as Constantine’s loyalties settled, as Christ. Whatever he saw, the pattern in the sources is plain: the further an account sits from the battle and the closer to the church, the more Christianity it carries.

Of the consequences there is far less dispute. Early in 313 Constantine met his fellow emperor Licinius at Milan and agreed to let Christians worship freely and to return the property seized from them; the text known as the Edict of Milan survives as a letter Licinius had posted in Nicomedia that June, copied out by Lactantius. Constantine defeated Licinius in 324 to rule the whole empire, summoned the bishops to Nicaea in 325, refounded Byzantium as Constantinople in 330, and was baptized only as he lay dying in 337. The empire he left was on its way to becoming a Christian one, and the decisive public step had been taken by soldiers carrying a monogram into a fight they were expected to lose.

Here is the position worth holding. Constantine’s conversion was real and gradual, and the bridge is where he first staked his fortunes on the Christian god in front of an army, while letting the Senate carve a phrase any pagan could read as his own. When someone quotes In hoc signo vinces, the informed reply is that the words come from the latest and most embroidered witness, and that the earliest Christian one remembers a dream and two letters on a shield.
