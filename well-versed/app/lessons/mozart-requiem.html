Mozart’s Requiem

The score stops eight bars into the Lacrimosa, and everything after that belongs to a twenty-five-year-old finishing another man’s work against a deadline his widow could not afford to miss.

One page of the autograph score is headed Lacrymosa, and it gives out. A string figure runs across the staves at the top, the four voices enter halfway down the sheet with the word, and the ruled paper between them and after them stays empty. Eight bars, ending on judicandus homo reus, and then no more notes that Mozart wrote anywhere in the Requiem. The recording you have heard runs on for another half hour past that point.

The job had come in that summer from a man who withheld his name. A messenger arrived at the apartment with an unsigned commission for a Requiem mass and half the fee down, fifty ducats. He was almost certainly Franz Anton Leitgeb, steward to Count Franz von Walsegg, whose wife Anna had died on 14 February 1791. Walsegg kept players at his castle at Stuppach and a habit his neighbours understood: he bought pieces from working composers, copied the parts out in his own hand, and let the room draw its own conclusion about who had written them. There was nothing supernatural about the secrecy. It was a clause in the deal.

Mozart wrote at it in the gaps, before the Prague journey for La clemenza di Tito and again once The Magic Flute was up, perhaps six or seven weeks of real work spread across the autumn. He died shortly after midnight on 5 December 1791 with one movement finished. The Introitus is scored complete, every instrumental line written out. Everything after it survives as particell, a composer’s working shorthand: the voices and the bass fully notated, the harmony implied, a phrase of violin or basset horn jotted in wherever the colour mattered enough to fix. A musician reading those pages has all of Mozart’s music and almost none of Mozart’s orchestra.

Constanze was left with a spent advance and a contract that would have to be repaid if nothing was delivered. Within a fortnight the manuscript was with Joseph Eybler, who orchestrated most of the Sequence and then gave it back; by the new year it sat with Franz Xaver Süssmayr, who had copied for Mozart and sat with him over these very pages, and who had the thing finished by early March 1792. The fair copy that went to the count imitated Mozart’s handwriting and carried the date 1792, a year he did not live into.

Süssmayr took the Lacrimosa from bar nine, scored what Mozart had sketched through the Offertory, and then wrote the Sanctus, the Benedictus and the Agnus Dei outright. For the closing Communion he set the new words to the music of the Introitus and Kyrie, which is the soundest decision in the whole completion, since it returns the Requiem to Mozart’s own material at the end. The Sequence is where he fell short. A sketch leaf in Berlin, identified by Wolfgang Plath in the 1960s, carries the head of an Amen fugue whose subject is the Requiem’s opening theme turned upside down. Mozart meant the Dies irae to close with a fugue. Süssmayr closed it with two bars of cadence.

ListenBruno Walter, Wiener Philharmoniker, 1956

Lacrimosa dies illa, qua resurget ex favilla, judicandus homo reus: that day of weeping, when out of the ashes rises guilty man, to be judged. Mozart’s writing reaches the end of that third line and stops. Up to there the strings rock in falling two-note sighs while the chorus climbs a step at a time and the harmony keeps opening outward. After it the same figure carries on and the movement is brought to a full close inside three minutes. Listen for the point where the music stops discovering and starts finishing.

The Introitus and Kyrie were sung at a memorial for Mozart at St Michael’s in Vienna on 10 December, five days after he died. Baron van Swieten put on a performance for Constanze’s benefit on 2 January 1793. And the count, once his score reached him, copied the parts out himself and conducted the Requiem at the Neukloster in Wiener Neustadt on 14 December 1793 in memory of his wife, leaving the room to think what he wanted it to think. The schoolmaster Anton Herzog, who played in that performance, wrote the whole scheme down decades later, which is how it is known at all.

Choirs still mostly sing Süssmayr. Franz Beyer published a revision in 1971, Richard Maunder recomposed the Lacrimosa from bar nine in 1988, and Robert Levin finished the Amen fugue from the sketch in 1991; all three get performed and none has displaced him. Knowing which bars are whose changes the listening. You are hearing a half-finished score that a young man on a deadline made singable, and that nobody working at leisure has since bettered.
