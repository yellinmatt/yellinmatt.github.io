The Dreyfus Affair

Alfred Dreyfus was convicted on a file his own lawyer was never allowed to see, and the French army did not say so out loud until 1995.

Madame Marie Bastian cleaned the German embassy on the rue de Lille, where her job was to burn the military attaché’s waste-paper and where she had for years been carrying it to French intelligence instead. On 26 September 1894 Major Hubert-Joseph Henry found six pieces of thin paper in her delivery, written in French rather than the usual German. Reassembled, they made a covering note offering the attaché five military items, among them the draft firing manual for the field artillery. The sheet was unsigned and undated, and everything France suffered over the next twelve years came off that page.

Nobody had a suspect, so the document was made to produce one. Its range of subjects suggested an artillery officer who moved between departments, which pointed at the trainees on the General Staff. One of them was Captain Alfred Dreyfus. He had money through his wife’s family, no debts, no politics and a clean record, which left a prosecutor nothing to build a motive out of. He was also the only Jew on the staff. Colonel Jean Sandherr, who ran the Statistics Section, summed his man up for a visitor from the Foreign Ministry: the indiscreet curiosity, the air of mystery, and “all the pride and ignominy of his race.”

Handwriting was the only evidence anyone ever produced, and the experts split two against two. Alphonse Bertillon, who had built the police identification system that carries his name, agreed that the writing did not match and then argued that Dreyfus had disguised his own hand to make a frame-up look likely. On 15 October 1894 Major du Paty de Clam told Dreyfus his finger was sore and asked him to take a letter down. The letter was the text of the bordereau. When it was finished, du Paty put a hand on his shoulder and arrested him for high treason. He was shown the document itself fifteen days later.

The court-martial opened on 19 December 1894 with the doors closed on the prosecution’s motion. Two days of testimony established that the accused was inquisitive, had gambled a little, and struck his colleagues as unpleasant. On that showing, ministers expected an acquittal and so did Dreyfus. General Auguste Mercier, the Minister of War, then sent du Paty to hand the presiding judge a file in private: letters between foreign attachés, some altered and some invented, among them one reporting that “cette canaille de D.”, that swine D., had supplied plans for the works at Nice. The defence was never shown it and never told it existed, which the military code forbade in plain terms. The judges read it aloud to one another, deliberated about an hour, and convicted unanimously. The D. turned out to be a civilian draughtsman in the ministry’s own map room.

Then Devil’s Island: a stone hut on less than a square mile of rock off French Guiana, a guard changed every two hours, orders that he speak to nobody, and after a false rumour of escape, two months shackled at the ankles to his bed frame each night. He held himself together by translating Shakespeare and rebuilding the calculus from memory.

In March 1896 Bastian’s basket produced a second torn draft, on the blue paper of the Paris pneumatic post, addressed to Major Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy at his home. Georges Picquart, newly in charge of the Statistics Section, set Esterhazy’s handwriting beside the bordereau and found his match. He read the secret file, found it worthless, and put all of it in a report. General Gonse gave him the reply that has outlasted him: “What do you care if that Jew rots on Devil’s Island?” Picquart was posted to Tunisia. Henry, left at his desk, forged a fresh letter incriminating Dreyfus, pasting his own lines to a genuine one on paper of a slightly different blue.

It came apart anyway, through a stockbroker. J. de Castro saw the bordereau reproduced in a newspaper and recognised a client’s hand, and in November 1897 Mathieu Dreyfus denounced Esterhazy by name. The army tried Esterhazy and acquitted him on 11 January 1898. Two days later L’Aurore gave its front page and most of its second to an open letter from Émile Zola to the President of the Republic, under a title supplied by the paper’s editor Georges Clemenceau, in a run of three hundred thousand copies the vendors sold out of in the street.

The famous part is the ending, where the argument stops and the indictment starts.

I accuse General Mercier of complicity, at least by mental weakness, in one of the greatest inequities of the century.

I accuse General Billot of having held in his hands absolute proof of Dreyfus’s innocence and covering it up, and making himself guilty of this crime against mankind and justice, as a political expedient and a way for the compromised to save face.

I accuse Gen. de Boisdeffre and Gen. Gonse of complicity in the same crime, the former, no doubt, out of religious prejudice, the latter perhaps out of that that has transformed the War Office into an unassailable .

I accuse the three handwriting experts, Messrs. Belhomme, Varinard and Couard, of submitting reports that were deceitful and fraudulent, unless a medical examination finds them to be suffering from a condition that impairs their eyesight and judgement.

Finally, I accuse the first of violating the law by convicting the accused on the basis of a document that was kept secret, and I accuse the second court martial of covering up this illegality, on orders, thus committing the judicial crime of knowingly acquitting a guilty man.

In making these accusations I am aware that I am making myself liable to regarding the press, which make libel a punishable offence. I expose myself to that risk voluntarily.

Let them dare, then, to bring me before a court of law and let the enquiry take place in broad daylight! I am waiting.

Émile Zola, open letter to Félix Faure, President of the Republic, L’Aurore, 13 January 1898. The closing section; the earlier accusations against du Paty de Clam, de Pellieux, Ravary and the War Office press campaign are omitted. English from the Chameleon Translations version in the Marxists Internet Archive.

Zola spends most of the letter on motive, character and cover-up, none of which a court can settle, and finishes on the one thing a court can: a conviction obtained with a document the accused was never allowed to answer. Naming the libel statute was how he forced that question into open court. He was convicted on 23 February 1898 and left for England rather than serve the year. That summer a new Minister of War read the Henry letter aloud to the Chamber, and the forgery came apart under the light. Henry confessed on 30 August and cut his throat at Mont-Valérien the next day. On 3 June 1899 the Cour de cassation quashed the 1894 verdict and ordered a new trial.

He came home to Rennes in August 1899 and lost again. The officers on the bench voted five to two to convict, with extenuating circumstances, and gave him ten years. Treason does not come with extenuating circumstances. The verdict was a settlement between the evidence and the institution, and crowds gathered outside French embassies from London to New York to say so. President Émile Loubet pardoned him on 19 September as mercy for a sick man, which spared the army the word error. Dreyfus accepted because he was close to dead, and then said what a pardon leaves undone: “The government of the Republic has given me back my freedom. It is nothing for me without my honor.”

Seven years later, on 12 July 1906, the Cour de cassation sat with all its chambers together and held that nothing remained of the accusation at all. The judges agreed on his innocence and divided only on what came next, voting thirty-one to eighteen to annul the Rennes judgment sans renvoi, without sending it back: no third court-martial, no further chance for officers to rule on officers. Dreyfus was made a major and a knight of the Legion of Honour, and put the uniform back on in 1914. He died on 12 July 1935, twenty-nine years to the day after the judgment.

The army was slower, and the last stretch is what tells you what the Affair was about. In January 1994 the colonel running the army’s own historical service published a piece in its magazine that never quite got there, writing that the innocence of Dreyfus “is the thesis now generally accepted by historians.” Libération printed the sentence and the Minister of Defence had him out of his office within days. His successor faced a gathering convened by the French Jewish community in September 1995 and described a military conspiracy that had convicted and deported an innocent man, in part on a falsified document. The civil courts had needed twelve years. The army needed a century.
