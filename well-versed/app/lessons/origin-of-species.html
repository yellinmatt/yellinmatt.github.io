On the Origin of Species

Darwin held his theory back for twenty years, until a younger naturalist in the Malay Archipelago mailed him the same idea.

In June 1858 a letter reached Down House, Charles Darwin’s home in the Kent village of Downe, from Alfred Russel Wallace, a collector of birds and insects working his way through the islands of what is now Indonesia. Inside was a short essay, written that February while Wallace recovered from a fever, setting out a mechanism by which one species could turn into another. Darwin wrote to the geologist Charles Lyell the day it arrived. “Your words have come true with a vengeance that I should be forestalled,” he told him, and if Wallace had seen the sketch Darwin wrote out in 1842, “he could not have made a better short abstract!” Even Wallace’s terms, he added, “now stand as Heads of my Chapters.”

Darwin had been keeping the idea to himself for two decades. Home in October 1836 from five years aboard HMS Beagle, he opened a private notebook on the transmutation of species the following year, and on its thirty-sixth page drew a little branching figure under the words “I think”: living species as the tips of twigs, related by descent from a common trunk. In September 1838 he read Thomas Malthus’s Essay on the Principle of Population, which held that people multiply faster than their food supply, and saw that the same arithmetic must press on every plant and animal. A pencil sketch of the theory followed in 1842 and a longer essay in 1844. On 5 July 1844 he wrote to his wife, Emma, asking that if he died she “devote 400£ to its publication.”

What the notebooks worked out fits in one long sentence, and Darwin gave it that sentence in the introduction to the book he eventually published.

Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species, 1st edition (London: John Murray, 1859), Introduction, p. 5. Text from the first edition as transcribed by Project Gutenberg, checked against Darwin Online.

Four facts carry the whole argument. Individuals of a species differ from one another; many of those differences are inherited; far more young are born than can live; and so whatever slight difference helps its owner survive and breed will be passed on more often than the rest. Over enough generations the differences add up, a population drifts apart from its relatives, and a new species stands where a variety stood. Darwin opened the book with domestic pigeons, of which he had “kept every breed which I could purchase or obtain,” because the breeder who picks his parents makes the process visible within a human lifetime. He could not say how traits were inherited. That answer belonged to genetics, and it came after his death.

Lyell and the botanist Joseph Hooker, the two friends who knew how long Darwin had held the theory, arranged a settlement. On 1 July 1858, at a meeting of the Linnean Society in London, the secretary read extracts from Darwin’s unpublished manuscript, then part of a letter he had sent the American botanist Asa Gray in September 1857, which fixed the date of his priority, and then Wallace’s essay. Neither author was in the room. Wallace was still in the Moluccas and knew nothing of it, and Darwin was at home in mourning: his eighteen-month-old son, Charles Waring, had died of scarlet fever on 28 June. The audience was not stirred. Reviewing the year the following May, the society’s president, Thomas Bell, judged that it had seen none of “those striking discoveries” that revolutionise a branch of science.

Darwin dropped the giant treatise he had been writing since 1856 and started what he called an abstract, on the Isle of Wight that summer, finishing it in March 1859. John Murray agreed to publish it on Lyell’s word without reading the manuscript, and Darwin was talked out of putting “an abstract” in the title. Murray priced it at fourteen shillings and printed 1,250 copies. The booksellers at his trade sale on 22 November ordered more than that, Mudie’s lending library took 500, and the book was published on 24 November with a second edition already called for. It appeared on 7 January 1860, 3,000 copies strong.

Readers who open the first edition expecting the famous vocabulary will not find it. The word “evolution” first appears in the sixth edition, of 1872; “survival of the fittest,” Herbert Spencer’s phrase, entered with the fifth. The only illustration is a fold-out diagram of branching lettered lines, the notebook tree drawn out properly. Man gets a single sentence near the end: “Light will be thrown on the origin of man and his history.” The reception split along the expected lines. Adam Sedgwick, who had taught Darwin geology at Cambridge, wrote that he had abandoned the true method of induction, and John Herschel called natural selection “the law of higgledy-piggledy”; Lyell found that the whole had “infinitely exceeded my wildest hopes.”

Within weeks Darwin softened his closing paragraph. In the first edition the powers of life were “originally breathed into a few forms or into one”; the second edition has them “breathed by the Creator.” The rest of the paragraph stayed as he wrote it, down to its last word.

ReadThe closing sentence, first edition

There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed into a few forms or into one; and that, whilst this planet has gone cycling on according to the fixed law of gravity, from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved.

Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859), p. 490.

Wallace never claimed the theory back. He went on calling it Darwin’s, and in 1889 he titled his own defence of it Darwinism. The arrangement looks unfair to Wallace until you set the two documents side by side. His essay of a few pages stated the mechanism; Darwin’s fourteen chapters tested it against pigeons, barnacles, fossils, islands and embryos, and answered the objections before the critics raised them. When Darwin died on 19 April 1882 he was buried in Westminster Abbey, and the idea carried his name because he had done the work of proving it.
