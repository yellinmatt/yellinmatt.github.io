The Cuban Cigar

Two boxes on the same shelf can carry the same famous name, and the reason runs back to one afternoon in Havana in 1960.

What makes a Cuban cigar Cuban sits in a stretch of the island’s western end that has never moved and cannot be carried. The zone is the Vuelta Abajo, in the province of Pinar del Río, where the soil is limestone rubble worn off the surrounding hills and then covered in silt by the rivers that cross it. Habanos S.A., the state company that sells every Cuban cigar abroad, calls it the only zone on the island that grows all three of the leaves a cigar needs: the wrapper on the outside, the binder that holds the bunch together, and the filler at the core. Wrappers come mostly from the district around the town of San Luis, fillers and binders from San Juan y Martínez a short way west. Even inside the Vuelta Abajo, fewer than a quarter of the tobacco fields carry the rating vega de primera, first-class field, and only those may grow leaf for a Habano.

The land stayed where it was. Nearly everything else about the business left within two years. On 15 September 1960, at half past five in the afternoon, soldiers walked into the H. Upmann factory in Havana, which was where Montecristo was made, and informed Alonso Menéndez and Pepe García that the company was no longer theirs. An hour later a second detachment walked into Partagás and did the same to Ramón Cifuentes. “They came inside and said, ‘We’re here to intervene the company,’” Cifuentes told an interviewer three decades afterward, “and they didn’t allow me to take anything from there.” By the end of that single day the Cuban state had taken sixteen cigar factories, fourteen cigarette plants and twenty tobacco warehouses.

What the families still owned when they reached the airport was a set of brand names and the knowledge of how to use them. Alonso Menéndez arrived in Miami with seven dollars in his pocket, and Cifuentes, who had been offered the running of every cigar factory on the island and had declined it, was in New York within days. In 1961 Menéndez’s son Benjamin opened a factory in the Canary Islands with four rollers and put out a cigar called Montecruz, a straight copy of the brand his family had lost, wrapped in the dark Cameroon leaf the trade turned to once Havana tobacco went out of reach. It became the best-selling premium cigar in the United States. Gulf and Western bought the company in 1971 and shifted production in the early 1980s to La Romana in the Dominican Republic, which is where the non-Cuban Montecristo, H. Upmann and Romeo y Julieta are rolled today, and where Benjamin Menéndez went on to oversee the Dominican version of his father’s brand.

Kennedy signed Proclamation 3447 on 3 February 1962 and the embargo took effect at one minute past midnight on 7 February. Pierre Salinger, his press secretary, wrote thirty years later that the president had called him in a few evenings earlier and asked for a thousand Petit Upmanns by the following morning, and that he emptied every humidor in Washington and came back with twelve hundred. That story rests on Salinger’s telling alone and no record confirms it either way. What the proclamation certainly did was fix the geography of the trade. Cuban brands belong to the Cuban state, are sold worldwide by Habanos S.A., and cannot legally be sold in the United States. Those same names, held in America by Altadis U.S.A. or by General Cigar, are rolled in the Dominican Republic and Nicaragua and sit in American shops without difficulty. The useful question in front of a humidor is which Montecristo you are holding, because both of them are authentic and only one is Cuban.

One brand escaped the doubling by arriving too late for it. Cohiba was made in 1966 at the El Laguito factory in Havana and for sixteen years was not sold at all, going to Fidel Castro and to visiting heads of state and to nobody else before it reached the public in 1982. The word is Taíno, what the island’s first people called the rolled leaves that Columbus’s men watched them smoke in 1492. Its leaf is drawn from the best fields of San Luis and San Juan y Martínez, and it is the only Habanos brand in which two of the three filler grades are put through a third fermentation, which is where its particular sweetness comes from. Because no exile carried Cohiba out of Cuba, both sides claim it whole. Cubatabaco registered the mark in Cuba in 1972, General Cigar’s American registration issued in February 1981, Cubatabaco sued in January 1997, and the case has run ever since. In May 2025 a federal judge in Virginia upheld the cancellation of General’s registrations under Article 8 of the Inter-American Convention of 1929. General is appealing, and its Cohibas remain on American shelves while it does.

Watch what the pickers do with their hands. Leaves are taken two or three at a time from one height on the stalk and no other, then sewn onto poles in the order they came off, because a cigar’s blend is built out of leaf position long before anyone rolls it. Notice the colour of the earth between the rows, and the open-sided barn the poles go into, which is the same building the whole zone runs on.

The exiles took something else out with them, which was seed. José Orlando Padrón left Cuba in 1961 and opened a small factory in Miami in 1964, and in 1967 a Nicaraguan businessman brought him a few leaves to try. Padrón rolled one of them rough against his thigh the way he had at home, lit it, and said on the second draw that he was smoking tobacco from the second Cuba. He moved production to Nicaragua three years later. The verdict on that experiment is legible in the ratings: of the fifteen cigars Cigar Aficionado named its cigar of the year between 2004 and 2018, seven were rolled in Nicaragua and four in the Dominican Republic, against three from Cuba.

So the word still names something real, and what it names is that ground in Pinar del Río, which nobody has managed to reproduce and which the people who understood it best have not worked since 1960. Everything else about it travelled. Carlos Toraño Jr., whose father held soldiers off the family farm for three days before giving it up, put the outcome plainly: most of the cigars made today have some Cuban family somewhere in them.
