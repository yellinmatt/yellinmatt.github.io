The Hand Plane

A chisel can dive into the wood. A plane cannot, and nearly everything else about the tool follows from that.

A properly set smoothing plane throws a shaving you can read newsprint through, unbroken from one end of the board to the other, and leaves a surface with a faint wet shine that no abrasive will give you. Sandpaper scratches fibres and leaves the torn ends standing; a keen edge severs them and burnishes what it passes over. The tool doing this is a chisel bedded at a fixed angle inside a body with a flat bottom, and the bottom is the whole invention. A chisel goes as deep as the hands let it. A plane cannot, because the sole rests on the wood and the edge reaches only as far below it as you have set it to reach, so the tool bridges a hollow and takes nothing, then meets a rise and takes the rise off.

Every argument about a bench plane's size starts from that. A long sole spans more of the board at once, registers on high spots further apart, and walks a bowed edge straight in a few passes; a short sole follows whatever it rides on and lifts a fine shaving off a face already flat. So the trade settled on three. The jack, around fourteen inches, does the coarse removal. The jointer, twenty-two inches and up, makes an edge straight enough to glue without a gap. The smoother, about nine inches, takes the last pass before finish. Leonard Bailey numbered them by length in the 1860s, and the numbers held so firmly that a maker in Maine still sells a No. 4 and a No. 7 and nobody asks how long they are.

Wood, though, does not consent to be cut evenly in both directions. Where the grain runs down into the board the edge slices it cleanly; where it rises toward the surface, the fibres split ahead of the edge and lift like a splinter being peeled, and the split runs on until it dips below the level of the cut and pulls a pit out of the finished face. That is tearout, and figured timber does it everywhere at once, which is why the prettiest boards are the hardest to surface. A bench plane carries two defences. The mouth, the slot the shaving rises through, closes to a hair so the sole holds the fibres down right up to the edge. The cap iron, the second piece of steel screwed on top of the cutting iron, is the other, and for most of a century the English-speaking trade did not know what it was for.

It was treated as a stiffener and parked about a sixteenth of an inch back from the edge, where it does very little. Yasunori Kawai and Chutaro Kato at Yamagata University put the cut under a microscope, filmed it, and published in 1989 in the journal of the Japan Wood Research Society. Bring the cap iron to a tenth of a millimetre off the edge, four thousandths of an inch, and it catches the shaving as it forms, folds it sharply back and presses it down into the board so the split has nowhere to run. The tearout stops. Their paper sat in Japanese for two decades until Bill Tindall and Steve Elliott went looking for it, and woodworkers on the WoodCentral forum spent the spring of 2012 relearning a setting their tools had supported since 1869.

That date belongs to Bailey, who built cast-iron planes in Boston through the 1850s and 60s and arrived at the arrangement every bench plane has used since: an adjustable wedge called the frog to bed the iron solidly, a knurled wheel behind it to advance the cut a thousandth at a time, and a small lever to swing the edge square. Stanley Rule & Level bought seven of his patents in 1869, went on stamping BAILEY into the castings for a century, and drove him out over royalties by 1875. What he drew is the plane on the bench at Lie-Nielsen today, and the plane in the twenty-dollar box at an estate sale.

The shape is far older than the man who patented it. The earliest planes anyone has dug up came out of Pompeii, stopped in 79 AD, with others from Roman Britain and from the frontier fort at Saalburg in Germany. A Roman plane is a wooden core wrapped in iron: a blade held down by a wedge, a sole, a mouth, a hand-hold. Put one in a joiner's hands this afternoon and he would need a moment for the wedge and no time at all for the rest.

Japan answered the same problem from the other end. The kanna is a block of white oak holding a laminated iron in a tapered bed, no screws and no wheel, and the user pulls it toward the body instead of pushing it away. Pulling puts the tool in tension, which lets a lighter body stay flat under load, and it leaves the owner tuning constantly, since the only adjustment available is a hammer. Japanese carpenters scrape the sole hollow on purpose so that two or three narrow strips touch the wood and nothing else does. Competitions there are judged on shaving thickness in microns, and a shaving that thin comes only off a plane whose every surface sits where its owner put it.

A working joiner sharpening and setting a plane at the speed he actually does it. Watch how little time the sharpening takes and how much attention goes into seating the cap iron against the back of the iron, which is the part beginners rush.

A prewar Stanley No. 4 costs less than a decent router bit, and the money buys a sound casting and Bailey's adjuster; the rest of it is your labour. Flatten the sole against abrasive stuck down to glass, hone the iron until it takes hair off your forearm, then bring the cap iron up until you can barely see light between its edge and the cutting edge. Then listen. A plane that is right makes one long hiss the length of the board, and what comes off the bench afterward wants nothing but wax.
