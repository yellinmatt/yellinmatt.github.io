The Sistine Chapel Ceiling

A sculptor who did not want the job painted Genesis across the Pope’s vault in four years, and the ceiling still shows him learning on the way across.

Stand just inside the door of the Sistine Chapel and look straight up. The first picture over your head shows an old man asleep and naked beside a vat of wine while his sons hurry to cover him, the Drunkenness of Noah, busy with small figures that are hard to make out from the floor. Walk toward the altar and the panels change as you go. The figures thin out and swell in size, the painted frames seem to open, and over the altar there is only God, alone in a whirl of pink drapery, pulling light apart from darkness. The story of Genesis begins over the altar, but Michelangelo began at the door, so that walking the vault from the entrance lets you watch a sculptor teach himself to paint, in public, on the Pope’s own chapel.

He had not asked for the work. Julius II, pope from 1503, had first hired him for something he did want, a colossal marble tomb, and then let it stall; decades later Michelangelo still called it the tragedy of the tomb. The chapel built by Julius’s uncle Sixtus IV had a vault painted blue and scattered with gold stars, and in May 1504 a long crack opened across it. Once Bramante had braced it with iron tie rods, Julius wanted it repainted. The contract signed on 8 May 1508 called for twelve apostles around the edges and ornament everywhere else. Michelangelo told Julius the plan was a poor thing, and the Pope handed him the whole programme.

What he designed covers a vault about forty metres long and fourteen wide. Down its spine runs a painted stone framework convincing enough to pass for real architecture, and inside it sit nine scenes from Genesis: three on the making of the world, three on Adam and Eve, three on Noah. Muscular nude youths, the ignudi, perch on the painted cornices holding bronze-coloured medallions. Along the sides sit twelve enthroned seers, Hebrew prophets alternating with sibyls, the pagan prophetesses whom Christians credited with foretelling Christ.

He painted it standing. The image of Michelangelo flat on his back is later storytelling. He designed his own scaffold, a bridge that spanned the chapel, and worked on it with his head thrown back and the brush overhead, a posture he described in a sonnet to his friend Giovanni da Pistoia, with a small sketch of himself at work in the margin.

Strip out the jokes and the poem is a report from the job: paint dripping onto his face, his back bent like a bow, and a craftsman’s fear that a painter who cannot see straight cannot paint straight.

That fear shows on the vault. By August 1510 he had worked from the door as far as the Creation of Eve, the halfway mark, and then the painting stopped for about a year. With the first half visible from the floor, the problem was plain. The Deluge, one of his earliest panels, packs dozens of small, struggling bodies into a single frame, and from below they blur into a crowd.

In the second half he paints for the person standing below. The Creation of Adam, the first scene past the break, gives its whole field to two bodies and the gap between a pair of fingers. The last three scenes belong to God alone, and the final one, over the altar, is a single figure twisting through the frame, big enough to read without straining for detail. Between the Deluge and this panel stand the same scaffold and two more years of looking up at his own work.

The scaffold came down for good in the autumn of 1512, and on 1 November, All Saints’ Day, Mass was said under the finished ceiling. Vasari, writing in 1550, says Rome came running to see it and stood dumbfounded. Raphael, at work in the papal rooms a short walk away, went back to his School of Athens and added a figure with Michelangelo’s face.

The colours you see today would surprise most of the people who stood there in the five centuries since. A ten-year cleaning of the ceiling, sponsored by a Japanese television company and finished in 1989, lifted candle smoke, grime and old varnish and uncovered colour far brighter than anyone alive had seen there, at odds with the sombre, sculptural Michelangelo generations had admired. Critics charged that the restorers had also stripped touches he added on dry plaster after the fresco set; conservators now treat the argument as closed. The Last Judgment, which he painted on the altar wall in the late 1530s, a quarter-century after the vault, was cleaned by 1994.

You can walk the ceiling tonight from your desk. Open the Vatican Museums’ free virtual tour of the chapel, turn to face the entrance wall, and tilt the view straight up.

  1. Start over the door. Find the Drunkenness of Noah and the Deluge, zoom out until the whole bay fits, and count the figures you can pick out in five seconds.
  2. Walk the spine toward the altar. Stop at the Creation of Eve, the fifth panel. That is where he climbed down in 1510 and saw his work from the floor.
  3. Finish over the altar. Count again on the last three panels, then zoom out further. Note which scenes still read when they are small.

Keep the method for any fresco cycle, chapel or painted hall: find where the painter began, walk the work in that order, and ask whether the figures grow fewer, larger and plainer as he goes. Early bays show an artist still finding the scale of the room; late ones show what he concluded. If someone at the table calls the Creation of Adam the crown of the ceiling, you have a line ready: Michelangelo painted the vault from the door to the altar, so Adam is the work of a man who had already spent two years practising on Noah, and you can see the difference from the floor.
