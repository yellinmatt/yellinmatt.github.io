Driving a Manual

A brass ring took the grinding out of shifting in 1928. Everything that ring cannot reach is still the driver's job.

You are coming up on a corner at eighty miles an hour in fifth gear, the engine turning eight thousand, and you want fourth. Look at a gear chart and the arithmetic leaves nothing to negotiate: at that same eighty, fourth puts the crankshaft at ten thousand. A gearbox holds no opinion about where the engine happens to be spinning, since the ratio settles it, so when the clutch comes back out something has to find two thousand revolutions in a quarter of a second. If the driver has not supplied them, the road will, hauling the engine up to speed through the driveline. The car pitches onto its nose, the back end goes light, and on a slick entry the driven wheels can stop turning altogether.

Why the ratio is so absolute is worth a moment, because very few people who drive a manual every day could draw one. Inside the case sit two shafts, one turned by the engine and one turning the wheels, carrying pairs of gears that are permanently meshed and permanently spinning. None of those gears is fixed to anything. Each rides free on its shaft until a splined collar slides across and locks it down, and the lever in your hand moves a fork that moves the collar. So the lever never pushes gears together at all. It picks which of the already-turning pairs will carry the load.

Choosing is the easy half. Getting the collar's teeth to enter the gear's teeth is the hard one, because two sets of teeth arriving at different speeds refuse to mesh and hammer at each other instead. Before about 1930 the driver did that work himself, and the gearboxes of the period were known as crash boxes for the noise they made when he failed. The method was double-clutching: clutch in, into neutral, clutch out and a stab of throttle to spin the layshaft up, clutch in again, then into gear. Earl Thompson learned it on a neighbour's 1911 Pierce-Arrow in Portland and never forgave it. “At the rate of 10 miles an hour,” he told the Detroit News in 1931, “you had to wait 10 seconds before a shift could be made without clashing.”

Two years of engineering at Caltech and Oregon State had not produced a degree, and Thompson was running a water company in rural Oregon when he and his brother Kirk built a synchronized gearbox around 1916 and fitted it to a Buick. He applied for the patent in March 1918, waited until November 1922 to be granted it, and drove to Detroit three separate times before anyone would look properly. What finally held Cadillac's chief engineer, Ernest Seaholm, was the crudeness of the thing. “The gear box was full of a sort of bewildering assortment of mechanisms that had been whittled out in some local machine shop in his western state,” he recalled. “It lacked a professional touch in design, but it worked — and intrigued me.” Cadillac built ten variants, ran twenty-five test cars a million and a half miles at the Milford proving ground, threw out Thompson's elaborate preselector, and kept the single piece it wanted: a small friction cone that drags two spinning parts to a common speed before their teeth ever touch. Synchro-Mesh went into production in August 1928 as standard equipment on every 1929 Cadillac and LaSalle, and General Motors bought the patents outright two years later for a reported million dollars.

With the Cadillac Synchro-mesh transmission there is no necessity either for the hesitation in neutral or for the rapid movement of the lever during the later part of the shift. Instead, the movement of the control lever should be one smooth, continuous motion.

The ease and certainty with which a noiseless shift can be made with the new transmission, may tempt some drivers to perform “stunts” for which it is not intended. … There is no advantage to be gained, however, in using intermediate at speeds above 30 miles per hour, and any attempt to shift at higher speeds should be regarded as abuse.

Here is the part that matters, and the reason for everything below it. A synchronizer reconciles the gearbox to itself. It has no authority whatever over the engine, which is sitting on the far side of a disengaged clutch doing whatever the idle circuit tells it to do. The grinding went away in 1928; the lurch did not. Let the clutch out into fourth with the crankshaft two thousand revolutions short and the car will still snatch, because the flywheel and the road have to be brought into agreement and the driveline is the only place available for the argument. Thompson made the shift quiet. Smoothness stayed exactly where it had always been.

That leaves one job, and there is a name for doing it well. Arrive with the engine already turning where the new gear wants it: clutch in, a blip of throttle, then let the clutch back out onto an engine that no longer has anything to catch up on. In traffic this is easy, because the right foot is idle. Into a corner it is not, because the right foot is holding the brake and cannot leave. So it works both pedals at once, the ball of the foot steady on the brake while the outside edge rolls over to catch the throttle. Heel-and-toe, the name a fossil of upright pedal boxes in which the heel really did the work. Scott Mansell breaks it into six movements: brake, clutch in, select the lower gear, blip while still braking, release the clutch, and the revs meet the road. Done properly it makes no sound at all, which is the whole test. A good downshift is one you cannot hear.

The Environmental Protection Agency has counted transmissions in its Automotive Trends Report since 1975, and the line it draws is not ambiguous. Just under 35 percent of new American vehicles came with a manual in 1980. The share fell below one percent in model year 2021 and has stayed under it since, reaching 0.6 percent for 2025, with a little over two dozen models still offering the option at all. Where it survives, though, the buyers are emphatic: 90 percent of Subaru BRZ customers took the manual in 2025, 88 percent of Lotus Emira customers, and 83 percent of Porsche 911 customers on the trims that allow it. About one percent of Toyota Tacoma buyers did, which says the same thing from the other side. The third pedal has stopped being equipment and become a request.
