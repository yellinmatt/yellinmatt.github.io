The Ski Turn

Two thin grooves in the snow are the signature of a turn that took skiing a hundred and twenty years to learn to make.

Behind a skier who knows what he is doing, groomed snow keeps two grooves no wider than the steel edges that cut them, curving away downhill and holding their shape. Behind everybody else there is a fan of loose scrapings thrown sideways off the hill. The two marks record two different things happening under the boot. In the first, every point along the edge of the ski passes through the same slot in the surface, and the ski travels the direction it is pointing. In the second the ski is moving partly sideways while it comes round, shaving snow as it goes, and that shaving is most of what the skier feels. Skiers call the first a carve and the second a skid, and nearly all real skiing is some ratio of the two.

A ski can carve at all because it is not the same width along its length. It is wide at the shovel, pinched underfoot, wide again at the tail, and that waisting is called the sidecut. What the sidecut does is make the steel edge down each side of the ski a shallow curve rather than a straight line. Set the ski up on that edge and stand on it, and the curve pulls the middle of the ski down toward the snow until the whole edge is touching; the ski is now bent into an arc, and having no way to travel sideways it runs around the shape it is holding. Every ski sold today prints the radius of that circle on the topsheet, most recreational skis somewhere between seven and fifteen metres. Tipping the ski further over onto its side tightens the turn by a factor that is exactly the cosine of the tipping angle, so a fifteen-metre ski held at sixty degrees comes round in seven and a half metres, and at seventy degrees in a little over five. That is what the skier is doing with his body when he throws it so far inside the turn that his hip is on the snow. The lean is how he sets the radius.

None of this geometry is new. Woodcarvers in Telemark, in southern Norway, were cutting sidecut into skis before 1840, in the same years they worked out camber, the upward bow that keeps a thin ski from sagging under the boot and ploughing a trench for itself. In 1868 a tenant farmer from Morgedal named Sondre Norheim came down to the Christiania meet at forty-two, twenty years older than the field he beat, in front of spectators who had never watched anybody turn a ski deliberately. His skis measured 81 millimetres at the shovel, 67 underfoot and 70 at the tail, which is four and a quarter millimetres of sidecut and the first ski shape a modern skier would recognise. He also stiffened the binding so that the heel stayed over the ski through a turn, which is the other half of being able to steer one.

Then the shape sat still for a hundred and twenty years. Skis got segmented steel edges in 1926, waterproof laminated cores in the 1930s, aluminium in the late 1940s, fibreglass from 1959, and through every one of those revolutions the sidecut stayed near seven millimetres, which describes a curve far too lazy to bring anybody round a slalom pole. Technique had to supply what the equipment would not. Hannes Schneider, hired at seventeen to guide skiers out of the Hotel Post in St Anton am Arlberg in 1907, turned the workaround into a ladder that could be taught to a whole class at once: snowplough, snowplough turn, stem turn, stem christie, parallel christie, each rung a way of twisting the skis across their own path and then governing the skid that followed. The Arlberg method travelled the world with him and his films, and the stem christie remained the standard turn in ski schools until the late 1960s.

It moved again in a factory in Slovenia. In 1990 two engineers at Elan, Jurij Franko and Pavel Škofič, stopped drawing skis and built a model instead: name the radius you want, the speed, the forces, the angle a human body can hold against them, and solve for the ski that bends into it. What came back was a plank pinched hard at the waist, 110 millimetres at the shovel, 63 underfoot, 105 at the tail, carrying 22.25 millimetres of sidecut against a slalom standard of seven, and a fifteen-metre radius. It took eight of the top ten places the first time it was raced. Elan put it on the market in 1993 as the SCX, for sidecut extreme, and American instructors began reporting that students were making clean parallel turns in an afternoon after years of being told it took seasons. Inside three years the straight skis would not move even when discounted, and several manufacturers who had waited to see went under.

Watch the feet rather than the upper body. The turn starts with the ankles and knees rolling both skis over onto their new edges while the skis are still pointing down the hill, and nothing twists them; the shape arrives afterward, on its own, once the edge is in the snow. Then watch what she leaves behind her.

The last thing the deep sidecut changed is how a skier slows down. A skidded turn scrubs speed the whole way through, because a ski moving sideways is a brake held on continuously. A carved turn has no brake in it, and gives back at the end most of the energy it stored bending; speed comes off it only through the shape of the line. So a carving skier who wants to go slower waits longer before rolling onto the new edges and lets each turn run further across the hill before starting the next, which is why good skiing looks unhurried at speeds that are not. All of that is legible in the snow afterward. The narrowness of the grooves says the skier was riding his edges, and how far they reach across the hill before they turn back says he was choosing his speed with geometry instead of with the snow he threw away.
