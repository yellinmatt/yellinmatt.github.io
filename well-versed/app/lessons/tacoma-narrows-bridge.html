The Tacoma Narrows Bridge

A new bridge over Puget Sound twisted itself apart in a 42-mile-an-hour wind in 1940, and the film of it became the classroom’s favourite picture of resonance, which is the one thing it does not show.

Look past the man to the road behind him. The near deck is level, and a few hundred feet on it leans like the floor of a ship, one curb high and the other low; in a few seconds it will lean the other way. The bridge had opened on 1 July 1940, a main span of 2,800 feet slung across the Narrows of Puget Sound, and it had a reputation before it had a single toll. Workmen riveting the deck that May had felt it rise and fall in long waves, and someone gave it a name first used for the Wheeling suspension bridge over the Ohio, which fell in 1854: Galloping Gertie.

On the morning of 7 November the wind near the east end was blowing at 42 miles an hour. The deck heaved up and down as it had all summer, and then, a few minutes after ten o’clock, the motion changed. The roadway began to twist about its centre line, one side rising while the other fell, a full twist roughly every five seconds. Leonard Coatsworth, news editor of the Tacoma News Tribune, was driving across with his daughter’s cocker spaniel, Tubby, on the back seat.

ReadLeonard Coatsworth, 7 November 1940

I saw the Narrows bridge die today, and only by the grace of God, escaped dying with it.

Just as I drove past the towers, the bridge began to sway violently from side to side. … I jammed on the brakes and got out, only to be thrown onto my face against the curb.

I decided the bridge was breaking up and my only hope was to get back to shore.

… within a few hours I must tell my daughter that her dog is dead, when I might have saved him.

Leonard Coatsworth, eyewitness account, as collected by the Washington State Department of Transportation. Excerpts.

He crawled the rest of the way on his hands and knees. F. B. Farquharson, the University of Washington engineering professor who had been testing a scale model of the bridge in a wind tunnel, walked out to the car to save the dog and was bitten for his trouble. At 11:02 a 600-foot length of roadway tore loose and dropped into the Narrows, and by about ten past eleven the main span was gone; from the toll plaza Coatsworth watched his car go into the water with the dog inside. Barney Elliott of The Camera Shop had been filming on the deck, and his footage and Farquharson’s are why everyone who has sat through a physics class has seen the bridge die.

That film went into the physics textbooks as the great demonstration of resonance, and the label is wrong. Resonance is a push timed to a body’s own rhythm, the way you push a child on a swing at the top of each arc; Halliday and Resnick’s widely used texts of the early 1960s said the wind made “a fluctuating resultant force in resonance with a natural frequency of the structure.” In 1991 K. Yusuf Billah and Robert Scanlan argued in the American Journal of Physics that a steady gale offers no such timing. Once the thin deck twisted, the twist changed how the air struck it, and the air pushed the deck further the way it was already turning. The bridge kept time and the wind paid for every swing, a self-excited motion engineers call flutter.

Why this bridge? The state’s engineer, Clark Eldridge, had drawn a deck stiffened by open trusses 25 feet deep. Leon Moisseiff, the consultant brought in as a condition of the federal grant, substituted a solid plate girder 8 feet deep and told the Public Works Administration his changes would cut the estimated cost from $11 million to $7 million. Do the division and the difference is plain: Eldridge’s stiffening was about a hundred-and-twelfth of the span, Moisseiff’s a three-hundred-and-fiftieth, on a roadway only 39 feet wide between the cables. Farquharson’s report named the solid girders, which met the wind like a wall, as the probable cause of the instability, and proposed cutting holes in them or fitting streamlined fairings. The replacement that opened on 14 October 1950 hung from the same piers on a Warren truss 33 feet deep, with dampers and double lateral bracing.

Do this The 1940 film, with a stopwatch

Open the film and find the stretch where the deck is twisting, with the centre line staying still while the edges see-saw. Start a timer and count full twists, one edge up and back down, for one minute. Farquharson’s own stopwatch count, published in 1949, was twelve a minute, a twist every five seconds. Many copies online run fast: in 2015 Donald Olson, Steven Wolf and Joseph Hook showed in Physics Today that a film-to-video conversion speeded the common version by half, so it shows about eighteen. If you count eighteen, you have caught the error yourself.

Watch the collapse footage on Wikimedia Commons →

The count is also the tool to keep. When something shakes, ask who keeps time. If an outside push arrives on its own schedule and the motion is worst at one particular rate, smaller if you push faster or slower, you are looking at resonance, and the cure is to change the timing or the stiffness. If the structure keeps its own pace and the motion simply grows as the push gets stronger, it is feeding itself, and the cure is to change its shape so the push stops helping. The bridge twisted every five seconds, at its own pace, in a steady gale, and that is the second case.

The wreck of the 1940 span still lies on the bottom of the Sound, visited by divers from Titlow Park. When a textbook or a museum placard comes up, here is your line: Tacoma Narrows is the most famous film of resonance in physics, and it is misnamed, because the wind had no rhythm, the deck made its own, and a twist that feeds on the wind is flutter.
