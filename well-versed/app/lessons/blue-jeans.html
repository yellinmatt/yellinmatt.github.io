Blue Jeans

A Reno tailor could not afford the patent on his own idea, so he offered half of it to his cloth supplier for about sixty-eight dollars.

In 1872 a tailor in Reno, Nevada, posted a cheque for $350 to his cloth wholesaler in San Francisco and sent two pairs of work trousers after it by express. Jacob Davis had been born in Latvia and wrote English by ear. He made his trousers from cloth bought off Levi Strauss & Co., and late in 1870 he had begun setting copper rivets at the corners of the pockets, where a working man’s seams gave way first. They were selling faster than he could sew them. The letter that went with the cheque asked Strauss to pay for a patent and take half of it in return.

Jacob W. Davis to Levi Strauss & Co., 1872, in his own spelling, as transcribed from the letter in the company’s archives

Strauss paid. Patent 139,121 was granted on 20 May 1873 to Davis and to Levi Strauss & Company jointly, under the title “Improvement in Fastening Pocket-Openings,” and what it protects is small: “the employment of a metal rivet or eyelet at each edge of the pocket-opening, to prevent the ripping of the seam at those points.” Nothing in it mentions a colour, a cloth or a cut. The garment the firm put into production later that year was sold as a “waist overall,” meaning an overall that stopped at the waist, and it had one back pocket, a watch pocket, a cinch across the back, buttons for suspenders and a rivet at the crotch. The cloth was nine-ounce denim from the Amoskeag mill in Manchester, New Hampshire.

Denim is a twill, a weave that throws a diagonal rib across the surface, and Britannica gives the detail that matters in half a line: coloured warp, white filling. The lengthwise threads are dyed and the crosswise ones are left alone, so the cloth is blue on its face and pale on its back, and wherever the face is rubbed down the white comes up from underneath. The seat and knees of the pair above have had years of that. The name is said to come from serge de Nîmes, after the French town.

Nearly every other feature of the garment arrived afterwards, and the company’s archive can date each one. The leather patch with two horses straining to pull a pair apart came in 1886. Around 1890 the patent lapsed into the public domain, the firm began giving its products lot numbers, and the copper-riveted waist overall drew 501. A second back pocket followed about 1901 and belt loops in 1922, with the suspender buttons kept on for the men who would not change. The red tab was sewn to the right back pocket in 1936. A year later the back-pocket rivets were hidden under the cloth, and in 1966 they were dropped for bar tacks, a dense row of stitching. Wartime rationing took the crotch rivet and the cinch, and for the duration the curved pocket stitching was painted on. The company itself went on printing “overalls” on the label until 1960, when it gave way to the teenagers who had been saying “jeans” for a decade. The Smithsonian took a pair into its permanent collection in 1964.

One more date explains what enthusiasts pay for. Around 1927 Cone Mills of Greensboro, North Carolina, developed a ten-ounce denim for the 501 alone, with a red line woven into its selvage. A selvage, or selvedge, is the edge a shuttle loom leaves when it carries a single weft thread back and forth across the warp: the cloth finishes itself and cannot fray. Shuttle looms are slow and weave a narrow bolt, and American mills gave them up in the 1970s for faster machines whose cut edges have to be overstitched. Japan went the other way. Big John sewed the country’s first jeans in 1965 from American cloth, Kurabo wove the first wholly Japanese denim in 1972, and the old looms stayed in use there. Cone’s White Oak plant, the last large American source, closed in 2018.

That gives three benchmarks and a way to read any pair. The plain 501 still sold in every mall is the pattern itself, button fly and five pockets. Levi’s Vintage Clothing reissues particular years from the archive, the 1890 model among them, with the single back pocket and the cinch restored. Above those sit the Japanese selvedge makers, whose cloth comes off shuttle looms and is sold raw, unwashed and stiff, for the owner to fade himself. To place a pair, turn up the cuff and look for the finished edge, weigh the cloth in the hand, since denim is graded in ounces to the square yard, and check the pocket corners. Davis called his idea “a trifle hardley worth speakeing off.” He asked for seventeen years of protection on it, and the rivets are in the same place today.
