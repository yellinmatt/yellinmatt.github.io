Etiquette · The Table

The Place Setting

The silver around a plate is the host’s menu, written out before the first course arrives, and it reads in one direction.

Sit down at a properly laid table and you may find a dozen pieces of silver, china and glass in front of you in an arrangement nobody ever explains. Everything the host means to serve has already been laid out there, and the whole of it reads from one rule: start at the outside and work in. The fork on the far left belongs to the first course, the one beside it to the second, and by the time your hand reaches the pieces closest to the china you are eating whatever the kitchen thought worth serving last. Nothing on a formal cloth is decorative. Every piece is there because something is coming that needs it.

The grammar of the arrangement is short, and the Emily Post Institute has published it in more or less the same form since 1922. Knife blades always face the plate, cutting edge in. No more than three of any one implement go down at once; a fourth course arrives carrying its own fork. The oyster fork is the single exception to the whole left-hand rule, sitting out beyond the spoons on the right, and it is the only fork ever laid on that side of the plate. Glasses stand above the knives in the order they will be poured, the water goblet directly above the dinner knife with the wines to its right, and each glass is cleared when its course is. Bread sits on the left and drinks on the right, which is worth having in the fingers rather than the head at a crowded table: touch each thumb to its forefinger and the left hand makes a lowercase b, the right a d.

Three minutes, and the useful part is watching the setting empty. Each course leaves with its own silver, so the piece you want is always the outermost one still on the cloth, and the dessert fork and spoon come down from above at the end.

None of this is old. For most of the history of the European table there was nothing to arrange, because a guest brought his own knife, ate from a dish he shared with his neighbours, and worked with bread, a spoon and his fingers. Bruegel’s Peasant Wedding of 1567 shows a barn full of people at dinner and not one fork in the room.

The fork reached the West from Byzantium and was received as decadence. In 1004 a noblewoman of Constantinople named Maria Argyropoulina married Giovanni Orseolo, son of the doge, and came to Venice with golden forks among her possessions. Plague killed her within three years, and half a century later the reforming monk Peter Damian wrote her up as a cautionary case: she declined to touch her food with her fingers, had her eunuchs cut it into pieces small enough to spear on “a certain golden instrument with two prongs,” and died as such vanity deserved. Later retellings pinned the anecdote on a different Byzantine bride, the dogaressa Theodora Doukaina, who arrived seventy years afterward; the woman Damian was actually attacking was Maria. Italy came round regardless. By the fourteenth century the fork was unremarkable on the peninsula, helped along by a national dish that defeats every other implement.

England held out for another two hundred years, until a Somerset eccentric walked home from Venice with the habit. Thomas Coryat covered close to two thousand miles of Europe on foot in 1608 and published the account three years later as Coryat’s Crudities, and buried in the Italian chapters is the first description in English of what a fork is for.

ReadCoryat, 1611 · tap the dotted words for a gloss

I observed a custome in all those Italian cities and townes through the which I passed, that is not used in any other country that I saw in my travels… The Italian and also most strangers that are in Italy, doe alwaies at their meales use a little forke, when they cut their meate. For while with their knife which they hold in one hande they cut the meate out of the dish, they fasten their forke, which they hold in their other hand upon the same dish, so that whatsoever he be that sitting in the company of any others at meale, should touch the dish of meate with his fingers, from which all at the table doe cut, he will give occasion of offence unto the company, as having transgressed the lawes of good manners, in so much that for his error he shall be at the least , if not in wordes…

The reason of this their is, because the Italian cannot by any means indure to have his dish touched with fingers, seeing all mens fingers are not alike cleane.

Thomas Coryat, Coryat’s Crudities (London, 1611).

He brought the habit home and was teased for it. A friend named Laurence Whitaker took to calling him Furcifer across the table, a Latin pun that reads as fork-bearer and also carries the schoolroom sense of gallows-bird, near enough to scoundrel. Coryat sets the joke down in his own book without any sign of embarrassment. Within two generations the silver fork was standard among fashionable Englishmen and the laugh had changed sides.

Which leaves the one habit at the table that Americans and Europeans still notice in each other. A European cuts with the knife in the right hand, eats with the fork in the left, and puts neither down. An American cuts the same way, then lays the knife on the plate, shifts the fork across to the right hand, and lifts the food with it. Emily Post called the manoeuvre zigzagging. Everyone takes it for a homegrown American practice, and it is French: Darra Goldstein, the founding editor of Gastronomica, traces the cut-and-switch to a fashion that took hold in France in the early eighteenth century, on the reasoning that a delicate mouthful belongs in the stronger hand. Nineteenth-century Americans copied it from Paris because Paris was then the authority on such questions. Paris promptly moved on, and a French manual of 1853 was already describing the newer style as the one that keeps the fork where it is.

So the manner most people read as native to America is a piece of eighteenth-century French fuss that France itself gave up, which is roughly how these things go. Both styles are correct, and Anna Post, Emily’s great-great-granddaughter, has said her sense is that the American one is slowly falling out of use. Fights like that come round every century or so. The rule underneath them has not moved: whatever sits farthest from your plate is what you need first.
