Medicine · The End of Smallpox

Vaccination

A country doctor tested a piece of dairy-farm folklore on his gardener’s son, and the disease that killed three of every ten people it infected has not occurred naturally since 1977.

On the fourteenth of May, 1796, in Berkeley, Gloucestershire, a physician of forty-six made two shallow cuts in an eight-year-old boy’s arm and worked into them fluid from a sore on a milkmaid’s hand. The boy was James Phipps, son of the doctor’s gardener. The milkmaid was Sarah Nelmes, who had caught cowpox from a Gloucester cow named Blossom. Seven weeks later he did the thing that makes a modern reader wince and deliberately gave the boy smallpox. Nothing happened. Edward Jenner had shown that a trivial disease of cattle could be borrowed to make a person proof against smallpox, and every vaccine anyone has ever received descends from those two cuts.

Smallpox earned the fear. Caused by variola virus and passed by the breath of the infected, it had been with humanity at least three thousand years; lesions read as smallpox mark the mummified head of the pharaoh Ramesses V. Of the people it infected, roughly three in ten died. Most of those who lived carried its pitted scarring on their faces for good, and some were left blind. Trade and empire carried it everywhere, and in the Americas it killed on a scale that helped decide who ruled.

Surviving smallpox once bought protection for life, and long before Jenner people had learned to buy that protection at a discount. The technique was variolation: matter from a smallpox pustule scratched into the skin or blown up the nose, a deliberate small dose in place of an accidental large one. Old practice in China, India and the Ottoman Empire, it reached fashionable England in 1721 through Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, who had watched it done in Constantinople. It worked, and because it used live smallpox it sometimes killed the person it was meant to save. Jenner had been variolated as a schoolboy and his health never entirely recovered, so he knew the price from the inside.

He also knew a piece of dairy-country talk. Milkmaids who picked up cowpox from the udders of their cows, a scattering of sores on one hand and a day or two of feeling unwell, seemed never to take smallpox afterward. Jenner spent years collecting such cases, and when Sarah Nelmes walked into his surgery in May 1796 with a rash he could identify as cowpox, he had his experiment ready. What he wrote up afterward reads like a careful man keeping notes.

“The more accurately to observe the progress of the infection, I selected a healthy boy, about eight years old, for the purpose of inoculation for the Cow Pox. The matter was taken from a sore on the hand of a dairymaid, who was infected by her master’s cows, and it was inserted, on the 14th of May, 1796, into the arm of the boy by means of two superficial incisions, barely penetrating the , each about half an inch long.

On the seventh day he complained of uneasiness in the , and on the ninth he became a little chilly, lost his appetite, and had a slight head-ach. During the whole of this day he was perceptibly indisposed, and spent the night with some degree of restlessness, but on the day following he was perfectly well…

The only difference which I perceived was, in the state of the limpid fluid arising from the action of the virus, which assumed rather a darker hue, and in that of the spreading round the incisions, which had more of an look than we commonly perceive when matter has been made use of in the same manner; but the whole died away (leaving on the inoculated parts scabs and subsequent ) without giving me or my patient the least trouble.

In order to ascertain whether the boy, after feeling so slight an affection of the system from the Cow-pox virus, was secure from the contagion of the Small-pox, he was inoculated the 1st of July following with variolous matter, immediately taken from a pustule… but no disease followed.”

Edward Jenner, An Inquiry into the Causes and Effects of the Variolae Vaccinae, London, 1798.

The Royal Society was not persuaded, so Jenner published the Inquiry himself in 1798, with the case histories of thirteen people whose old cowpox had already protected them. The long title named the cattle disease variolae vaccinae, smallpox of the cow, from vacca, the Latin for cow. That one word is why the practice came to be called vaccination, and in 1881 Louis Pasteur extended the term to his own protective inoculations, as a tribute to Jenner. Every shot you have had, against tetanus or measles or influenza, is quietly named after a Gloucestershire dairy cow.

What Jenner did with the discovery matters as much as the discovery. He took out no patent, discouraged others from profiting, and posted cowpox matter, dried onto threads and glass, to anyone who asked. He called himself the Vaccine Clerk to the World and held that the sciences are never at war. In 1807, with Britain and France fighting, he asked Napoleon to free two prisoners and got the answer “what that man asks is not to be refused.” In his garden at Berkeley he turned a thatched summerhouse, the Temple of Vaccinia, into a clinic open one day a week, where the poor were vaccinated free.

Objection came from several directions at once, and the argument is as old as the practice. Some refused on religious grounds to be treated with matter drawn from an animal. Others complained, fairly, that cowpox was scarce and that samples passed hand to hand were often contaminated in smallpox wards. The loudest opponents were the variolators, whose considerable incomes Jenner had just made obsolete. Britain banned variolation in 1840 and made vaccination compulsory in 1853, and the compulsion produced anti-vaccination leagues and protest marches through English towns.

Getting to zero took a different method. When the World Health Organization launched its intensified campaign in 1967, up to fifteen million cases were still occurring every year, and blanket vaccination had already failed in dense cities. What worked was surveillance and containment: find each new case, isolate the patient, vaccinate everyone in a ring around them, and pay rewards for reports. A steel needle forked at the tip held one exact dose between its prongs, so a barely trained worker could vaccinate hundreds a day. Region by region the disease went out: North America in 1952, Europe in 1953, South America in 1971, Asia in 1975, and Africa last of all.

The end came down to a handful of names. Rahima Banu, three years old, on an island in the Bay of Bengal in late 1975, was the last natural case of variola major, the killing form, and she recovered. Ali Maow Maalin, a hospital cook in Merca, Somalia, developed the rash in October 1977 and was the last natural case of any kind; he lived, later vaccinated children against polio, and died of malaria in 2013. The last person to die of it was Janet Parker, a medical photographer infected in 1978 through the Birmingham medical-school building, one floor above a laboratory that still held the virus.

· · ·

On the eighth of May, 1980, the Thirty-third World Health Assembly declared the world free of smallpox, the only human disease ever deliberately erased. Jenner had written in 1801 that “the annihilation of the smallpox, the most dreadful scourge of the human species, must be the final result of this practice,” and he was right by a hundred and seventy-nine years. What survives of the virus now sits in two guarded freezers, one at the CDC in Atlanta and one at the VECTOR institute in Koltsovo, Siberia, which is the only reason anyone still keeps the vaccine on a shelf.
