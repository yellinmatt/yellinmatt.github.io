Bretton Woods

The postwar world ran on a promise to trade dollars for gold at thirty-five dollars an ounce, and on a Sunday night in 1971 the promise was suspended.

On 1 July 1944, less than four weeks after the landings in Normandy, 730 delegates from 44 nations arrived at the Mount Washington Hotel, a white resort under red roofs at the foot of the tallest peak in New Hampshire. The meeting went on the books as the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference. Henry Morgenthau Jr., the American Treasury Secretary, presided over three weeks of work whose object was to design the money of a world that had not yet stopped fighting, and the delegates signed their Final Act on 22 July.

Two men had been drafting it since 1942. John Maynard Keynes, adviser to the British Treasury, wanted a clearing union, in effect a central bank for central banks, issuing a currency of its own that he called bancor. Harry Dexter White, chief international economist at the United States Treasury, wanted something smaller: a stabilization fund pooled from the members’ gold and national currencies, able to lend to a country that had run short of foreign exchange. Both of them wanted fixed exchange rates. The United States held about three-quarters of the world’s official gold in those years, and the agreement came out looking like White’s plan. Keynes won concessions, among them a clause letting the new fund ration the currency of a country that piled up surpluses, and he chaired the commission that drew up the bank.

The machine they built had a single gear made of metal. Every member declared a par value for its currency, a fixed rate that could be changed only to correct a serious imbalance, and kept its market rate within 1 percent of that figure against the dollar. The dollar alone was tied to gold, at $35 an ounce, and the United States took on the job of holding that price: a foreign central bank could bring dollars to the Treasury and leave with bullion. Two institutions kept the arrangement running. The International Monetary Fund lent to members with short-run deficits, and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, soon known as the World Bank, financed rebuilding and later development. The Fund came into legal existence in December 1945, once 29 countries had signed its Articles, though the system ran as designed only from 1958, when the European currencies became convertible for everyday trade.

Its weakness lay in the arithmetic. Other countries wanted dollars to hold as reserves, and dollars reached them in quantity only through American deficits: imports, investment in Europe, the bases and aid of the Cold War. The dollar shortage of the late 1940s had become a glut by 1960. That year the economist Robert Triffin set out the trap for Congress. If the United States stopped running deficits, the world would go short of reserves; if it kept running them, the dollars held abroad would outgrow the gold behind them, and confidence in the $35 price would drain away. In time there were more dollars in foreign hands than the United States had gold to pay for.

Washington and its allies spent the 1960s propping up the price. In 1961 eight countries pooled their gold to hold the London market at $35. Private buying drained the pool after 1966, Britain devalued sterling in November 1967, and the speculators turned on the dollar; a rush for gold between that November and March 1968 broke the pool apart. After that only central banks could swap dollars for gold at the official rate, and in May 1971 they began doing so in large amounts.

Richard Nixon took fifteen advisers to Camp David from Friday 13 to Sunday 15 August 1971. Among them were the Treasury Secretary John Connally, the Federal Reserve chairman Arthur Burns and a Treasury undersecretary named Paul Volcker, who would chair the Fed eight years later. On the Sunday evening Nixon went on television with three orders: a 90-day freeze on wages and prices, the first in peacetime; a 10 percent surcharge on imports; and the one that ended the system.

In recent weeks, the speculators have been waging an all-out war on the American dollar. The strength of a nation’s currency is based on the strength of that nation’s economy—and the American economy is by far the strongest in the world. Accordingly, I have directed the Secretary of the Treasury to take the action necessary to defend the dollar against the speculators.

I have directed Secretary Connally to suspend temporarily the of the dollar into gold or other , except in amounts and conditions determined to be in the interest of monetary stability and in the best interests of the United States.

Now, what is this action—which is very technical—what does it mean for you?

Let me lay to rest the of what is called .

If you want to buy a foreign car or take a trip abroad, market conditions may cause your dollar to buy slightly less. But if you are among the overwhelming majority of Americans who buy American-made products in America, your dollar will be worth just as much tomorrow as it is today.

The repair came that December. The ten largest industrial economies met at the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, where the United States dropped its surcharge and the others accepted a dollar worth roughly a tenth less against their own currencies. The new rates did not hold, in part because the Federal Reserve kept lowering interest rates and dollars kept flowing out of the country. In March 1973, faced with buying up a flood of dollars they did not want, the major governments let their currencies float. The fixed-rate order had lasted twenty-nine years from the week at the hotel, and gold convertibility was never restored.

What survived was the pair of institutions. The IMF and the World Bank still lend from Washington, in a world of floating rates their founders never planned for, and the Fund keeps about 2,814 metric tons of gold on deposit, under rules that bar it from buying another ounce. The measure Nixon announced in defence of the dollar is now in its fifty-sixth year.
