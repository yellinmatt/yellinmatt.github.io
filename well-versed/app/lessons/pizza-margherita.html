Pizza Margherita

Naples says its most famous pizza was named for a queen in June 1889, but the dish is older than the visit, and the one document tying the two together looks like it was made later.

Beside the door of Pizzeria Brandi in Naples hangs a marble plaque under the crowned arms of the House of Savoy. Qui 100 anni fa nacque la pizza Margherita, it reads, with the dates 1889 and 1989: here, a hundred years ago, the pizza Margherita was born. The story behind it is told in every pizzeria in the city. Margherita of Savoy, Queen of Italy, wife of her cousin Umberto I, wanted to taste the food of the Neapolitan streets, so the pizzaiolo Raffaele Esposito was sent for and made her three pizzas. She liked best the one dressed in tomato, mozzarella and basil, the red, white and green of a flag that had flown over a united Italy only since 1861, and Esposito named it after her.

The house keeps a letter as proof. Dated 11 June 1889 from the royal residence at Capodimonte and signed by Camillo Galli, head of table services to the royal household, it confirms that “the three kinds of pizza” Esposito prepared for Her Majesty were found delicious. Esposito had bought the shop in 1883, when it traded as the Pizzeria di Pietro e basta così. The Brandi nephews who took it over in the 1930s gave it their own name, and the letter stayed with the house.

In 2014 the food historian Zachary Nowak laid that letter beside other documents of the royal household and published what he found in Food, Culture & Society. The royal seal sits in the wrong place on the page. Galli’s signature fails to match the specimens of it held in Italian archives. The letter addresses its recipient as “Raffaele Esposito Brandi,” a double surname that binds the cook to the family who would own the shop forty years later, and it says nothing at all about what was on any of the three pizzas, though naming the queen’s favourite is the whole point of the legend. Nowak titled his paper “Folklore, Fakelore, History,” and the middle word is his verdict on the letter: a tradition dressed up as a record, most plausibly by someone who stood to gain from it.

The dish itself needs no queen. When the European Union registered Pizza Napoletana as a traditional speciality in February 2010, the specification it published carried a short history, and it quietly gives the game away.

ReadCommission Regulation (EU) No 97/2010, annex, § 3.8

The first appearance of the ‘Pizza Napoletana’ may be dated back to the period between 1715 and 1725. Vincenzo Corrado, a native of the town of Oria, and chief cook for Prince Emanuele di Francavilla, in a treatise on the foodstuffs most commonly used in Naples, stated that the tomato was used to season pizza and macaroni…

The most popular and famous pizzas from Naples were the ‘Marinara’, created in 1734, and the ‘Margherita’, which dates from 1796-1810. The latter was presented to the Queen of Italy upon her visit to Naples in 1889, specifically on account of the colour of its seasoning (tomato, mozzarella and basil) which are reminiscent of the colours of the Italian flag.

Commission Regulation (EU) No 97/2010 of 4 February 2010, entering Pizza Napoletana in the register of traditional specialities guaranteed. English text from legislation.gov.uk.

Read closely, the official history keeps the queen and drops the invention. On its own dating the tomato, mozzarella and basil pizza was being baked in Naples some eighty years before 1889, so the most the royal visit could have done was lend a famous name to an old habit. The Marinara, older still, carries no cheese at all: tomato, garlic, oregano and oil.

What the same regulation fixes in law is the method, and the numbers are worth knowing because they explain the pizza on the plate.

StageThe rule
DoughFor every litre of water, 50 to 55 g of sea salt, 3 g of brewer’s yeast and about 1.8 kg of soft-wheat flour, kneaded twenty minutes at low speed
RisingTwo hours in the mass, then four to six hours as balls of 180 to 250 g, at about 25°C
DiscStretched by hand to no more than 35 cm, 0.4 cm thick at the centre, with a rim of 1 to 2 cm (the cornicione)
Margherita60 to 80 g of crushed peeled tomatoes, 80 to 100 g of sliced buffalo mozzarella or Mozzarella STG, fresh basil, 4 to 5 g of extra virgin olive oil
FireWood-fired oven, about 485°C on the floor and 430°C under the dome
BakeNo longer than 60 to 90 seconds

Those last two lines do most of the work. At nearly five hundred degrees the rim, which carries no topping and has been left thick on purpose, swells with steam in seconds and blisters dark in spots, while the thin centre cooks through before the tomato can dry out. The result is soft enough at the middle to fold, and it is meant to be eaten at once, before the centre softens further.

The legend survived the scholarship because Naples found it useful. On 3 July 1984 seventeen of the city’s leading pizzaioli founded the Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana and registered a production rulebook, and in November 1989 the association marked the Margherita’s centenary with the first world congress of pizzaioli, the same anniversary the Brandi plaque commemorates. The rulebook became the EU specification of 2010. On 7 December 2017, at a committee meeting in Jeju, South Korea, UNESCO inscribed the art of the Neapolitan pizzaiuolo on its list of the intangible heritage of humanity, noting that some 3,000 practise it in Naples and that the craft is still learned in the bottega, by watching a master at the oven.

The sensible conclusion is that the queen is a garnish. The letter is almost certainly later than its date, the pizza is older than its name, and neither fact touches what Naples has really protected, which is a dough, a fire and ninety seconds. Order a Margherita anywhere and judge it by the rim: if it rose high and took its spots of char in a minute and a half, the cook has kept the tradition, whoever it was named for.
