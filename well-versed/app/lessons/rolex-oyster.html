The Rolex Oyster

Rolex proved its sealed watch case on a Channel swimmer who was pulled from the sea after ten hours, and the words on the back of your own watch still carry that story.

On 21 October 1927 Mercedes Gleitze walked back into the English Channel with a small gold Rolex tied around her neck. She had already swum it. Two weeks earlier, on 7 October and at her eighth attempt, she had crossed in fifteen hours and fifteen minutes and become the first British woman to make the swim. Then a doctor named Dorothy Cochrane Logan, swimming as Mona McLennan, announced a faster crossing of her own. Her story turned out to be a fabrication, but it had already cast doubt on Gleitze’s, and the only answer a swimmer has to doubt is to get back in the water.

The second attempt never reached the far shore. The sea that late in the year was much colder than anyone chooses for a crossing, and after ten hours and twenty-four minutes she gave up and was taken out. Those who watched her hold on in that cold were satisfied that the first swim should stand. The watch at her throat had kept time through all of it, and that unglamorous fact is how the waterproof wristwatch first earned the public’s trust.

A watch has three ways in for water: the bezel that holds the crystal, the back, and the crown, where a stem passes through the wall of the case so that you can wind and set the hands. Rolex had tried once to close them, with a model called the Submarine in 1922. The case it introduced in 1926 screwed all three down hard against the middle of the case, the bezel, the back and the crown together. Hans Wilsdorf, who ran the company, chose the name himself.

Rolex knew what it had. A front-page advertisement that year heralded her crossing, and the company now counts her as, in effect, its first testimonee, the athlete whose feat does the selling that a catalogue cannot. The demonstration outlived the swim by decades: in the 1960s Oysters were still often displayed inside fish tanks, to show that water could not get in. Her own watch, in nine-carat gold, was sold by a descendant at Christie’s in 2000 for £17,037. On 9 November 2025 it went for $1.7 million at Sotheby’s in Geneva, to a private bidder from Asia sitting in the room.

The back of your own watch carries the rest of the story, so take it off and turn it over. The Federation of the Swiss Watch Industry sets out how to read what you find. A watch marked “water-resistant” must meet the standard ISO 22810, and the figure beside the word is a pressure in bar with its rough depth in metres. “Waterproof” and its cousins, the federation says, correspond to no official standard at all. “Diver’s watch” followed by a depth claims a separate standard, ISO 6425, which asks for at least 100 metres, markings you can read in the dark and a way to time the dive. A watch that says nothing is held by default to 2 bar, about 20 metres, which the federation calls enough for the shower and for ordinary swimming.

Then try the crown. If it turns several times before it will pull out to the setting position, you are wearing Wilsdorf’s idea, and it only works screwed back down before the watch meets water. The gaskets behind those threads do the real sealing, and they age, which is why the federation tells owners to follow the maker’s service advice; a fogged crystal that clears within a few minutes, it adds, is condensation and no fault. A twenty-year-old rating on an unserviced case describes a test the watch passed long ago.

So the line to keep is a modest one: the first waterproof watch anyone believed in was proved on a Channel swim that was called off after ten hours.
