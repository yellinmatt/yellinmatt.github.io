The Map and Compass

Your compass needle points at a wandering spot in the Arctic Ocean, your map tells you how far off that is, and the rest is learning to read the brown lines.

The red end of a compass needle settles on a patch of the Arctic Ocean that in 2025 lay at 85.8 degrees north and 139.3 degrees east, nearer to Siberia than to Canada and about three hundred miles short of the North Pole. The angle between where the needle points and where north actually is has a name, declination, and it is not a rounding error. Across the lower forty-eight it runs from roughly twenty degrees east in parts of Washington to roughly twenty degrees west in parts of Maine. One degree off line costs you ninety feet in a mile. Twenty degrees puts you in the wrong drainage by lunch.

Sailors had been correcting for the discrepancy long before anyone could account for it, and the first man to map it was Edmond Halley, remembered for the comet and owed something for this. In two Atlantic voyages between 1698 and 1700 he commanded the small ship Paramore, sent out to measure rather than to trade, and read the variation of the compass hundreds of times in foul weather. The chart he published in 1701 joined every place where the needle erred by the same amount with one curving line. Those were the first isogonic lines ever printed, and the trick of drawing a curve through equal values went on to give us the isobars on a weather forecast and the contours on the sheet in your pocket.

Halley’s lines were good for his own decade and no longer, because the thing they described will not hold still. In 1831 James Clark Ross stood on the north magnetic pole in Arctic Canada, and for the next century and a half it worked its way around that archipelago at a crawl. Then in the 1990s it accelerated, crossed the top of the world and struck out for Siberia. Over the past year it covered thirty-six kilometres, against nine for its counterpart in the south. NOAA and the British Geological Survey publish a new World Magnetic Model every five years to keep pace, and that model is what quietly corrects the compass in a phone, an airliner and a NATO warship.

The drift turns up in places nobody files under navigation. Runway numbers are magnetic headings rounded to the nearest ten degrees with the last digit dropped, so a strip lying along 172.5 degrees is runway 17. When the field has moved far enough the airport repaints them, which is why Fairbanks changed 1L-19R to 2L-20R in 2009 and expects to do it again around 2033.

On paper the correction is drawn in the margin as three lines springing from one point: a star for true north, MN for magnetic north, GN for the grid the sheet is ruled on, with the angles written underneath. A good compass lets you dial that angle in once with a small screwdriver and then forget it. Without the adjustment you carry the arithmetic instead, and the arithmetic has a rhyme: west is best, east is least. Add a west declination to a bearing you took off the map, subtract an east one, and what you are left with is the number to steer.

The map’s own language is the brown line. Every point along one contour stands at the same elevation, and every fifth line is drawn heavier and labelled so the eye can count without tracing. On the standard American sheet, the 7.5-minute quadrangle at a scale of 1:24,000 where one inch covers two thousand feet, the interval between lines is ten, twenty or forty feet depending on how much the country moves. After that the pattern does the work. Lines crowded together are a face too steep to want; lines opening out are a bench you could camp on; and a V in the contours points uphill where a stream cuts back into the slope, downhill where a spur runs out of it.

Three drills turn the pair into an instrument. Orienting the map comes first: lay the compass on it and turn the whole sheet until its north agrees with the needle, so that a ridge on your right is a ridge on the right of the paper. Taking a bearing comes second: set the straight edge of the baseplate along the line from where you are to where you want to be, rotate the bezel until its orienting lines run north on the map, read the number at the index, then hold the compass out in front of you and turn your body until the needle drops into the orienting arrow. The third is resection, which finds you when you are unsure. Shoot a bearing to a summit you can name, draw that line back on the map, repeat with two more landmarks well separated from the first, and you are standing inside the small triangle they make. A large triangle means a mistake worth hunting down.

Six minutes, and worth doing with a compass in your hand instead of watching it cold. Catch the body turn near the end. Once the bearing is locked into the bezel, the work moves from the dial to your feet, and you walk until the needle sits back down in the orienting arrow.

Then there is a piece of field craft that sounds like cheating. Walking four miles to a bridge over a river, you will miss it, because nobody holds a bearing that cleanly through brush and blowdown. So aim deliberately upstream, hit the river knowing which way to turn, and walk down to the bridge. The same habit puts a road, a lakeshore or a ridgeline across your line of travel as a handrail, so any error runs into something that catches it.

· · ·

All of which invites the obvious objection, since the phone in your pocket already knows exactly where it is. The first answer is that batteries die in the cold and screens break. The second is more interesting. In 2020 Louisa Dahmani and Véronique Bohbot of McGill found that the more lifetime GPS use a person reported, the worse they navigated once the device was taken away, with heavier use over time tracking a steeper decline in the hippocampus-dependent memory the task calls on. A map and compass leave you no way to move except by building a picture of the country in your head and testing it against what you can see, and an afternoon spent doing that teaches you more terrain than a season of following the blue dot.
