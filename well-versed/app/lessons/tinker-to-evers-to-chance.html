Tinker to Evers to Chance

A columnist eight lines short wrote three Cubs into the Hall of Fame, and the play he mourned is the one every scorecard still writes as 6-4-3.

In July 1910 Franklin P. Adams wanted to leave the New York Evening Mail and get out to a ball game, and the foreman of the composing room told him his column was eight lines short. So he wrote eight lines. Adams was a Cubs man working in a Giants town, and he put on a New Yorker’s grief for the occasion. The verses ran on 12 July under the title “That Double Play Again,” and they are worth your morning for two reasons: they teach the most useful play in baseball, and they show how far a good line can carry a modest statistic.

ReadFranklin P. Adams, 1910 · tap the dotted words for a gloss

These are the saddest of possible words:
  “Tinker to Evers to Chance.”
Trio of bear cubs, and fleeter than birds,
  Tinker and Evers and Chance.
Ruthlessly pricking our bubble,
  Making a hit into a —
Words that are heavy with nothing but trouble:
  “Tinker to Evers to Chance.”

Text as printed by Representative Poetry Online. The Hall of Fame’s copy has “Thoughtlessly” for “Ruthlessly.”

Here is what the Giant has just suffered. Put a runner on first with fewer than two out and let the batter hit a grounder to the shortstop. The batter must run, so the man on first loses his claim to the bag and has to head for second. Baseball calls that a force, and it means a fielder can retire him simply by holding the ball on the base, with no tag. Tinker, at shortstop, throws to Evers at second for one out. Evers, already moving, relays to Chance at first ahead of the batter for the other. One swing has cost two outs and, usually, the rally.

Scorekeepers say all of that in three digits. Henry Chadwick, an English-born sportswriter, adapted the box score from the cricket scorecard and saw the first one into print in 1859; his habits gave us K for a strikeout, taken from the last letter of “struck.” Every fielder now has a number, and the only one you have to memorize is the shortstop, who sits out of sequence.

So Tinker to Evers to Chance is a 6-4-3. Cover the right-hand column below, say each answer aloud, then find an archived game tonight, take a pen and an envelope, and score one full inning yourself.

Keep one test for any game you watch: is a man on first, are there fewer than two out, and is this hitter likely to put it on the ground? Three yeses mean a double play is on, and you will see the shortstop and second baseman edge toward the bag before the pitch.

The numbers behind the poem are smaller than its fame. One count gives the trio 54 double plays from 1906 through 1910, across 770 games, or about one every fourteen games. The whole Cubs team turned 491 in those years, third in the National League, and in 21 World Series games the three never completed one together. Winning explains the reverence better: 116 victories in 1906, four pennants in five seasons, championships in 1907 and 1908. Then the poem kept circulating, and in 1946 all three were elected to the Hall of Fame together, with the verses widely credited for it. Chance had died in 1924, and the other two were too unwell to attend.

Tinker and Evers also could not stand each other. On 14 September 1905 they fought on the field after Evers took a cab to the park and left his teammates in the hotel lobby. Evers later dated the silence to a hard throw in 1907 that drew a curse and a laugh: “That’s the last word we had.” His summary is the best sentence anyone said about them. “Tinker and myself hated each other, but we loved the Cubs.” The legend has them speechless until they met at a radio broadcast of the 1938 World Series and embraced, although the two signed on together for a stage skit in 1929, so treat the thirty-three years as a good story with a hole in it.

The play now has legal protection. On 10 October 2015, in Game 2 of the National League Division Series, Chase Utley slid past second base into Rubén Tejada of the Mets to break up a double play and fractured Tejada’s right fibula. Rule 6.01(j) arrived the next season and requires a bona fide slide. Since 2023 teams must also keep two infielders on each side of second, which puts the 6 and the 4 back on opposite sides of the bag, where Tinker and Evers stood. When it comes up, here is your line: Tinker to Evers to Chance reached Cooperstown on fifty-four double plays, eight lines of filler and two men who weren’t speaking.
