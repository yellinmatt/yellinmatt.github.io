The Broad Street Pump

More than five hundred people died within a few Soho streets in ten days of 1854, and the doctor who blamed one pump had to win his argument after the dying had stopped.

On the last night of August 1854 cholera broke out in the streets north of Golden Square, in Soho, and it killed faster than anything London had seen. Most of its victims were dead within hours of the first cramps. By the second week of September more than five hundred people had died within 250 yards of a single street corner, where Cambridge Street met Broad Street, and the households that could afford to leave had already gone.

John Snow lived a short walk away, in Sackville Street. Born in York in 1813, the son of a labourer, he had made himself the leading anaesthetist in London, and the year before he had given chloroform to Queen Victoria at the birth of Prince Leopold. Since 1849 he had also been arguing, to an audience that mostly declined to listen, that cholera did not travel on bad air. The medical consensus held to miasma, the belief that epidemics rose from the stench of rot and sewage. Snow thought the disease was swallowed. Some agent in the evacuations of the sick reached the drinking water, he argued, and the water carried it into the next gut.

He suspected the pump on Broad Street almost at once. A sample he examined on the evening of 3 September looked nearly clean, so he went for the dead instead: a list of names and addresses from the General Register Office, then door after door, asking the families where their water had come from. Nearly every death lay closer to the Broad Street pump than to any other. The few that did not usually turned out to belong to households that sent for Broad Street water because they preferred its taste.

The cases that settled it were the ones who lived. The parish workhouse on Poland Street stood almost surrounded by stricken houses; it had its own well, and of its 535 inmates five died. The brewery on Broad Street employed more than seventy men, who drew a daily allowance of beer, had a deep well on the premises and, by the proprietor’s account, never went to the street pump; few of them fell ill. And in Hampstead, miles to the north, a widow of 59 who had not set foot in Broad Street for months had its water carted out to her because she liked it. She drank it on 31 August and again on 1 September, and died on the 2nd. A niece who drank from the same bottle on a visit died at her own home in Islington, where there was no cholera at all.

The most terrible outbreak of cholera which ever occurred in this kingdom, is probably that which took place in Broad Street, Golden Square, and the adjoining streets, a few weeks ago. Within two hundred and fifty yards of the spot where Cambridge Street joins Broad Street, there were upwards of five hundred fatal attacks of cholera in ten days. The mortality in this limited area probably equals any that was ever caused in this country, even by the plague; and it was much more sudden, as the greater number of cases terminated in a few hours. The mortality would undoubtedly have been much greater had it not been for the flight of the population.

As soon as I became acquainted with the situation and extent of this of cholera, I suspected some contamination of the water of the much-frequented street-pump in Broad Street, near the end of Cambridge Street; but on examining the water, on the evening of the 3rd September, I found so little , that I hesitated to come to a conclusion.

The result of the inquiry then was, that there had been no particular outbreak or increase of cholera, in this part of London, except among the persons who were in the habit of drinking the water of the above-mentioned .

I had an interview with the of St. James’s parish, on the evening of Thursday, 7th September, and represented the above circumstances to them. In consequence of what I said, the handle of the pump was removed on the following day.

The handle came off on Friday 8 September, and here the legend runs ahead of the record. By then the outbreak had largely burned out. Snow’s own table counts 143 fatal attacks beginning on 1 September and 12 on the 8th, a fall driven by the emptying of the streets and perhaps by the well clearing itself. What the board’s order did was prevent a second wave, and what Snow had done was name the cause while the evidence was still warm.

He drew his map afterwards. Snow showed it to the Epidemiological Society of London on 4 December 1854 and printed it in the second edition of his book the next year: each death a short black bar at its address, the bars stacking into dark ledges along the streets nearest the pump. The story in which he spreads out a street plan and watches the culprit emerge reverses the order of events. He had the suspicion from the start, and the map was built to persuade.

Persuasion took longer than the epidemic. In the spring of 1855 Henry Whitehead, the 29-year-old curate of St Luke’s in Berwick Street, went back over the outbreak house by house for the parish’s inquiry committee, and he found what Snow had lacked: a first case. Frances Lewis, five months old, lived at 40 Broad Street, a few feet from the pump. She fell ill in late August, and her mother soaked the child’s soiled napkins and emptied the water into the cesspool at the front of the house. A surveyor opened it in May 1855 and found its brickwork decayed, the sewage free to seep toward a well that lay under three feet away. The baby died on 2 September. Her father, a police constable, fell ill on the 8th, the day the handle came off.

Snow had meanwhile run a larger trial across the river, where two water companies laid their pipes down the same streets. The Southwark and Vauxhall Company drew from the Thames in London, below the sewer outfalls; the Lambeth Company had moved its intake upstream in 1852. Neither the households nor the miasma could tell the difference. The cholera could.

Snow did not live to see the argument won. He died of a stroke at his house in Sackville Street on 16 June 1858, aged 45, and The Lancet gave him two sentences, praising his researches on chloroform and saying nothing of cholera. Robert Koch isolated the comma-shaped bacterium that causes the disease in 1883. In April 2013 The Lancet published a correction to the obituary.

Broad Street became Broadwick Street, and a replica pump stands near the corner today, its handle missing on purpose, with a pink granite kerbstone set into the pavement to mark the site of the well. The pub across the road carries Snow’s name, a tribute he would have declined in person, since he had given up drink as a young man. His method outlived all of it: count the dead, find where they drank, and study the people who should have died and did not.
