The Axe

Why a round of wood is already half-split before you touch it, and what shape of iron finishes the job.

On a Friday night in February 2013 the Norwegian state broadcaster gave twelve hours of prime time to firewood. Four of them were a made programme about cutting it, drying it and stacking it. The other eight were a fire burning in a hearth, filmed live, with nothing else on the screen. Close to a million people watched, a fifth of the country. The complaints that arrived afterwards concerned the stacking: half the viewers wrote in to say the bark had been laid facing up, and the other half wrote in to say it had been laid facing down. “One thing that really divides Norway is bark,” said Lars Mytting, whose book had set the whole thing off.

That book is Hel ved, published in 2011 and bought by more than a hundred and fifty thousand Norwegians out of a population of five million. The title means solid wood, and in Norwegian it is also what you call a person who can be relied on. The bark question has no settled answer. Two questions underneath it do: why a log comes apart when you hit it, and what shape of head opens it fastest.

Wood is a bundle of long cells glued side by side, and its strength runs with them. Samuel Record, writing for the Yale forest school in 1914, gives the first half plainly. “Wood exhibits its greatest strength in tension parallel to the grain.” The second half is the one that matters at the chopping block. “The tensile strength at right angles to the grain is only a small fraction of that parallel to the grain.” His tables put longleaf pine at 240 pounds to the square inch pulled apart across the radius and 298 across the tangent, and he files the behaviour under a single heading. “Tension at right angles to the grain is closely related to cleavability. When wood fails in this manner the thin fibre walls are torn in two lengthwise while the thick-walled fibres are usually pulled apart along the primary wall.” A round standing on a block is already most of the way to being two pieces. The plane it will part on was decided by the tree, and the axe has only to find it and spread it.

The two axes in a woodshed are therefore answering different questions. A felling axe has to cut across the fibres, which asks for a thin bit and a narrow angle behind it so that the steel keeps moving. A splitting axe has to enter and then keep widening. Gränsfors Bruk, the Swedish forge, describes its splitting heads as “forged and ground to a concave, quite thin blade at the bit,” a shape that “helps the axe to go fast into the wood — and then split efficiently when the thicker part hits and pushes apart the wood.” The Forest Service manual compresses the same point into an instruction: a splitting axe’s “blade should have a much steeper angle than a felling and bucking ax.” The edge buys entry. The swell behind it does the work, trading a downward blow for a sideways push against a material that has almost no strength in that direction.

Because the shape carried the argument, the shapes multiplied. American catalogues of the last century offered a Michigan, a Dayton, a Maine, a Jersey and a dozen besides, each one a regional opinion held firmly enough to be stocked and sold. ReadManual · USDA Forest Service, 1999

IStand the wood on end, either on the ground or on a chopping block, if the wood has been cut straight.

IISwing straight down toward the top of the block. Use your body weight, with your knees snapping into position just as you hit the block of wood.

IIIGive the ax handle a slight twist just as the bit hits the block of wood. This throws the block of wood apart and prevents the ax from sticking.

IVOn a knotty, gnarly block of wood you’ll need to start your split from the outside edges and slab off the sides.

Bernie Weisgerber, An Ax to Grind: A Practical Ax Manual, USDA Forest Service Technology and Development Program, 1999

Three rules sit outside that sequence. If the bit buries itself, rap the end of the handle sharply downward with the palm of your hand, which the manual prefers to working the head from side to side. And keep the edge honest: the bevel runs at “about 25 degrees, but slightly convex,” and the manual is blunt about the shortcut. “Never use an electric high-speed dry bench grinder to sharpen your ax. That type of a grinder will almost certainly draw the temper from the ax and ruin it.” Gränsfors adds the rule that saves fingers: the poll of a splitting axe was never meant for heavy pounding, so driving a steel wedge with it is work for a maul.

Watch the hands rather than the swing. The twist at contact is a few degrees and it happens in the last instant before the bit lands, which is why a beginner who has been told about it still does not do it. Watch also where the round sits: low, so that a miss ends in the block or the ground.

Which brings the argument back to Norway. Green wood carries between fifty and more than a hundred percent of its dry weight in water, and it has to come down to twenty or thirty before it will burn well, because the fire must boil every drop off before the fibre itself can catch. Splitting dries the wood as well as sizing it. It opens the long faces of the round to the air and shortens the distance the water has to travel to leave. A season in a good stack finishes the work, and the last thing anybody controls is which way each split is turned while it sits there. That is the whole substance of the quarrel, and nothing I can find settles it. Everything upstream of it is settled: where the wood will part, what makes it part fastest, and how long it then has to stand in open air.
