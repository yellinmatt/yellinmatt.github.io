The Panama Hat

The best-known straw hat in the world is woven by hand on the coast of Ecuador, takes its name from the place it was sold, and is graded by how many weaves fit in an inch.

Theodore Roosevelt anchored off Colón on 14 November 1906, the first sitting American president to travel outside the country, and he had come in the wet season on purpose. For three days it barely stopped raining. At the great cutting through the continental divide he climbed into the cab of a 95-ton Bucyrus steam shovel and sat at the controls in a white suit and a black-banded straw hat, while the photographers worked. Underwood & Underwood copyrighted the scene as a stereograph the same year. The president was digging a canal in Panama, so the hat on his head was plainly a Panama. It had been woven far to the south, in Ecuador.

Roosevelt wrote up the trip for Congress a month later, and the report shows a man who had enjoyed himself enormously in the mud.

The name was already old by then; the photograph only fixed it. Hats from the Ecuadorian coast had been shipped north through Panama since the early nineteenth century, and the term appears in print by 1828. In 1835 a Spanish trader named Manuel Alfaro settled in Montecristi, in the coastal province of Manabí, and organised its weavers for export through the isthmus; his son Eloy, later twice president of Ecuador, helped pay for the Liberal Revolution of 1895 with money from the trade. When gold was found in California, thousands of prospectors crossed Panama on the way west and bought a cheap, light hat against the sun at the crossing. By 1850 the United States was taking some 220,000 a year. A buyer named a hat after the place where the money changed hands, and nobody in New York or San Francisco had reason to ask further.

Every one of those hats begins as a plant. Carludovica palmata, called toquilla in Ecuador, looks like a palm without being one, and grows in the coastal lowlands. Its young shoots are cut before they open, split into strands, boiled to strip the green, dried, and bleached with sulfur smoke over a wood fire. The weaver starts at the centre of the crown, working a small rosette outward, then presses the growing crown over a wooden block and spirals down its sides before turning the brim at a right angle. On the coast the best weavers work in the early morning and the late afternoon, when the heat will not dry the straw past bending. A coarse hat takes a day and a great one eight months. UNESCO added the craft to its list of the intangible heritage of humanity on 5 December 2012.

Quality is a count. Hold a hat up to the light, look halfway up the side of the crown, and count the rows of weave across an inch; dealers use a jeweller’s loupe. Price rises much faster than the number. Simon Crompton of Permanent Style puts anything above eighteen rows in the range of a very good everyday hat, and notes that a hat of thirty-three rows can cost ten times one of twenty. At the summit sits Simon Espinal of Pile, a village near Montecristi, who wove from 19 June 2013 to 20 February 2014, just under a thousand hours, to finish a hat with about four thousand weaves to the square inch. An earlier hat of his at around three thousand sold for $25,000. Brent Black, the Hawaii dealer who commissioned it, said of the masterpiece, “This is not a hat that should be on anybody’s head.”

In the 1920s and 1930s the Panama, blocked in the shape of a fedora, displaced the stiff straw boater as the summer hat a man wore with a light suit, and it has kept that place since. My advice is to buy in the middle. A Montecristi of around twenty rows, from a dealer who can tell you which village wove it, gives the soft hand and the even weave that make the hat worth wearing, while a superfino is a textile you will be afraid to take into the rain. Crompton’s rule is the better test in any case: a hat that suits your face outranks a finer hat that does not. And when someone at the table says it is from Panama, you can tell them about Montecristi, and about the president in the shovel.
