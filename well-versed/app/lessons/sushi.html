Sushi

The word is an old adjective meaning sour, and for a thousand years it described the rice, which was thrown away.

On the western shore of Lake Biwa, in Takashima, a shop called Kitashina has been selling sushi since 1619, and nothing in it is raw. The fish is nigorobuna, a carp endemic to the lake. Each is gutted through the gills so the body stays whole and the roe stays in, packed in salt for a year, then buried in cooked rice under a weighted lid for up to three years more. The eighteenth generation of the same family slices it thin and serves it with sake: amber roe, flesh gone soft and faintly cheesy, and a smell that newcomers reliably mistake for something that has gone wrong. The rice comes off in a grey paste and goes in the bin.

That is narezushi, the oldest form of the dish, and the word belonged to it long before anyone thought to lay a slice of fish on a pillow of rice and eat both. Sushi began as an adjective. It is the archaic terminal form of sui, sour, a conjugation that survives in modern Japanese almost nowhere else, the everyday word for sour having become suppai. The name describes a taste, and the taste belonged to the grain.

The rice in that barrel is equipment. Cooked grain packed around salted fish and sealed away from the air feeds lactic acid bacteria, which turn its starch into acid; the acid drives the pH low enough that nothing dangerous can establish itself, and the fish keeps for years while its own enzymes work it soft and savoury. Sauerkraut and yoghurt run on the same principle. The technique came up out of the Mekong basin, where Thailand still salts and rices its own version and calls it pla som, sour fish, and it reached Japan alongside wet-field rice farming. By 718 the Yōrō Code records a pickled-fish tribute owed to the imperial court. For something close to a thousand years, sushi was how a summer catch survived until winter.

Then people started eating it. Sometime in the Muromachi period cooks began opening the barrels early, after weeks rather than a year, while the rice still had structure and a clean sour edge. Namanare was the name for it, half-fermented, and the whole history turns on that hinge: sushi stopped being a way of storing fish and became a thing you sat down to, which made the sourness a flavour to be judged rather than a by-product to be scraped off.

Sour rice can be had by waiting a year while bacteria manufacture the acid, or by pouring acid on, and it took another two centuries to take the second route. Vinegar had lately become cheap. In Handa, on Ise Bay, Nakano Matazaemon worked out how to brew it from the lees left over from sake, a red vinegar with a sweetness to it, and founded on that trade the house now called Mizkan. Rice seasoned with vinegar and salt tastes like rice that has been fermenting since last spring, and it takes about a minute. Hayazushi, fast sushi, was what they called the result. The fermentation left the dish and its name stayed behind.

Into the gap walked fast food. Hanaya Yohei opened a shop at Ryōgoku, on the Sumida, in about 1824, having spent years before that carrying sushi through the night streets in a lacquered box. Where a cook had pressed rice into a wooden frame under a weight, laid the topping across it and cut the slab into portions, Yohei took a mouthful of vinegared rice in one hand, set the fish on it and closed his palm, and the piece was finished in seconds. Nigiri is that verb, nigiru, to grip. One piece cost 8 mon and a rolled omelette 16, eggs being a luxury; the pieces ran about three times the size of a modern one, and a man had two or three standing at a stall on his way home. Edo held roughly a million people, most of its men unmarried, and almost none of them cooking.

The style is still called edomae, in front of Edo, after the bay the fish came out of, and the phrase carries the correction most people need. There was no ice. Anything sitting on a counter through a Japanese August had to be treated first, and the classical edomae repertoire is a list of treatments: gizzard shad firmed in salt and then vinegar, conger eel simmered down in its own sweet broth, prawns boiled and butterflied, tuna steeped in soy sauce and still sold that way under the name zuke. Raw fish was the exception at the stall. A reflex that hears the word sushi and pictures a slice of raw salmon is an artefact of refrigeration. Salmon carries parasites that kept Japan off it for centuries, and it entered the canon only in the late 1980s, when Norway had a glut of farmed fish and sold five thousand tons into the country behind a marketing campaign.

Ten steps, most of them about the rice. Watch how little the hands actually do to it: the rice is gathered rather than packed, turned two or three times and set down, because a piece squeezed hard enough to hold its shape on the plate is too dense to come apart in the mouth. That tension is the entire craft, and it is what the senryū above is watching.

All of which returns to the rice. An apprentice spends years on it before he is allowed near a fish, for a structural reason. Shari, the seasoned rice, is the one component the chef makes rather than buys, and the one a customer cannot inspect before it is in his mouth. The balance of vinegar, salt and sugar is his signature. The grains have to hold together on the counter and fall apart on the tongue, and the temperature is meant to sit near that of skin, so that no part of the piece arrives colder than the person eating it. Yoshiaki Shiraishi, who had watched bottles ride through an Asahi brewery, put the whole business on a conveyor belt at Higashi-Osaka in 1958, and Expo ’70 carried the idea across Japan. The belt and the expensive counter have run alongside each other since, much as the night stalls and the specialist shops did in Edo, and at either one the chef is judged on the part he made himself.
