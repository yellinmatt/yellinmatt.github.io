The Dodo

Europeans met the dodo in 1598 and lost it within a lifetime, and nearly all we know of the bird comes from a London sideshow, a few paintings and one head in Oxford.

Some time around 1638, a gentleman named Hamon L’Estrange was walking through London when he saw a painted cloth hung on a wall, the usual sign that a curiosity was on show indoors. He went in with a friend or two and found a big bird kept in a room, with a heap of pebbles at the end of the chimney that the keeper fed it one at a time. L’Estrange set the scene down in his notes on Sir Thomas Browne’s Pseudodoxia Epidemica, a catalogue of popular errors, and his note is how we know that a live dodo was ever shown in London.

The keeper’s theory about the stones was sound. Birds that live on hard fruit and seed carry grit in the gizzard to grind it, and the pebbles as big as nutmegs were doing that work. The passage also shows the trouble every later naturalist ran into. L’Estrange gives a size, a posture and two colours, and that is close to the most exact description of the living animal that any eyewitness left.

The bird had been known to Europeans for only forty years. The Dutch claimed Mauritius, an island east of Madagascar with no people on it, in 1598, and their ships’ journals began describing a heavy grey bird that could not fly. The last sighting most historians accept comes from 1662, from a crew of castaways left on the island after a storm sank their ship. Hunting gets the blame in the popular story and plays the smaller part in the evidence. The settlers brought cats, dogs, livestock and rats, which got at eggs laid on the ground, and they cleared the forest the birds lived in.

Of the animal itself, very little reached a museum. Most dodo material is bone, dug from a marsh on Mauritius from the 1860s onward. The one specimen with flesh on it came from the Tradescants, a father and son of royal gardeners who kept a cabinet of curiosities in Lambeth. Their catalogue of 1656 lists a “Dodar, from the Island Mauritius; it is not able to flie, being so big,” and the collection passed to Elias Ashmole and through him to Oxford. By the 1820s only the head and one foot were left. A story told for two centuries says the university burned the moth-eaten mount in 1755 and someone pulled the head and foot from the flames; curators now trace the fire to a misread word in the Latin records. In 2018 the museum found that the bird had been shot in the back of the head, which makes it unlikely to be the pet L’Estrange watched eating pebbles, as rumour long held.

That scrap of skin settled what the bird was. Naturalists had guessed at vultures, ostriches and albatrosses, and in 1848 Hugh Strickland and Alexander Melville, working in part from the Oxford head, argued in The Dodo and Its Kindred that it was a giant pigeon. In 2002 Beth Shapiro and her Oxford colleagues drew DNA from the same specimen and confirmed the case in the journal Science: the dodo was a pigeon, and its closest living relative is the Nicobar pigeon of South-East Asia. Its ancestors flew across the ocean to the Mascarene islands, met no predator on the forest floor, and over millions of years lost the use of their wings.

By then the dodo had a second life in a children’s book. On 4 July 1862 Charles Dodgson, a mathematics don at Christ Church, rowed up the Thames with his colleague Robinson Duckworth and the three Liddell sisters and made up a story with everyone in it as a bird: Lorina a lory, Edith an eaglet, Duckworth a duck, and Dodgson, who stammered, the Dodo. He often walked the girls round the Oxford museum, and some believe a dodo painted by Jan Savery that hangs there gave him the idea. The story came out in 1865 as Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, with John Tenniel drawing the Dodo solemnly presenting Alice with her own thimble.

“Dead as a dodo” is now the standard phrase for anything finished, and the bird stands for every species people have wiped out. The view worth holding is that its fame rests on how little of it survives. A head, a foot, some bones, a few paintings of uncertain accuracy and a gentleman’s note about pebbles are the whole record of an animal Europeans first described in Shakespeare’s lifetime. It was gone more than a century before Georges Cuvier persuaded scientists that a species could die out at all, and the cause was the rats in the ships’ holds at least as much as the sailors who hunted it.
