Respect

The most famous eight seconds in American soul are not in the song being covered, which is how a cover becomes the original.

On 14 February 1967, in Atlantic’s New York studio, Aretha Franklin sat down at a piano to record a two-year-old Otis Redding single that her producer, Jerry Wexler, had put in front of her. Her sister Carolyn was in the room, and the two of them had already spent evenings at home pulling the tempo and the phrasing apart. By the time the session ended, the record carried a passage Redding had never written and never sung. Seven letters, spelled out, over a band that drops away to bare downbeats so that nothing at all stands between the room and her voice. It runs about eight seconds, and it is the part almost anyone in the English-speaking world can produce on demand.

Redding wrote the song and cut it in Memphis for Otis Blue: Otis Redding Sings Soul, released on Volt in 1965. His record drives hard and the horns are the loudest thing in it, and the man singing wants something fairly narrow. He goes out, he earns, he hands over what he earns, and what he asks in return is to be treated properly once he is through his own front door. His band plays in D major and his vocal line stays mostly inside the major pentatonic scale, the bright five-note set that underpins a great deal of soul and country alike. He never spells the word. The single reached number thirty-five.

Franklin answers that man rather than covering him. Redding’s second verse has him granting his partner licence to be unfaithful while he is away, provided she confines it to then. Franklin’s woman states that she will not be, and the reason she offers is that she does not care to, which moves the whole matter off his permission and onto her preference. A verse later she informs him that her money is every bit as sweet as his kisses. The demand stops being a private arrangement between two people in one house and turns into something a stranger can pick up and carry out into the street.

The change most listeners feel before they can name it sits in the notes themselves. Franklin’s rhythm section plays in C major and her vocal line does not. She sings out of C minor pentatonic, leaning on a lowered third and a lowered seventh, the flattened degrees musicians call blue notes because they fall below where the major scale would put them and because they are the sound of nearly every American vernacular music from the field holler forward. Her very first note is one of them. She enters on the flattened third, which is also the highest pitch in the phrase, so the record declares its distance from Redding’s inside a single syllable. Victoria Malawey, who transcribed both performances bar by bar for the journal Popular Music, reads that as deliberate escalation, one singer answering another in the same idiom and pushing further into it than he did.

Three things in the arrangement were built in the room. Wexler hung a new bridge on the chord changes of Sam and Dave’s “When Something Is Wrong with My Baby” and handed it to King Curtis, whose tenor now fills a gap where Redding’s version had simply gone round again. Carolyn and Erma Franklin sang behind their sister, and they answer her rather than harmonise with her, so that the whole record runs on call and response. The sisters also brought in a phrase the two of them had picked up around Detroit, sock it to me, which the country took off them within a year and a television show borrowed the year after that.

That way of phrasing has a source, and it is not a studio. Their father, the Reverend C. L. Franklin, preached at New Bethel Baptist in Detroit and was recorded doing it dozens of times, and his sermons work by alternating short heavily stressed bursts with short unstressed ones while the congregation fills the gaps. Aretha’s verses are built on that same alternation, and her sisters occupy the position the congregation held. She had been singing in that building since she was a girl. The architecture of the record is the architecture of the room she learned in.

Three things to catch. The first syllable she sings is the flattened third, sitting a semitone under the major chord the band is holding, which is the whole argument of the record in one note. About ten seconds before the two-minute mark the band stops filling and marks only the downbeats while she spells the word, so those eight seconds feel like the volume went up when nothing did. Then stay to the end, where every line she sings comes straight back at her from Carolyn and Erma.

Atlantic released it on 10 April 1967 and it went to number one. Redding played the Monterey Pop Festival that June, introduced his own version from the stage by saying that “a girl took away” the song from him, called her a good friend, and laughed. He had the ownership right. Scholars who classify this sort of thing put Franklin’s version past the boundary of a cover and closer to a separate composition, on the grounds that the melody, the form, the words and the person speaking have all moved. It arrived, too, into the summer that Detroit and Newark burned and the National Organization for Women published its bill of rights, and it was heard at once as a demand rather than a request, by more constituencies than any one of them could claim.

When the Library of Congress opened the National Recording Registry in 2002, “Respect” was among the first recordings selected. The entry is hers, with Redding credited as the writer. He supplied the words and the chord changes. She supplied the thing people mean when they say the song.
