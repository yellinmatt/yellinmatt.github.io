The Match of the Century

The most famous chess match ever played opened without the challenger, and two games in he was packing to fly home.

The opening ceremony went ahead with one player missing. On 1 July 1972 the world chess championship was inaugurated at the National Theatre in Reykjavik, and Boris Spassky, the Soviet champion, was there for it. Bobby Fischer, the American challenger, was still in New York. He had cancelled his flight of 25 June because the Icelandic organisers would not add thirty per cent of the gate receipts to a prize fund of $125,000, and the first game was due the next day.

Three people rescued the match in two days. Max Euwe, the Dutch president of the world chess federation, FIDE, postponed the start and gave Fischer until noon on 4 July to appear. On 3 July a London financier named James Slater offered $125,000 of his own money, which doubled the purse. The same day Henry Kissinger, President Nixon’s national security adviser, telephoned from Washington and reportedly began, “This is the world’s worst chess player calling the world’s best chess player.” Fischer boarded an Icelandic airliner at Kennedy Airport that night. It took off at 10:04, three hours late, and put him down at Keflavik about five hours inside the deadline. Spassky then wanted an apology, a written one reached him on 6 July, and play was fixed for the eleventh.

The stakes explain the telephone call. Soviet players had held the title without a break since 1948, and the state treated it as proof of a system. Fischer was twenty-nine and had arrived by a route nobody had travelled before: he beat Mark Taimanov six games to none, did the same to Bent Larsen, and then took the former champion Tigran Petrosian apart by 6½ to 2½. His rating stood at 2785 against Spassky’s 2660. The match was set at twenty-four games, a point for a win and half for a draw, and a 12–12 tie would leave the title where it was, so the challenger needed 12½.

He began by throwing a game away. In the first, played before 2,300 people in the Laugardalshöll sports hall, the position was heading for a draw when Fischer, on his twenty-ninth move, sent his bishop to capture a pawn on the edge of the board. Spassky’s pawns closed behind it, the bishop was lost, and Fischer resigned on move 56, complaining that the film cameras had distracted him. For the second game, on 13 July, he stayed away and demanded the cameras be taken out. The arbiter, a German grandmaster named Lothar Schmid, started his clock at the appointed time and after an hour declared the game forfeit. Fischer was two points down without having played a second move, and he told the president of the Icelandic federation that he was taking the next plane home.

He stayed because of a second call from Kissinger, a flood of cablegrams, and a concession from his opponent. Spassky could have refused every demand and waited for a default. He agreed to play the third game on 16 July in a small room backstage, normally used for table tennis, with no audience at all. Fischer won it. He had never beaten Spassky before in his life. A draw followed, then another win, and after five games the score was level.

The sixth game, on 23 July, is the one players still set up on a board. Fischer had opened with his king’s pawn for nearly his whole career. This time he pushed the pawn in front of his queen’s bishop two squares, written 1.c4, and steered into the Queen’s Gambit, an old opening in which White offers that pawn in order to pull Black’s centre apart. He had never played it in a serious game. Spassky answered with the Tartakower Defence, a line in which he had never lost. By move 19 Black’s two central pawns stood side by side, looking strong, and Fischer struck at them at once.

From there the game ran one way. Fischer’s king’s pawn reached the sixth rank and split the black army in two, his rooks doubled on the file beside it, and on move 38 he gave up a rook for the knight that was holding Spassky’s king together. Three moves later the champion resigned. Then he stood up and joined the 1,500 spectators in applauding. Fischer called him “a true sportsman.”

Fischer led for the first time and was never behind again. The Soviet delegation, short of explanations, alleged that he was using chemicals and electronic devices to unsettle their man, and the Icelandic police swept the hall and found nothing conclusive. He won the thirteenth game on 10 August from a position Spassky called “very strange.” Seven draws followed. The twenty-first game was adjourned on 31 August after forty moves with Spassky lost, and the next day he telephoned his resignation instead of coming back to the board. The final score was 12½ to 8½: seven wins for Fischer, three for Spassky, one of them the forfeit, and eleven draws.

· · ·

The new champion never defended his title. When his conditions for the next match were refused, FIDE awarded it in 1975 to Anatoly Karpov of the Soviet Union, without a game being played, and the championship returned to the Soviet Union. Fischer died on 17 January 2008, aged sixty-four, in Reykjavik.
