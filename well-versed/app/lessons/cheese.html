Food · Cheese and Affinage

Cheese

One decision made in a vat, and never taken back, separates fresh goat cheese from a wheel of Parmigiano aged three years.

The village of Roquefort-sur-Soulzon sits at the foot of a collapsed limestone cliff called the Combalou, and the rubble the collapse left behind is threaded with natural chimneys the locals call fleurines. Cold damp air moves through them in every season. Someone worked out, centuries before anyone could explain why, that cheese left on shelves in that draught turned blue and turned excellent, and on 26 July 1925 the French parliament wrote the observation into statute. Roquefort became the first food in France whose name belonged to a place by law. Until then the name was custom, and custom stops nobody; dairies well outside the Aveyron made blue cheese from cow’s milk, sold it as Roquefort, and broke no rule doing so. Wine, which France is supposed to guard more jealously than anything, waited until 1936 for the same protection.

The law defends a place. The cheese itself begins in a vat. Milk is roughly seven-eighths water carrying a protein called casein, which floats in tiny clusters held apart by a fuzzy outer coat. Rennet, an enzyme drawn traditionally from the fourth stomach of a young calf, shears that coat away. The clusters find one another, knit into a soft gel across the whole vat, and take the fat and most of the water in with them. Cut that gel with a wire harp and the liquid trapped inside it, the whey, begins to run. Every cheese ever made is an argument about how much of it to let go.

Leave the whey in and you get something soft and mild and crowded with live bacteria that will finish the job within weeks, which is why fresh goat cheese is eaten within days and will not wait for you. Press the curd hard enough to squeeze the rest out, salt it, store it cool, and you get a wheel that keeps for years, which was the whole purpose before refrigeration, since a cheese is a way of carrying the milk of August into February. Parmigiano-Reggiano is that logic taken as far as it goes. Its consortium withholds the name from any wheel under twelve months, and at the twelve-month mark an inspector walks the racks tapping each one with a small hammer and listening. A true bell-like ring means the paste inside is whole. A dead thud means a crack or a hollow, and the wheel is refused the brand.

Cheesemongers sort a counter into eight families, and the sorting turns on two questions: how much water stayed in the curd, and what was permitted to grow on the outside of it.

Fresh

Barely drained, no rind, nothing growing on it, eaten young. Ricotta, which is made from the whey the other cheeses throw away; chèvre; cream cheese.

Bloomy rind

Dusted with Penicillium camemberti, a white felt that grows inward and turns the paste to cream. Brie, Camembert, Brillat-Savarin.

Washed rind

Wiped again and again with brine, beer, or spirit, which favours the bacterium Brevibacterium linens: an orange tacky rind and the smell people warn you about. Époisses, Munster, Taleggio.

Semi-soft

Pressed lightly, aged briefly, sliceable and mild. Morbier, Havarti, young tomme.

Hard

Curd cut fine and cooked, pressed hard, aged long; the alpine wheels belong here. Comté, Gruyère, Parmigiano-Reggiano, aged Cheddar.

Blue

Mould grown through the interior rather than over the surface, admitted by needles. Roquefort, Stilton, Gorgonzola.

Pasta filata

Curd heated and stretched by hand until it runs in fibres; the name means spun paste. Mozzarella, provolone, scamorza.

Processed

Cheese melted with emulsifying salts so that it will never break or separate again. American singles, Velveeta.

Roquefort sits in the sixth of those families, and the blues are the one group where the mould does its work from the inside. Penicillium roqueforti needs air to grow, so a young wheel is run through with long needles, and the veins follow the channels the needles opened. Folklore holds that there is rye bread in every wheel. There is not. The producers do raise their mould on rye dough, inoculating it with a house strain, letting it bloom and then drawing the spores back out, which is where the story came from, but no crumb of the loaf reaches the cheese. Seven houses hold the licence today, down from something near three hundred in the late nineteenth century. The milk must come from Lacaune ewes and no other breed, and every wheel must lie at least two weeks in the caves under the Combalou, breathing whatever the fleurines bring it, before anyone may sell it under the name.

Which brings all of it to the board at the end of dinner, where most people actually meet cheese. Three to five is plenty, and the way to choose them is across the families rather than across the map: something soft, something hard and aged, something blue, and three different milks if the shop allows it. Take them out an hour before you serve them, because cold cheese is mute and a Camembert straight from the refrigerator tastes of very little. Cut so that everyone gets rind and centre in the same piece, which means wedges from a round and chips off the corner of a hard wheel rather than slices that leave the last guest the rind. Work from mild to strong, and pour whatever you were already drinking.

· · ·

De Gaulle is supposed to have asked how anyone governs a country with two hundred and forty-six kinds of cheese. The remark reaches us secondhand, through a collection of his sayings published in 1962, and the number moves depending on who is telling it. The count was never the useful part. Eight families will place whatever a monger hands you, and once you can say why a rind came out orange, or why a wheel was pierced before it went into the dark, the counter stops being a wall of foreign names and becomes a set of answers to a question you already know how to ask.
