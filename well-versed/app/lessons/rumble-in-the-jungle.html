The Rumble in the Jungle

In Kinshasa in 1974 Muhammad Ali leaned on the ropes and let the hardest puncher alive wear himself out, then finished him in the eighth.

On 30 October 1974, in Kinshasa, the capital of Zaire, a stadium crowd chanted two words at a man who was supposed to lose: Ali, bomaye, “Ali, kill him.” The man in the other corner was George Foreman, the heavyweight champion of the world, twenty-five years old and unbeaten in forty fights, thirty-seven of them won by knockout.

Foreman frightened people for good reason. In January 1973 he had taken the title from Joe Frazier by stopping him in two rounds, and then he had knocked out Ken Norton, also in two. Frazier and Norton were the only two men who had ever beaten Ali. Ali was thirty-two and slower. In 1967 he had been stripped of the championship and suspended from boxing for more than three years for refusing to be drafted during the Vietnam War, and since his return he had been chasing a belt he had never lost in the ring. Don King, a promoter with more nerve than capital, had signed each fighter to a five-million-dollar contract, and Mobutu Sese Seko, the president of Zaire, agreed to stage the fight for the publicity it would bring his country. Hardly anyone outside the stadium gave Ali a chance.

What Ali did next is the reason the fight is still taught. He went to the ropes and stayed there. He leaned back, covered up, and let Foreman hit him, so that the heaviest blows landed on gloves and arms while the ropes took his weight, and in every clinch he kept talking to the man in front of him. Round after round the champion threw the punches that had finished Frazier and Norton, and round after round they were absorbed. Ali later named the tactic the rope-a-dope.

By the eighth round of a fight scheduled for fifteen, Foreman had punched himself tired. Ali came off the ropes with a final combination, a left hook and then a hard right, and Foreman went to the canvas. The referee, Zack Clayton, stepped in and the fight was over. Ali had his title back, seven years after it had been taken from him outside the ring.

Two men made the night permanent. Norman Mailer, who covered it, made a book of it, The Fight, in 1975. Leon Gast’s footage became When We Were Kings, which won the Oscar for Documentary Feature at the Academy Awards of March 1997, more than twenty years after the night it records.

Here is the claim worth carrying away. The Rumble is the cleanest example in sport of beating strength by refusing to meet it head on: Ali judged that Foreman’s power was a fuel that burned fast, gave him something harmless to spend it on, and saved his own punches for the moment the tank ran dry.

The rope-a-dope testAsk before you meet force with force
Who is stronger early?If it is the other side, your best rounds come later, so do not spend yours first
Can you absorb it safely?Ali covered up and let the ropes carry the load; know what is carrying yours
Is he spending more than you?Every miss and every blocked punch costs him; patience only works if the cost is his
Do you know the signal?Ali waited for the eighth; decide in advance what tells you the tank is empty
Do this Feel the posture, then watch the eighth

Stand with your back a foot from a wall and lean until your shoulders touch it. Tuck your elbows against your ribs, bring your fists up beside your cheekbones and drop your chin. Notice that the wall now holds your weight and your legs are resting: that is what the ropes did for Ali for most of seven rounds. Then watch the end of the fight and pick out the left hook and the right hand that finished it.

When it comes up, here is your line: Ali was the underdog in Kinshasa and won by letting the most feared puncher in the world tire himself out on his arms, which is why “rope-a-dope” still means outlasting someone who is stronger than you.
