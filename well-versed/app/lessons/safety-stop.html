The Safety Stop

Everything that makes diving dangerous happens on the way up, and the rule that governs it was settled on goats in 1908.

Two divers hang on a line at fifteen feet and do nothing for three minutes. There is nothing below them worth looking at and nothing above them they need to reach. They are waiting for a gas to leave the blood at a rate slower than it would leave on its own.

A diver breathes air, and four-fifths of air is a gas his body will not metabolise. Under pressure that nitrogen dissolves into tissue in proportion to the pressure, which is Henry’s law, and pressure underwater climbs quickly. Every thirty-three feet of seawater adds one atmosphere to the one already pressing down from the sky. A diver at thirty-three feet is therefore at two atmospheres absolute, meaning the total pressure on him with the atmosphere counted in; at ninety-nine feet he is at four. The nitrogen goes in quietly and does nothing at all while he stays down.

The trouble is the way back. Drop the pressure faster than the lungs can carry the nitrogen off and it comes out of solution where it is sitting, as bubbles in the joints, the spinal cord and the skin. The condition had a name long before anyone dived for pleasure. In 1873 some six hundred men were working the compressed-air caisson sunk for the Brooklyn Bridge, a chamber that reached 78.5 feet below the river. Andrew Smith, the physician in charge, logged a hundred and ten serious cases and fourteen deaths and called it caisson disease. The men had their own word for it, taken from the way a stricken worker stood, bent forward from the hips in the attitude of a then-fashionable posture called the Grecian bend. They shortened it to the bends. Washington Roebling took a bad case and ran the remainder of the bridge from his sickbed.

The rule that ended the guesswork came out of a physiology laboratory. The Royal Navy commissioned John Scott Haldane in 1905 to find a way of bringing divers up alive, and he worked the problem on goats, saturating them at pressures equivalent to a hundred and sixty-five feet and then decompressing them at different rates. Across thirty-six trials of a straight, uniform ascent, one animal died, two were paralysed, one showed severe general symptoms, and eleven more took the bends. What the goats told him was that the body’s tolerance is a ratio of pressures.

“it seemed probable that, if it is safe to decompress suddenly from two atmospheres of absolute pressure to one, it would be equally safe to decompress from four atmospheres absolute to two, from six atmospheres absolute to three, etc.”

The Prevention of Compressed-air Illness, The Journal of Hygiene, June 1908, p. 357

That is the two-to-one rule, and every decompression schedule since has been a refinement of it. Halving the absolute pressure is tolerable wherever in the water column you do it, which means a diver may come from a hundred feet to thirty-three in one movement and must then wait. Haldane also declined to treat the body as a single substance. He modelled it as five hypothetical compartments loading and unloading on half-times of 5, 10, 20, 40 and 75 minutes, fast tissue and slow tissue filling and emptying on separate clocks, and that five-compartment scheme stayed in service for fifty years. The method it produced was stage decompression, a series of held depths during the ascent in place of one long even drift.

Everything a recreational diver is taught descends from that paper, softened by a century of measurement. The United States Navy allowed sixty feet a minute on the ascent for many years and has since come down to thirty, which is the figure NOAA also publishes. PADI’s Open Water manual recommended a stop in 1984, and the Recreational Dive Planner carried the habit to everyone in 1988 under the slogan Slowly Ascend From Every Dive. Andrew Pilmanis had begun measuring ascent rate against bubble formation in 1974; Donna Uguccioni’s Doppler work in 1994 put numbers on the benefit, finding less gas in the blood of divers who paused. Hence fifteen feet, three minutes, on every dive.

Certification depths follow the same arithmetic from the other end. A PADI Open Water diver is trained to eighteen metres, sixty feet, where the no-decompression limit runs to fifty-six minutes; an Advanced Open Water diver to thirty metres, a hundred feet, where the same limit collapses to twenty. Forty metres, a hundred and thirty feet, is the floor of recreational diving. Those bottom times shrink because the nitrogen loads faster the deeper a diver sits.

So the thing to carry out of this is a shift in where the danger lives. Depth is a budget, drawn down at a rate the tables can tell you in advance, and the ascent is where the bill comes due. A diver at a hundred feet is in no difficulty whatever until he decides how fast to leave.
