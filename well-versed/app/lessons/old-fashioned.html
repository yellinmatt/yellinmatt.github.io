The Old Fashioned

Spirit, sugar, water and bitters: the oldest written definition of a cocktail, still poured as written.

Build

Stirred, in the glass

Glass

Old fashioned tumbler

Garnish

Orange zest

In the spring of 1806 a subscriber to The Balance, and Columbian Repository, a weekly paper in Hudson, New York, wrote in with a question. The issue of 6 May had printed the accounts of a defeated candidate for the state legislature, and under his losses sat the line “25 do. cock-tail.” The reader wanted to know “what is meant by this species of refreshment?” The editor, Harry Croswell, answered in the issue of 13 May, and his reply is the earliest account of what a cocktail is that anyone has found.

ReadThe Balance, 13 May 1806 · tap the dotted words for a gloss

“Cock tail, then, is a stimulating liquor, composed of spirits of any kind, sugar, water, and bitters … it is called sling … is supposed to be an excellent potion … a person having swallowed a glass of it, is ready to swallow any thing else.”

Editor’s reply, The Balance, and Columbian Repository, Hudson, New York, 13 May 1806. Excerpt.

Read the first clause as a recipe and it still works. Spirit gives the strength, sugar rounds it, water opens it and bitters supply the edge. The rest of the reply was a jibe at the candidate and at the voters he had been buying drinks for. Every cocktail on a modern menu descends from those four words, and the Old Fashioned is the one drink that has kept to them for two centuries, which is why it is the right place to learn what a cocktail is.

The name came later and arrived as a grumble. By the 1870s, Difford’s Guide records, bartenders were “improving” the Whiskey Cocktail with dashes of absinthe, curaçao, maraschino and Chartreuse. Drinkers who wanted none of that asked for an Old-Fashioned Whiskey Cocktail, meaning the plain one, and the request stuck as a title. The habit of drinking it shifted too. The old Whiskey Cocktail was taken quickly; the Old Fashioned, built over ice in a short heavy tumbler that now carries its name, was sipped.

The Louisville story

Sooner or later someone at the bar will tell you the drink was invented at the Pendennis Club in Louisville for Colonel James E. Pepper. The tale reached print in Old Waldorf Bar Days, a 1931 memoir of the Waldorf-Astoria’s bar, and the club credits its own bartender, Martin Cuneo, sometime between 1889 and 1895. The dates sink it. Printed references to the Old Fashioned are older than Cuneo’s drink, and the club now accepts as much, claiming only the version with fruit. Difford’s finds no evidence for that claim beyond a paper the club produced in 2009, and the 1931 recipe has no fruit in it at all.

The fruit came with Prohibition’s end. An orange slice and a maraschino cherry, sometimes a wedge of pineapple, went into the glass with the sugar and were crushed together, possibly to cover the taste of poor bootleg whiskey.

That fruit-salad version lasted in America through the disco years while Britain mostly ignored it. In London in the mid-1990s Dick Bradsell was stirring Old Fashioneds down in the glass for as long as five minutes, and as the cocktail revival crossed the Atlantic, American bars went back to the drink of the 1880s.

Make one tonight

01

The IBA spec

The International Bartenders Association lists it among “The Unforgettables.”

  • Whiskey45 ml (1½ oz) bourbon or rye
  • Sugar1 sugar cube, saturated with Angostura bitters
  • WaterA few dashes of plain water; muddle until the sugar dissolves
  • MethodFill the glass with ice, add the whiskey, stir gently
  • GarnishOrange slice or zest, and a cocktail cherry

02

The bartender’s version

Difford’s Guide swaps the cube for syrup and stirs the drink before it meets the glass.

  • Whiskey1½ oz bourbon and 1 oz 100-proof rye
  • Sugar⅓ oz demerara syrup, two parts sugar to one of water
  • Seasoning8 drops of bitters, 3 drops of saline or a pinch of salt
  • MethodStir with ice, strain over one large cube in a chilled glass
  • GarnishExpress an orange zest twist over the top

Make it both ways with the same bottle and drink them side by side. The cube version asks you to work the sugar until no grit is left at the bottom, and the ice in the glass does the diluting as you drink; the stirred version arrives already cold and watered to the bartender’s choice. Decide which you prefer, because that preference is what you will order by from now on: at a good bar, say the whiskey you want and whether you would like it stirred over a large cube.

The tool to keep is the 1806 test. Hold any drink on a menu up against spirit, sugar, water and bitters. If it has those four and nothing else of weight, it belongs to the Old Fashioned family whatever the menu calls it, and the same build will work with rum, tequila or brandy in place of the whiskey. Anything that adds citrus, vermouth or a liqueur has left Croswell’s formula behind and joined another branch of the tree.
