The Michelin Star

The most feared rating in cooking was written by a tyre company, and its three ranks still measure a restaurant by how far you should drive to reach it.

Nothing on the cover of the first edition mentions food. It is a red cloth book of about four hundred pages, stamped with a tyre and the words “Offert gracieusement aux Chauffeurs,” and in 1900 the brothers André and Édouard Michelin of Clermont-Ferrand printed 35,000 copies for a country that held fewer than 3,000 cars. Inside were town maps, instructions for changing a tyre, and lists of fuel depots, mechanics and hotels. The reasoning was a salesman’s. A motorist with somewhere to go wears out rubber, and the brothers sold rubber. André’s preface promised that the book paraît avec le siècle; il durera autant que lui, that it appeared with the century and would last as long.

For twenty years it was given away. The company’s own account of what changed is that André walked into a tyre shop, found a stack of his guides propping up a workbench, and concluded that “man only truly respects what he pays for.” The new edition of 1920 went on sale at seven francs, the paid advertisements came out, and restaurants were listed by category. A book that took no money from the places it named was free to judge them. In 1926 a single star began to mark the good tables, in 1931 the scale ran to three, and in 1933 the first three-star houses were named, among them Fernand Point’s La Pyramide at Vienne and the two restaurants of Eugénie Brazier, one in the rue Royale in Lyon and one at the Col de la Luère in the hills above it. Her six stars went unmatched until Alain Ducasse in the guide for 1998. Three years after that first list, Michelin printed what the stars meant.

Guide Michelin France, criteria as published in 1936. The present wording keeps the detour and the journey and changes “table” to “cuisine.”

Read them as a tyre company would. Each rank is a length of road: stop if you are passing, turn off your route, set out for this place and nothing else. The scale has never said anything about tablecloths, and the present rules put that in writing: “The style of a restaurant and its level of comfort or formality have no bearing on the award.” Inspectors are told to weigh five things, all of them on the plate. They are the quality of the ingredients, the harmony of the flavours, the mastery of technique, the personality of the chef as it shows in the cooking, and consistency across the whole menu and over time.

The people doing the weighing are salaried employees of Michelin, most of them former cooks and hoteliers. By the account of the Lausanne hotel school, an inspector eats some 250 restaurant meals and drives about 30,000 kilometres in a year, takes no notes at the table, and has the bill paid by the company. Several of them visit a candidate in different seasons, and the guide says its verdicts are “always made collectively.” How many inspectors there are is the one figure Michelin will not give. Pascal Rémy, dismissed after sixteen years when the company learned he was writing a book, claimed in 2004 that the French team had fallen from eleven to five.

What a star is worth can be read from what its loss has cost. Bernard Loiseau had held three at La Côte d’Or in Saulieu since 1991. Early in 2003 the rival guide Gault Millau cut him from 19 out of 20 to 17, Le Figaro reported that Michelin meant to take a star away, and on 24 February, after the lunch service, he shot himself. He was fifty-two, and he still had all three.

Paul Bocuse’s restaurant on the Saône outside Lyon kept its third star without a break from 1965, the longest run anywhere. Bocuse died on 20 January 2018. Two years later the guide’s director, Gwendal Poullennec, went to Collonges in person to say that the house “remained excellent but no longer at the level of three stars.”

Some chefs have tried to hand the thing back. In September 2017 Sébastien Bras of Le Suquet in the Aveyron, a house with three stars since his father won them in 1999, asked to be left out. “You’re inspected two or three times a year, you never know when,” he said. “Every meal that goes out could be inspected.” Michelin dropped him from the 2018 edition and put him back in 2019 with two.

The maps in the old book were good enough that in 1944 the Allies had the 1939 edition reprinted for their own use in France. The stars travelled later, to New York in 2005 and Tokyo in 2007, and several recent editions have been paid for in part by the places they cover: South Korea’s tourism organisation put up 3.2 billion won in 2016, and Thailand’s agreed the next year to 144 million baht over five years. At home the 2026 guide to France and Monaco lists 668 starred restaurants, 31 of them with three. The rank to use most often sits below all of those, the Bib Gourmand, given since 1997 for skilful cooking at a moderate price and named for Bibendum, the company’s man made of tyres. As for the stars themselves, they grade the plate alone, on the word of inspectors salaried by a firm the restaurant never pays, though nowadays a city sometimes does.
