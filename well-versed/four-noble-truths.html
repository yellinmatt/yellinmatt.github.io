The Four Noble Truths

The most quoted list in world religion sets out four pieces of work to be done, and the two English words in its name are both the wrong ones.

Seven weeks after his awakening, by the reckoning the monastic code gives, a man walked to a deer park outside Varanasi to find five ascetics who had stopped speaking to him. They had starved beside him in the forest for years, and when he abandoned the fasting and began eating again they read it as a collapse of nerve and left. He had come to tell them that the starving was the error. The discourse he gave them takes about ten minutes to read, and every living school of Buddhism, from Sri Lanka and Thailand to Tibet, China and Japan, dates itself from that afternoon.

He did not open with the four truths. He opened with a remark about method: two extremes are not to be pursued, the chasing of pleasure through the senses, which is low and common, and the practice of self-mortification, which is painful and leads nowhere. Between the two lies a middle way. Coming from a man who by tradition had spent six years reducing himself to a skeleton in front of this exact audience, the sentence works as an admission before it works as a doctrine, and it explains why the first thing the sermon names is the Noble Eightfold Path, several paragraphs before the trouble with existence comes up at all.

Then the four. Birth, ageing, illness and death are dukkha; so are sorrow, being stuck with what you dislike, losing what you like, and not getting what you want. Dukkha originates in craving, the demanding sort that keeps reaching for the next thing. Dukkha ceases when that craving is let go of completely. And the road to that cessation is the eightfold path he had already listed: right view, right resolve, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.

“There are these two extremes that are not to be indulged in by one who has . Which two? That which is devoted to sensual pleasure with reference to sensual objects: base, vulgar, common, ignoble, unprofitable; and that which is devoted to self-affliction: painful, ignoble, unprofitable. Avoiding both of these extremes, the middle way realized by the — producing vision, producing knowledge — leads to calm, to direct knowledge, to self-awakening, to .

“Now this, monks, is the noble truth of : Birth is stressful, aging is stressful, death is stressful; sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, & despair are stressful; association with the unbeloved is stressful, separation from the loved is stressful, not getting what is wanted is stressful. In short, the are stressful.

“And this, monks, is the noble truth of the origination of stress: the craving that makes for further — accompanied by passion & delight, relishing now here & now there — i.e., craving for sensual pleasure, craving for becoming, craving for non-becoming.

“And this, monks, is the noble truth of the cessation of stress: the remainderless fading & cessation, renunciation, relinquishment, release, & letting go of that very craving.”

Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (SN 56.11), translated from the Pali by Thanissaro Bhikkhu, 1993.

The part that gets dropped in transit is what comes attached to each one. Every item on the list carries an instruction, and the four instructions differ from one another. The first is to be fully understood. The second is to be abandoned. The third is to be personally experienced. The fourth is to be developed. Comprehend, abandon, realise, cultivate, one verb apiece, and the sermon runs each of them three times over: as something to be done, as something being done, as something already finished. A creed asks for assent. This one hands over four separate assignments and reports that the speaker has completed all of them.

Which is where the English name begins to come apart. Peter Harvey, who translated the sermon for Access to Insight and appended a glossary to every term in it, points out that the second item is a thing to be abandoned, and a truth is not the sort of thing anybody abandons. Craving can be given up. An accurate statement about craving cannot be, and would be no use given up. The Pali word is sacca, which does duty for both “true” and “real,” and the second item names the reality rather than the sentence about it.

K. R. Norman, whose A Philological Approach to Buddhism came out of the School of Oriental and African Studies in 1997, dispatches the other word. He calls “noble truth” “the least likely of all the possibilities” for ariya-sacca, noting that the traditional commentators put it last if they mention it at all, behind the truth of the noble one, the truth of the noble ones, and the truth that makes a person noble. Dukkha fares no better in English. Harvey observes that through the sermon the word behaves as an adjective, agreeing in number and gender with whatever it describes, so that birth is dukkha the way a road is long. Birth is not carrying out the action of suffering. “Painful” fits the grammar, and “suffering” does not.

The title of the sermon is the last clue and the best one. The Buddha says his knowledge of the four came in three phases and twelve modes, three phases for each of four items, and that he made no claim to awakening until all twelve had come clear. Thanissaro Bhikkhu points out that ancient Indian philosophers and lawyers had a name for an argument that ran two lists against each other and worked out every combination, and the name was a wheel. Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, the discourse on setting the wheel of dhamma in motion, names that grid and starts it turning. The most reproduced emblem in Buddhism began life as the shape of a completed proof.

One man in the audience got it that afternoon. What arose in Kondañña, the text says, was the “dustless, stainless Dhamma eye,” and the Buddha, hearing him, asked twice over whether he really knew. He was renamed on the spot Añña-Kondañña, Kondañña Who Knows, and the monastic community dates from that exchange. Around 250 BCE Ashoka raised a polished sandstone pillar at the same deer park, topped with four lions standing back to back on a drum carved with a twenty-four-spoke wheel. The shaft broke long ago and the capital now stands in the site museum a few hundred yards from where the sermon was given. On 22 July 1947 a country in the last weeks before its independence put that wheel in the middle of its flag.
