Physical & Self-Defense · The Three Weapons

Fencing

Two of the three modern weapons refuse to award a hit that would have killed a man, and the third was invented by fencers who wanted the duel back.

Both lamps light at once. The two fencers have hit each other inside a fifth of a second, the apparatus has registered both, and the referee raises a hand and gives the point to one of them. The other blade landed in the middle of a living man’s chest and scores nothing at all. The rule that does this is called priority, or right of way, and it governs two of the three weapons in Olympic fencing. It is a teaching device about three hundred years old, and working out why anyone would want it is most of what there is to understand about the sport.

Three weapons, three separate events, three different answers to the question of what counts. Foil thrusts, and its valid target is the trunk alone, neck to hips, front and back. Sabre cuts as well as thrusts, and its target is everything above the hips, both arms and the mask included. Épée thrusts only, and its target is the entire body, from the crown of the mask to the sole of the shoe. Foil and sabre are called conventional weapons because they carry priority. Épée carries none, and when both fencers land within forty milliseconds of each other the machine awards a point to each.

The target areas are archaeology. By the middle of the seventeenth century the rapier had given way in France to the small sword, shorter and faster and entirely capable of killing a man in a garden before breakfast. A practice bout with a buttoned small sword could still take out an eye, and the wire mask was not invented until the second half of the eighteenth century, so the masters built something safer to teach with. They took a light flexible blade, called it the foil, restricted its target to the part of the body where a thrust actually kills, and imposed a rule that an attack had to be parried or avoided before it could be answered.

For roughly two hundred years that is what a fencing master taught, and foil sat in a gentleman’s education alongside dancing and music. Malcolm Fare, who wrote the standard short history of the sport for British Fencing, records the detail that gives the game away: a pupil actually called out to fight was taken aside for a separate and hurried course in small sword play, in which the whole body was target and there was no right of way. Nobody believed the rule described a duel. It existed because two men who attack at the same moment both arrive, and a school that lets its students trade hits is a school that turns out pairs of corpses.

Some fencers wanted the other thing back. In the 1860s the French introduced the épée as a reaction against foil’s conventions, which they regarded as artificial, and masters began teaching pupils to aim at the hand and the forearm. The reason was practical. The authorities had pushed duelling toward first blood, so a nick on an opponent’s wrist now ended the affair, and the wrist was accordingly where a duellist went. Proving the hit was the difficulty, and the answer was the pointe d’arrêt, at first a single sharp spike standing two millimetres out of a cord binding, later a gentler triple point. An épée fencer could be picked out across a salle by the state of his sleeves. A later refinement dipped the tip in dye, which marked the white jacket and washed out in weak acid.

Sabre arrived from a third direction, out of military swordsmanship, where the army’s practice blades were too heavy to fence with quickly. Late in the nineteenth century an Italian master built a light sporting sabre that could be handled at a foil’s speed, Italian masters carried the method abroad, and Hungary took it up and held the weapon for most of a century. At first the whole body was target, since sabre was understood as preparation for combat. After the First World War the FIE cut the target off at the hips, which is the line a cavalryman’s horse used to cover for him.

Then came the wiring. Épée was the easiest weapon to electrify, having no invalid target and therefore nothing for a machine to tell apart, and it went electric in the 1930s; foil waited for the world championships of 1955, and sabre held out until 1986. Fare puts the consequence in a sentence: the golden rule of épée changed forever from hit without being hit to hit one twenty-fifth of a second before your opponent hits you. Forty milliseconds is now the whole distance between taking an exchange and splitting it, and épée is the one weapon whose central question is settled by a clock instead of by a referee’s reading of who meant to do what.

The thing all of this was built around lasted longer than most people assume. On 20 April 1967 Gaston Defferre, socialist deputy and mayor of Marseille, told the Gaullist deputy René Ribière to shut up, called him an abruti, and declined to apologise. Ribière sent his seconds. They met the following afternoon in a private garden at Neuilly-sur-Seine with the press watching, agreed to fight to first blood, and Defferre hit him twice in the arm before the presiding deputy stopped it, four minutes in. Ribière was married the next day. The weapon the two of them had agreed on was the épée.

Two sitting members of the National Assembly, their seconds and a row of reporters, in a suburban garden, a little over a year before the events of May 1968 filled the same city’s streets. Both of Defferre’s touches landed on the arm.

Watching a bout gets easier once you know which question is being asked. In foil and sabre the referee is reconstructing an intention from about a second of action, and the fight is over who began it, which is why those bouts are punctuated by fencers shouting and pointing at their own arms. In épée nobody argues, since the only question is which lamp came first and the box has already answered it. An épée bout looks patient for that reason and a foil bout looks like a quarrel, and the quarrel is the older of the two.
