The 2008 Wimbledon Final

The last Wimbledon final played without a roof, decided on five points out of four hundred and thirteen.

Centre Court had no roof in 2008. The men’s final was called for two o’clock on Sunday 6 July, rain held the start until about twenty-five to three, and rain took the match away twice more, once after the third set and once at two games all in the fifth. What was left of the afternoon ran out somewhere in the closing games. Rafael Nadal served out the fifth set 9–7 a little after nine o’clock and went flat on his back on grass that had been worn down to bare earth along the middle.

Roger Federer had won the previous five Championships, equalling Björn Borg, and had not lost a match on grass in sixty-five attempts since June 2003 or at Wimbledon in forty. A sixth consecutive title would have been the first since William Renshaw in 1886. Nadal had taken his fourth straight French Open a month earlier and was after the Channel Slam, the Paris-to-London double that only Rod Laver in 1969 and Borg between 1978 and 1980 had managed in the Open era. The two had met in this same final in 2006 and in 2007, and Federer had won both.

Their rivalry turned on a geometric fact. Nadal is left-handed, and he hits his forehand with enough topspin to make a hard ball drop early and then kick upward off the bounce. Struck cross-court from his forehand side, it arrives on Federer’s backhand, which Federer takes with one hand. A one-handed backhand is at its strongest in a narrow band around the waist; a ball rising past the shoulder has to be met with the arm stretched away from the body and nothing on the other side of the grip to hold the face steady. Clay raises the bounce and slows it, which is why the French Open had been going one way for three years running. Grass had been Federer’s insurance, because a low skidding ball stays inside the window the one-hander wants.

By 2008 that margin had closed. Nadal had rebuilt his serve and was taking returns from much closer in, and on the day he landed 73 percent of his first serves and won 71 percent of the thirty-one points he came forward on. Federer hit 319 backhand groundstrokes over the course of the match, and four of them were winners. In rallies that ran to seven shots or longer, Nadal took 55 percent. Federer’s counter was to stop the rallies from happening at all: twenty-five aces, eighty-nine winners, seventy-five trips to the net.

The plan very nearly worked, and the scoreline is where the lesson sits. Federer hit twenty-nine more clean winners and nineteen more aces than the man who beat him. Tennis keeps no running total. A point taken at forty-love counts the same as a point taken at break point and is then thrown away, every game restarts at love and every set restarts at nil. The scoring asks when a player was good. Nadal won 209 points in all and Federer won 204, and the five that separated them fell in the right places. Federer reached thirteen break points and converted one. Nadal reached thirteen and converted four.

Two sets down, Federer took the third on a tiebreak and the fourth on one of the finest tiebreaks anyone has played. Nadal led it 5–2 with two serves to come, double-faulted, then netted a backhand. At seven-all he hit a forehand down the line to reach championship point, and Federer answered the next ball with a backhand down the line, at which Andrew Castle told the BBC audience that “the two best passing shots of the tournament, without doubt, have just taken place.” Federer took the tiebreak 10–8. The whole match ran four hours and forty-eight minutes of playing time inside close to seven hours of elapsed time, and stood as the longest final in Wimbledon’s history until Federer and Novak Djokovic went nine minutes longer in 2019.

Watch Extended highlights from the All England Club

Watch the height of the ball at the moment Federer’s backhand meets it, especially in the longer rallies, and watch how far inside the baseline Nadal is standing to receive. Then watch the fourth-set tiebreak from 5–2 to the end, which is the passage the commentary box could not keep up with.

Forty-three days later Nadal took the world number one ranking, ending a run of 237 consecutive weeks that nobody has approached since. Federer reached the finals of the next six Grand Slams and won four of them. Jon Wertheim wrote a book about the match and called it Strokes of Genius. A retractable roof was in place over Centre Court for the following summer, which leaves 6 July 2008 as the last Wimbledon final to be played against the weather, and the one John McEnroe and a long row of writers have called the best ever played.
