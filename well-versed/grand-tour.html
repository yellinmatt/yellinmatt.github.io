The Grand Tour

For two centuries a rich young Briton finished his education on a two-year road to Rome, and the journey paid out most famously on one October evening in 1764.

Johan Zoffany spent five years in Florence working for Queen Charlotte, and the picture he brought home shows a room so packed with masterpieces that the visitors have to stand among the statues. This is the Tribuna of the Uffizi, the octagonal hall the Medici built for the best things they owned. The men gathered in knots around the marbles are English travellers who happened to be in the city, and at the centre of the canvas an attendant tilts a reclining Titian Venus toward a group of them as a dealer might at a private view. The Royal Collection now calls the painting the iconic image of the English Grand Tour. Horace Walpole found the crowd of “travelling boys” absurd, and the Queen, he reported, would not have it hung in any of her apartments.

The custom Zoffany caught ran for close to three centuries from the late 1500s, and its name came from a priest. Richard Lassels, an English Catholic who earned his living escorting young noblemen through Italy, used the phrase in The Voyage of Italy, published in Paris and London in 1670, after his death. The traveller was typically between seventeen and twenty-two and usually an elder son, since only an heir’s allowance could carry two or three years on the road. He went with a tutor, ideally a prudent clergyman, whom the century’s wits called his bear-leader. Paris came first and could not be skipped; Italy was compulsory, and inside Italy everything pointed at Rome.

Edward Gibbon made the journey in 1764 and wrote it up a quarter of a century later with the dry exactness of a man who distrusted his own feelings. He was twenty-seven, a good deal older than the usual tourist, and he had spent most of the previous year at Lausanne reading up the antiquities of Italy before he set out. He was carried over Mont Cenis on a light wicker seat by Alpine chairmen, and he takes the trouble to record that he crossed without an elephant. At Florence he sat silent at the table of Sir Horace Mann, the British envoy, whose real business, as Gibbon saw it, was giving dinners to the English. He reached Rome at the beginning of October.

My temper is not very susceptible of enthusiasm; and the enthusiasm which I do not feel, I have ever scorned to affect. But, at the distance of twenty-five years, I can neither forget nor express the strong emotions which agitated my mind as I first approached and entered the eternal city. After a sleepless night, I trod, with a lofty step, the ruins of the Forum; each memorable spot where Romulus stood, or spoke, or Caesar fell, was at once present to my eye; and several days of intoxication were lost or enjoyed before I could descend to a cool and minute investigation.

His guide was James Byres, a Scottish antiquary who led visitors through the ruins and collections for a fee, and Gibbon kept at it for eighteen weeks before borrowing six more for Naples, where the new envoy, Sir William Hamilton, presented him to the boy-king. A course with Byres usually ran about six weeks. In the 1770s it cost roughly what Pompeo Batoni asked for a half-length portrait, twenty guineas, and the portrait was the other thing a young man carried home from Rome as evidence that he had been. Batoni made some 175 pictures for British clients, and he sold it in parts: the Colosseum or the temple at Tivoli through the window, a piece of antique sculpture at the elbow, each added for an extra charge.

Sir Gregory Page-Turner sat to him in 1768, aged twenty and newly heir to estates worth about £24,000 a year. He takes the stance of the Apollo Belvedere, the Vatican’s most celebrated antique, with one hand on a well-used map of Rome and the Colosseum over his shoulder. The helmeted Minerva beside him was a studio property that appears in Batoni’s portraits of other Englishmen, which says plainly what the canvas was for. It certified the trip.

Whatever else could be packed went home as well. Joseph Smith, the British consul in Venice from 1744, sold Canaletto’s views of the city to visitors who wanted the Grand Canal over the fireplace, and George III bought Smith’s collection whole in 1762, which is why the Royal Collection holds so many Canalettos. Piranesi’s etchings of the ruins filled portfolios, and the third Duke of Beaufort shipped a Roman marble sarcophagus to Badminton in Gloucestershire. Some freight never landed. In 1778 the French seized a shipload of tourists’ purchases off Livorno, and Batoni’s portrait of Francis Basset, which was aboard, hangs today in the Prado.

The custom had its critics, and the sharpest published in 1776. Adam Smith granted in The Wealth of Nations that a boy who leaves at seventeen and comes back at twenty-one is bound to have improved somewhat in four years, then added that “he commonly returns home more conceited, more unprincipled, more dissipated, and more incapable of any serious application, either to study or to business, than he could well have become in so short a time had he lived at home.” Smith wrote from inside the trade. In 1764, the year Gibbon reached Rome, he left his Glasgow professorship to take the young Duke of Buccleuch abroad as tutor, by way of Toulouse and Geneva, where he met Voltaire, to nine months in Paris among the economists Quesnay and Turgot. The death of the duke’s younger brother in Paris ended the journey in 1766, and the pension the duke settled on him paid for the years in which he wrote the attack.

War shut the road. The French Revolutionary Wars began in 1792, French armies overran Italy between 1796 and 1798, and when the British returned after 1815 the railway was coming to shrink the old two years into a few weeks. The country houses kept what the tourists had sent back, and the taste those young men formed in Rome still shapes collections across Britain, the National Gallery’s among them.

The tour’s largest return came from the traveller who had prepared hardest. Gibbon’s first volume appeared in 1776, within a month of Smith’s book, and he wrote the last lines of the last page at Lausanne, in the summer-house in his garden, between eleven and midnight on 27 June 1787, nearly twenty-three years after the friars’ vespers. He had reached Rome a decade older than Smith’s boys with a year of reading behind him, and that remains the useful advice for anyone planning a first trip to Italy: do the reading before you book, so that the Forum is a place you recognise when you finally stand in it.
