The theft of 1911, and the Leonardo that came back a star

The Mona Lisa

A house painter walked out of the Louvre with it under his smock, and the twenty-eight months it spent in a trunk turned an admired Leonardo into the most famous painting on earth.

On the morning of Tuesday 22 August 1911 the painter Louis Béroud walked into the Salon Carré of the Louvre to work on a picture of the Mona Lisa and found four iron pegs in a bare stretch of wall. Béroud made his living painting the galleries themselves, and that year he had painted this wall with Leonardo’s portrait in its gilt frame between two larger canvases. A guard told him the panel was probably with the museum’s photographers. Nobody hurried to check. By the time the Louvre admitted the loss, late that day, the painting had been gone for about twenty-six hours.

What had vanished was a celebrated picture that was not yet the world’s favourite. Leonardo began it in Florence around 1503; in October of that year a Florentine named Agostino Vespucci noted in the margin of his copy of Cicero that Leonardo was at work on a portrait of Lisa del Giocondo, wife of the cloth merchant Francesco. The sitter’s husband never received it. The poplar panel, 77 by 53 centimetres, went with Leonardo to France, and the Louvre dates it from 1503 to 1519, the year he died there. Francis I acquired it for the French crown, Napoleon kept it for a spell in his bedroom at the Tuileries, and from 1797 it hung in the Louvre, admired by connoisseurs and far from the most visited object in the building. Baedeker’s guide to Paris gave it a paragraph in 1878 and two sentences in 1907.

The connoisseurs had their reasons. Florentine portraits of the generation before tended to set a sitter in stiff profile, like a head on a medal. Leonardo turned Lisa toward us in three-quarter view, brought her folded hands into the picture, and seated her in an open loggia, the bases of two columns just visible at either edge, with roads and water dissolving into the distance behind her. Over all of it he worked the technique Italians call sfumato, from their word for smoke: contours softened by thin layers of paint until forms rise out of shadow with no drawn line to hold them. He pushed it hardest at the corners of the mouth and eyes, where expression lives, and left them unsettled, so that the smile reads as warm in one glance and guarded in the next.

Leonardo undertook to execute, for Francesco del Giocondo, the portrait of Lisa, his wife; and after toiling over it for four years, he left it unfinished; and the work is now in the collection of King Francis of France, at Fontainebleau. In this head, whoever wished to see how closely art could imitate nature, was able to comprehend it with ease; for in it were all the that with subtlety are able to be painted, seeing that the eyes had that lustre and watery sheen which are always seen in life, and around them were all those rosy and pearly tints, as well as the lashes, which cannot be represented without the greatest subtlety. The eyebrows, through his having shown the manner in which the hairs spring from the flesh, here more close and here more scanty, and curve according to the pores of the skin, could not be more natural. The nose, with its beautiful nostrils, rosy and tender, appeared to be alive. The mouth, with its opening, and with its ends united by the red of the lips to the flesh-tints of the face, seemed, in truth, to be not colours but flesh. In the pit of the throat, if one gazed upon it intently, could be seen the beating of the pulse. And, indeed, it may be said that it was painted in such a manner as to make every , be he who he may, tremble and lose heart. He made use, also, of this device: Monna Lisa being very beautiful, he always employed, while he was painting her portrait, persons to play or sing, and jesters, who might make her remain merry, in order to take away that melancholy which painters are often to give to the portraits that they paint. And in this work of Leonardo’s there was a smile so pleasing, that it was a thing more divine than human to behold; and it was held to be something marvellous, since the reality was not more alive.

Giorgio Vasari, Lives of the Most Eminent Painters, Sculptors and Architects, “Leonardo da Vinci,” trans. Gaston du C. de Vere, vol. 4 (1912), via Project Gutenberg

Vasari published that in 1550, three decades after the panel had left Italy, and his loving account of the eyebrows suggests he was writing from report: the woman in the Louvre has none that anyone can make out. Whether Leonardo never painted them or a later cleaning took them off, as one technical study argued in 2007, is still disputed.

The theft itself was almost casual. On Monday 21 August, the day the Louvre shut to visitors, an Italian house painter named Vincenzo Peruggia came out of a narrow storeroom near the Salon Carré where he had spent the night. He knew the building because in 1910 he had helped fit its paintings with protective glass. Wearing a white workman’s smock, he lifted the panel off its pegs, took it out of its frame and glass case, hid the wood under his clothes, and left by a back stairwell and a side entrance. The museum at the time had more than a thousand rooms and fewer than 150 guards for some 250,000 objects.

Then the newspapers took over. The Louvre stayed closed for a week, and when it reopened people queued to look at the empty wall; Franz Kafka and Max Brod were among them, and they also caught a vaudeville sketch about the theft. Postcards, cabaret songs and a short film carried the face to people who had never stood in front of it. The police, meanwhile, arrested the poet Guillaume Apollinaire in September and held him for a week, then questioned his friend Pablo Picasso, because both had known a different Louvre thief. Both were cleared.

Detectives had already talked to the right man. They interviewed Peruggia in November 1911 with the rest of the Louvre’s workers, and he told them he had read about the theft in the papers and overslept that Monday. He had left a thumbprint on the glass, and his prints sat in police files from earlier arrests, yet no match was made, because his name was missed off the list checked against those records. The painting spent the next twenty-eight months in the false bottom of a wooden trunk in his room.

In November 1913 a Florentine dealer, Alfredo Geri, received a letter signed “Leonardo V.” offering to return the Mona Lisa to Italy for 500,000 lire. Geri brought in Giovanni Poggi, director of the Uffizi. Peruggia came to Geri’s shop on 11 December and took the two men to his hotel, where Poggi confirmed the panel was genuine, and the police arrested him there on the 12th. His defence was patriotism: he believed Napoleon had looted the picture and expected to be thanked for bringing it home. Leonardo had in fact carried it to France himself. A Florence court in June 1914 gave Peruggia a year and fifteen days, cut on appeal to seven months.

Italy gave the painting a farewell tour first, to the Uffizi, Rome and Milan; in Florence, by one New York Times report, a crowd of 30,000 broke past the police to see it. It was back in the Louvre on 4 January 1914, and more than 100,000 people came to look at it in its first two days on the wall.

Everything since has compounded. Duchamp drew a moustache on a postcard of her and Warhol printed her in rows; an estimated 1.7 million people queued for her in New York during the American loan of 1962–63; about ten million a year now stand at a rail in front of her, and in 2024 the Louvre decided she should get a room of her own. The museum is still learning the lesson of 1911. On 19 October 2025, a year ago tomorrow, four thieves dressed as workmen rode a furniture lift to a balcony of the Galerie d’Apollon, cut through a window in under eight minutes, dropped the crown of Empress Eugénie on the way out and escaped with eight other pieces of the French crown jewels, still missing as of June 2026.

Peruggia made the Mona Lisa a household face, and Leonardo had made it worth the queue long before: the turned shoulders, the folded hands, the haze at the corners of the mouth, and the sixteen years the Louvre allows him on one small panel. The fair verdict is that the theft explains the size of the crowd and the painting explains why, a century later, the crowd has not drifted away.
