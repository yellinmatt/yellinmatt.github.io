The Humidor

The box is lined with a tree that is not a cedar, held at a number most makers have quietly come down from, and defended against a beetle that the usual rule of thumb only slows down.

A new humidor will not hold a cigar. The lining arrives as dry lumber, and dry wood pulls water out of anything resting against it, so the first job is to give the wood its water back before a cigar goes near it. Shanken’s Cigar Handbook sets out the method plainly: wipe the interior down with distilled water on an unscented sponge, leave a damp sponge inside sitting on a piece of plastic so it never touches the wood, charge the humidification device, and shut the lid overnight. By morning the walls have drawn what they needed, and the box will hold a number instead of stealing one.

The wood doing that drawing is Cedrela odorata, a Neotropical tree of the family Meliaceae, which places it among the mahoganies. Its heartwood carries an aromatic resin that repels insects and smells enough like the true cedars of the genus Cedrus, to which it has no relation, to have borrowed their name. It grows from the Pacific coast of Mexico down to roughly 28 degrees south in Argentina, at altitudes up to 1,200 metres. The handbook asks specifically for a lining in “a fairly nonaromatic cedar, such as Spanish cedar,” because the wood has to trade moisture with the tobacco without seasoning it.

That supply is now regulated. On 28 August 2020 all seventeen American species of Cedrela entered Appendix II of CITES, which brings logs, sawn wood, veneer sheets and plywood under permit for international trade throughout the tree’s range from Mexico to Argentina. The lining of a humidor bought today is a controlled timber, which is a strange thing to learn about the inside of a box most people never think about twice.

Then the number. Seventy percent humidity at seventy degrees is the phrase every new smoker hears, and the handbook sits close to it, asking for 70 to 75 percent relative humidity at 68 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit. The makers have moved since. La Aurora, the oldest cigar maker in the Dominican Republic, publishes a working range of 65 to 69 percent at 65 to 70 degrees and says outright that holding steady matters more than hitting any particular figure, because each swing works the leaf.

What that range protects is combustion. Tobacco kept too dry burns faster and hotter and turns bitter as the oils go out of it, and the wrapper grows brittle enough to split under a thumb. Tobacco kept too wet draws tight, burns slowly and crookedly, and muddies whatever the blender put in; past roughly 72 percent La Aurora starts warning about mould.

The thermometer answers a different problem. Lasioderma serricorne, the cigarette beetle, is a reddish-brown oval two to three millimetres long that will happily work through flour, dates, coffee beans, spices and dog food, and appears to prefer tobacco to all of them. The handbook’s rule of thumb is that trouble shows up when a humidor holds above 75 degrees for more than twenty-four hours, and that is a threshold for an outbreak rather than for the insect. A female lays ten to a hundred eggs directly into the food she has found, larvae emerge within six to ten days, and the University of Florida puts the full cycle at forty to ninety days with five or six overlapping generations a year in warm climates. Minnesota’s IPM textbook has it running in as little as nineteen days when conditions suit. Cool storage slows that arithmetic. It does not stop it, which is why the remedy for pinholes is forty-eight hours in the freezer and a day in the refrigerator before the cigars go back.

WatchKirby Allison, How To Season a Humidor

The seasoning is the one step people skip and the one that decides whether the box ever settles. Watch what he does with the distilled water: the cedar is wiped rather than soaked, because standing water raises the grain and warps a panel, and the humidification device does the rest of the work over days instead of minutes. Watch too where the hygrometer sits, and notice that the reading he trusts is the one that has stopped moving.

Set against all of that, the box is doing something slow. The cedar holds the reservoir, the humidification device tops it up, the cigars take and give back a little every day, and nothing in the system moves quickly enough to be read off one morning’s glance. Which is why a hygrometer matters less for what it says today than for whether it has said the same thing all month. Buy one you can calibrate, pick a figure in the sixties, and leave the wood to do the work.
