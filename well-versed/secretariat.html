Secretariat

In five weeks of 1973 a chestnut colt set three track records that still stand, and he set the last one alone, with the rest of the field out of the picture.

On the far turn at Belmont Park on 9 June 1973, the CBS announcer Chic Anderson stopped calling a horse race and started calling one horse. “Secretariat is widening now,” he told an audience estimated at fifteen million. “He is moving like a tremendous machine.” Four other horses were still running behind him, far enough back that the call had stopped mentioning them at all.

What was being settled that afternoon has no real equivalent elsewhere in American sport. The Triple Crown asks a three-year-old to win the Kentucky Derby over a mile and a quarter at Churchill Downs on the first Saturday in May, the Preakness Stakes over a mile and three sixteenths at Pimlico two weeks later, and the Belmont Stakes over a mile and a half three weeks after that. The distances jump around, the rest never comes, and a horse is three years old exactly once, so the attempt is unrepeatable by definition. Since Sir Barton first swept the series in 1919, thirteen horses have managed it. When Secretariat walked into the Belmont paddock, twenty-five years had passed since Citation, long enough that good horsemen had begun to describe the thing as a relic.

He was foaled at the Meadow near Doswell, Virginia, on 30 March 1970, by the champion sire Bold Ruler out of a mare named Somethingroyal, and he belonged to Penny Chenery’s stable because of a coin toss. Meadow Stud sent its mares to Bold Ruler under an arrangement with the Phipps family that divided the resulting foals by flip, and at Saratoga in 1969 Meadow won the toss for a foal not yet conceived. Chenery’s father died in January 1973 and the estate taxes very nearly forced her to sell the colt; Seth Hancock of Claiborne Farm syndicated him instead, thirty-two breeding shares at $190,000 apiece for a world-record $6.08 million, paid on a horse who had not yet run a race at three. Charles Hatton of the Daily Racing Form, who had been appraising Thoroughbreds since the 1920s, put the general feeling plainly after watching him at Saratoga: “You carry an ideal around in your head, and boy, I thought, ‘This is it.’ I never saw perfection before.”

The Derby came first, on 5 May, before a crowd of 134,476. Secretariat left the gate last of thirteen, picked the field off around the turns, ran down the front-running Sham in the stretch and won by two and a half lengths in 1:59⅖, which remains the race record fifty-three years later. Under that number sits the one worth carrying away. His quarter-mile splits were :25⅕, :24, :23⅘, :23⅖ and :23. He ran every quarter of a mile-and-a-quarter race faster than the quarter before it, which is not how animals or engines are built to distribute an effort. The veteran racetrackers at Churchill Downs that afternoon could not name another horse who had done it over the distance.

At Pimlico two weeks later he did something no rider is taught to do, sprinting through the tight first turn from last place to the lead, and then coasted home by two and a half again. The electric timer failed. Pimlico announced 1:55, corrected itself two days on to 1:54⅖, and stood by that while two Daily Racing Form clockers insisted their stopwatches had read 1:53⅖. The argument outlasted almost everyone who started it. In June 2012, after more than two hours of testimony built on frame-by-frame video analysis, the Maryland Racing Commission voted seven to nothing to make the official time 1:53, handing over a track record thirty-nine years late.

Only four horses were entered against him at Belmont, which tells you what the sport thought was going to happen, and it still underestimated him. Ron Turcotte sent him straight to the front. He and Sham cut a half mile in :46⅕, a suicidal pace over twelve furlongs, and Sham was finished by the far turn. Secretariat kept going: six furlongs in 1:09⅘, the fastest ever run in the race; a mile in 1:34⅕; a mile and a quarter in 1:59 flat, meaning he passed the Derby distance faster than he had run the Derby, with two furlongs still in front of him. The margin opened to ten lengths, then twenty, then twenty-eight. He crossed the wire thirty-one lengths clear of Twice a Prince in 2:24, taking two and three fifths seconds off the stakes record and running the fastest mile and a half ever recorded on dirt. Taking a length at the customary eight feet, the gap at the finish ran to something near eighty yards. Braulio Baeza, riding the second horse, offered the line of the day afterward: “I won the race, didn’t I? After all, I was ahead of every horse I could see.” Andrew Beyer, whose speed figures later became the sport’s common currency, went back and scored the run at 139, the highest number he has ever assigned. Of the winning tickets, 5,427 were never cashed. People kept them.

WatchCBS, 9 June 1973

The whole broadcast, Anderson’s call included. Watch the camera rather than the horse. Around the far turn the operator has to choose between Secretariat and the field, and once he chooses there is no shot in television that can put them back together. Note also how little Turcotte does; the hand ride down the lane is the tell that nothing was being asked for.

He was euthanized at Claiborne Farm on 4 October 1989, aged nineteen, after a month of laminitis, a hoof disease that leaves a horse in a pain it cannot be walked out of. Dr. Thomas Swerczek of the University of Kentucky performed the necropsy and found every organ ordinary except one. An average Thoroughbred heart weighs about nine pounds. This one he judged to be roughly twice that, and a third larger than any equine heart he had ever handled, with no disease in it at all, chambers and valves entirely normal. He did not put it on a scale, so the celebrated figure of twenty-two pounds has always been an estimate and should be quoted as one. The mechanism it points to is simple enough: a larger pump moves more blood, more blood carries more oxygen, and a horse still delivering oxygen in the last quarter of a mile and a half is a horse that does not have to slow down. Everyone else in the Belmont did.

The country took him up for reasons that had little to do with racing. In the week after the Preakness he appeared on the covers of Time, Newsweek and Sports Illustrated at once, and William Nack, who followed him all spring for Newsday, later wrote that the colt “suddenly transcended being a racehorse and became a cultural phenomenon, a sort of undeclared national holiday from the tortures of Watergate and the Vietnam War.” Fifty-three years on, all three of his records are intact, and for the moment the greatest of them cannot even be challenged. Belmont Park has been shut since 2024 for a rebuild costing close to half a billion dollars, so the Belmont Stakes has been run at Saratoga over a mile and a quarter for three years running, and it returns to its own track and its own twelve furlongs in 2027. Whatever a horse does that afternoon will be timed against a clock that stopped in 1973.
