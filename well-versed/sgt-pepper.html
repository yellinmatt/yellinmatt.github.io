Sgt. Pepper

The Beatles gave up the stage in August 1966 and spent the winter making a record that four men could never have played live, ending on a chord that took three pianos and a harmonium.

At 9.27 on the evening of 29 August 1966 the Beatles walked onto a stage in Candlestick Park, San Francisco, and played eleven songs to a stadium that was barely half full. Twenty-five thousand tickets had sold for a ground that held 42,500. The summer had gone badly. Lennon’s remark that the band was more popular than Jesus had set off record burnings across the American South, a firecracker in Memphis had made them think one of them had been shot, and the stadium sound systems of the day could not carry what they were now recording, so the crowd screamed over a band it could not hear. On the flight out, George Harrison is supposed to have said, “That’s it, then. I’m not a Beatle anymore.” Nobody announced it, but they never played another paying concert.

What they did instead began on 24 November 1966 in Studio Two at EMI’s studios on Abbey Road in St John’s Wood. The first song was Lennon’s “Strawberry Fields Forever,” and it swallowed some fifty-five hours of studio time, more than they had once needed for an entire album. With no tour to rehearse for, there was no reason to stop, and the engineer Geoff Emerick later reckoned that the finished record took about seven hundred hours. It cost around £25,000, against roughly £400 for their first LP in 1963. All of it was made on four-track tape. Eight-track machines were not yet running in London’s commercial studios, so George Martin, the producer, and his engineers filled four tracks, mixed them down onto one track of a second machine, and filled the three that were left, which is how a single song came to carry dozens of overdubs.

The record lost its two best songs before it had a shape. EMI and Brian Epstein, the band’s manager, wanted a single, and in February 1967 “Strawberry Fields Forever” and McCartney’s “Penny Lane” went out back to back. Singles were kept off Beatles albums as a courtesy to fans who had bought them, so both were gone. Martin later called it the biggest mistake of his professional life. What filled the hole was a conceit. Flying home from Kenya the previous November, McCartney had played with the idea of an Edwardian brass band with a ridiculous name, the roadie Mal Evans supplied the name, and that February McCartney proposed that the whole album be presented as a show by this invented group. A band that had stopped performing would release a record pretending to be a performance.

The song that closes it was built from two halves. Lennon wrote his verses with the Daily Mail open in front of him, taking in a paragraph about a twenty-one-year-old heir, a friend of McCartney’s, killed in a car crash, and another about four thousand holes counted in the roads of Blackburn, Lancashire. McCartney had a brisk fragment about waking up and catching a bus. Between the two they left twenty-four empty bars, with Evans counting them aloud into a microphone and setting off an alarm clock to mark the end. The alarm could not be removed from the tape, so it stayed, and it now rings at the exact moment McCartney’s section wakes up.

The empty bars were filled on the evening of 10 February 1967 in Studio One, the big orchestral room. Forty musicians were booked for a total of £367 and ten shillings, and McCartney asked them to come in evening dress. Martin’s score gave each instrument a single instruction: start on its lowest note, rise across the twenty-four bars to its highest note near a chord of E major, and pay no attention to anyone else. “It’s every man for himself,” he told them. They played the passage several times, the takes were stacked on top of one another until forty players sounded closer to two hundred, and the engineer Ken Townsend made it possible by running two four-track machines in sync. When the first pass ended, the orchestra applauded itself.

Twelve days later, on 22 February, the song got its ending. Lennon, McCartney, Starr and Evans sat at three pianos, Martin took a harmonium, and on a count all five struck the same E major chord as hard as they could. The engineers then raised the recording level as the sound decayed, so that the chord rings for more than forty seconds and the hum of the room comes up underneath it. No touring band of 1967 could have produced either the orchestral swell or that ending in a concert hall, and the Beatles no longer had to.

Listen “A Day in the Life,” official video

Hear the seam. At the close of Lennon’s opening section the forty players begin their slow, unsynchronised ascent; listen for single instruments arriving at the top early and the whole mass converging on one chord. The alarm clock follows at once, and McCartney’s brighter, faster section starts as if a different record had been dropped in. The orchestra rises a second time before the end, and then comes the piano chord: keep the volume up for the full forty seconds, and the fading tail will give way to the hiss of the room. The BBC banned “A Day in the Life” from its radio stations before the album was out, on the grounds that one line seemed to encourage drug-taking. It hardly mattered. Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band was officially released on Thursday 1 June 1967, spent twenty-seven weeks at number one in Britain and fifteen in America, and became the first rock album to win the Grammy for Album of the Year. It was also the first Beatles LP issued with the same tracks on both sides of the Atlantic. Kenneth Tynan, the theatre critic, wrote in The Times that it was “a decisive moment in the history of Western civilization.” Three days after release, Jimi Hendrix opened his show at the Saville Theatre in London with the title song, with McCartney and Harrison in the audience. “It’s a pretty major compliment in anyone’s book,” McCartney said.

Here is the opinion worth holding at the table. As a set of songs, many who know the catalogue rate Revolver, from the summer before, above it, and the fictional-band idea fades after the second track. The case for Sgt. Pepper rests on what it made normal: an album conceived as a whole, recorded over months by a group that had no plans to play it live, and treated as the work itself. By 2018 it was the best-selling studio album in British history, and every band that has disappeared into a studio for a year since has been following the trail it cut from Candlestick Park to Abbey Road.
