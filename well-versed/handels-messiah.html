Handel’s Messiah

The year’s most familiar Christmas music was written in twenty-four days, first sung in Dublin in Holy Week, and paid to get debtors out of jail.

The page is headed Chorus allegro in Handel’s quick, slanting hand, and the first things he set down were the loud ones: trumpets and timpani on the top staves, then the strings, then four choral lines that all come in on the same word, written out under the notes as Hallelujah. He reached this page near the end of the second of the oratorio’s three parts, in a score he began in London on 22 August 1741 and finished on 14 September. That is twenty-four days for a work that runs well over two hours. The piece most of us now hear in December was made for spring, launched in Ireland as a charity benefit, and kept alive in London by a home for abandoned children, and once you know that history the music stops sounding like seasonal background.

The words came first, and they were not Handel’s. Charles Jennens, a wealthy Anglican landowner from the Midlands, assembled the libretto entirely from scripture, the King James Bible and the psalms of the Book of Common Prayer, and wrote to a friend in July 1741 that “the Subject is Messiah.” An oratorio is a sacred drama sung in a concert hall, without costumes or staging, and this one barely tells a story at all. Part One covers prophecy and the birth of Christ; Part Two the Passion and its aftermath, closing on the Hallelujah; Part Three the resurrection and the end of time. Jennens thought the composer had rushed it, and years later he was still grumbling that only “with great difficulty” had he got Handel to correct its “grosser faults.”

Worse, from Jennens’s side, Handel took the new score to Ireland. He landed in Dublin on 18 November 1741, probably at the invitation of the Duke of Devonshire, the Lord Lieutenant, gave a winter season of concerts, and then offered Messiah as a benefit for three causes: the relief of prisoners held for debt, Mercer’s Hospital in Stephen’s Street, and the Charitable Infirmary on the Inns Quay. The venue was the new Musick Hall in Fishamble Street, built to seat about six hundred. The organisers wanted more people than that through the door, and the Dublin papers printed their plan.

The plan worked. About seven hundred people packed into the hall on Tuesday 13 April 1742, five days before Easter, and the takings came to around £400, divided among the three causes; the prisoners’ share is credited with freeing 142 debtors. The choir was drawn from both of Dublin’s cathedrals, St Patrick’s and Christ Church, sixteen men and sixteen boys, after a tussle with the dean of St Patrick’s, Jonathan Swift, who granted his singers leave and then withdrew it. The alto was Susannah Cibber, an actress who had left the London stage under a cloud of scandal, and when she sang “He was despised” the Reverend Patrick Delany is said to have risen and cried, “Woman, for this be all thy sins forgiven thee!” Treat that story as legend; the review in the papers is the evidence that survives.

London was colder. At Covent Garden on 23 March 1743 Handel advertised the work only as “a New Sacred Oratorio,” keeping the holy title off playbills, because scripture sung in a theatre offended the devout; one correspondent doubted that “the Playhouse is a fit Temple to perform it.” This is the season of the most repeated story about the piece, that George II rose to his feet at the Hallelujah and the whole house rose with him, which is why audiences stand for it still. There is no convincing evidence the king was there, and the earliest written mention of anyone standing comes in a letter of 1756. The custom is old and genuine. The royal origin was attached to it afterwards.

Charity rescued the piece a second time. From 1750 it was sung every spring for the Foundling Hospital, Thomas Coram’s home for abandoned children, with Handel directing until his eyes failed, and the performances carried on after him. He heard it for the last time at Covent Garden on 6 April 1759, almost blind, and died eight days later. By 1784 the Handel commemoration in Westminster Abbey put 525 performers on the platform, and in 1789 Mozart re-scored it for a Viennese patron, adding flutes, clarinets, horns and trombones. The December habit came later still, carried by Part One’s nativity; nothing in the piece’s first decades tied it to Christmas.

Now hear the page you started on. The Hallelujah lasts about four minutes and is built from a handful of plain blocks, so a first-time listener can catch every seam. Put on the King’s College recording below and listen for the climb near the end, where the sopranos hold one long note after another, each a step higher, while the rest of the choir fires back underneath them.

Listen Choir of King’s College, Cambridge, “Hallelujah”

Four blocks. First the shout, “Hallelujah” in short, clipped bursts from the whole choir. Then “For the Lord God omnipotent reigneth,” sung by all the voices on a single line, answered each time by more Hallelujahs. At “The kingdom of this world” the music drops to a quiet, hymn-like hush. Then the climb on “King of Kings, and Lord of Lords,” with “for ever and ever” hammered out beneath it and the trumpets and drums from the top of Handel’s page driving it home. Open on YouTube →

When you go to a full performance this December, carry a three-line map so you always know where you are in the evening.

  1. Part One, prophecy and birth. It opens with the tenor’s “Comfort ye”; “For unto us a Child is born” is the Christmas chorus. This is the section the season borrowed.
  2. Part Two, the Passion. “He was despised” is Cibber’s long alto air, and the Hallelujah closes the part. If the people around you stand, stand with them; the courtesy is older than the king story told to explain it.
  3. Part Three, the resurrection. “I know that my Redeemer liveth” for soprano, “The trumpet shall sound” for bass with solo trumpet, and the closing Amen, which is the real finale.

Before you go, open the free score on IMSLP and follow the Hallelujah once with the recording, eyes on the soprano line through the long rising notes. Then you have a line ready for the interval: Messiah is Easter music that Christmas borrowed, premiered in Dublin in Holy Week to buy debtors out of jail, and the king who supposedly stood for the Hallelujah probably wasn’t in the room.
