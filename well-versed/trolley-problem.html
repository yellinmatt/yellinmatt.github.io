The Trolley Problem

Philippa Foot set a runaway tram loose in a 1967 paper about abortion, and Judith Jarvis Thomson turned it into the most argued-over thought experiment in modern ethics.

A tram has lost its brakes. Ahead on the main line five men are working with their backs to it, and just short of them a spur branches away, where one man is working alone. The driver can steer onto the spur. Asked what he should do, almost everyone answers at once, and the speed of that answer is what the Oxford philosopher Philippa Foot wanted to examine. If killing one to save five is plainly right on the railway, why does the same trade become monstrous a few sentences later?

In 1967 Foot, then teaching at Somerville College, put the tram into a paper, “The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect,” printed in the fifth number of the Oxford Review. Abortion was her subject, and the tram was a control case, set beside a judge who could stop a riot only by sending an innocent man to the gallows on a false charge. The arithmetic of the two cases is identical. The verdicts are opposite, and the distance between them is the trolley problem.

Suppose that a judge or magistrate is faced with rioters demanding that a culprit be found for a certain crime and threatening otherwise to take their own bloody revenge on a particular section of the community. The real culprit being unknown, the judge sees himself as able to prevent the bloodshed only by framing some innocent person and having him executed. Beside this example is placed another in which a pilot whose aeroplane is about to crash is deciding whether to steer from a more to a less inhabited area.

To make the parallel as close as possible it may rather be supposed that he is the driver of a runaway tram which he can only steer from one narrow track on to another; five men are working on one track and one man on the other; anyone on the track he enters is bound to be killed. In the case of the riots the mob has five hostages, so that in both the exchange is supposed to be one man’s life for the lives of five. The question is why we should say, without hesitation, that the driver should steer for the less occupied track, while most of us would be appalled at the idea that the innocent man could be framed.

Philippa Foot, “The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect,” Oxford Review, no. 5, 1967. The first appearance of the tram.

The oldest answer to her question comes from Thomas Aquinas, who asked in the Summa Theologica whether a man may kill in self-defence and replied that “nothing hinders one act from having two effects, only one of which is intended, while the other is beside the intention.” The principle is known as the doctrine of double effect. The driver foresees the death on the spur without aiming at it; if the lone worker leapt clear, the driver would be glad. The judge, as Foot pointed out, needs his victim dead; should the hanging go wrong, his plan requires another way to kill. Foot granted the distinction its force but thought a cleaner line ran between the strict duty not to harm and the weaker duty to help. A driver who holds his course kills five men, while a judge who refuses the mob only fails to rescue the hostages.

Judith Jarvis Thomson of MIT, who died six years ago today, on 20 November 2020, gave the puzzle its name in a 1976 essay, “Killing, Letting Die, and the Trolley Problem,” and came back to break Foot’s answer in “The Trolley Problem,” published in the Yale Law Journal in 1985. Her first change put a passer-by at the switch in place of the driver, a man who kills nobody if he does nothing. Her own feeling, she wrote, was that “an ordinary person, a mere bystander, may intervene in such a case,” which Foot’s duty not to harm cannot easily allow. Her second change moved the bystander onto a footbridge above the line, next to a very large man whose body would stop the tram. The exchange is still one life for five, and almost no one will push.

· · ·

Try the pair on the next person across a table from you, in Thomson’s order: the lever first, the footbridge second, then the question of what changed. The first reply is usually about the hands, since pushing a man feels more like killing him. Press it with a trapdoor in the footbridge, worked by a lever like the one at the switch, so that the body does nothing it did not already do on the railway. Most people still refuse, and at that point their reasons have moved from contact to purpose, which is where Foot and Thomson spent their careers.

The puzzle stopped being a seminar game once cars began to steer themselves. In October 2018 a team led by Edmond Awad at the MIT Media Lab reported in Nature on Moral Machine, an online game that asked players whom a driverless car with failed brakes should spare. It had gathered nearly 40 million decisions from respondents in 233 countries and territories. The strongest preferences held across the world, for humans over animals, for more lives over fewer and for the young over the old, while the respondents fell into western, eastern and southern clusters that weighed the remaining choices differently.

The tool to keep is a subtraction test taken from double effect. Remove the one person from the scene and ask whether the plan still works. If the man on the spur steps off the rails, the five are saved all the same, so his death was a side effect of the rescue. If the man on the footbridge is not there, the tram runs on, so the plan needed his body. The test travels well beyond railways, to any policy or bargain that costs someone dearly: ask whether the harm is the price of the good or the instrument of it.

Foot’s question has outlasted half a century of variations because people answer it with confidence long before they can explain the answer. The line to carry to the table: most of us will divert a tram onto one man to save five, almost none of us will throw one man in front of it, and the trolley problem is the attempt to say why.
