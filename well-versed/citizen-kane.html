What a studio bought when it gave final cut to a man who had never directed

Citizen Kane

RKO handed a twenty-four-year-old radio man control of his own picture before he had shot a frame of film, and what he and his cameraman did with it is why this one still sits third on the critics’ list.

Stand in any room and some part of you knows where the ceiling is. Films of 1940 kept it out of the frame, because the lamps hung on rails above the walls and the camera stayed politely underneath them, and audiences had long since stopped noticing the absence. Nearly every interior in Citizen Kane has a ceiling. They are muslin, hung deliberately low, and because they left nowhere overhead to put a light, all hundred and ten sets had to be lit from the floor up.

The man who insisted on that was Orson Welles, twenty-four years old and famous in the wrong medium. He had built the Mercury Theatre in New York and made himself notorious with a radio broadcast about Martians, and on the strength of it RKO signed him in July 1939 to a contract that has been argued over ever since. It gave him his choice of story, control of the script and the casting, and final cut, terms no established director in Hollywood then held. He had never directed a foot of film, which was rather the point. The studio was buying a reputation, and it came with a company of actors and no habits to unlearn.

His cameraman came to him. Gregg Toland had just shot The Grapes of Wrath and The Long Voyage Home for John Ford, and he walked into Welles’s office and asked for the assignment. The art director, Perry Ferguson, built him those hundred and ten sets for about sixty thousand dollars. Toland lit them with the twin-arc broadsides developed for Technicolor, loaded Kodak’s new Super-XX stock, fitted lenses treated to kill internal flare, and then spent the extra light on the one thing it was for. He stopped the lens down. Ordinary interiors were shot near wide open, somewhere around f/2.3, and anything past f/3.5 was rare enough that people talked about it. Toland shot almost all of Kane at f/8 or smaller, several scenes at f/11, and one at f/16.

A small aperture buys depth of field, and depth was the whole ambition. On the largest sets, which ran the length of two stages at RKO-Pathé, he had to keep two hundred feet of room readable at once. In one shot a man’s head fills the frame less than sixteen inches from the lens while a group of men stands sharp twelve to eighteen feet behind him. Conventional practice would have cut between the two, and a cut tells you where to look. Deep focus declines to. Foreground and background arrive in the same unbroken image, and the job of deciding what matters is handed over to you.

That refusal to choose for the viewer is the design of the entire film. Citizen Kane opens by killing its subject and then runs his obituary as a newsreel, News on the March, which lays out the public facts of a newspaper baron’s life and explains nothing about him. A reporter is sent to find out what Kane meant by his last word, and the rest of the picture is five people remembering a different man: the banker who raised him, the friend he betrayed, the manager who stayed loyal, the second wife he pushed onto an opera stage, the butler at the end. None of them agree, and the reporter never learns what Rosebud was. The camera shows the audience and nobody else, which leaves you holding a fact none of the characters has and no verdict to attach it to.

Four minutes, and not one second of it comes from the film. Welles made a separate little documentary about his own production instead, walking a microphone around the stages and introducing the Mercury players one by one. Listen for the voice and then notice that he never once appears on camera, which is a fair warning about a picture whose subject is a man everybody describes and nobody reaches.

Kane was built visibly out of William Randolph Hearst, and Hearst knew it before anyone had bought a ticket. His papers refused to print the film’s name or take its advertising, his lawyers raised libel, and the premiere slipped and slipped. Louis B. Mayer, acting for a nervous industry, offered RKO more than eight hundred thousand dollars in cash to hand over the negative and every print so they could be destroyed. George Schaefer, who ran the studio, turned him down, and Citizen Kane opened at the RKO Palace in New York on 1 May 1941. It did not recover its cost. The Academy nominated it nine times and gave it one award, for the screenplay, shared between Herman Mankiewicz and Welles.

Then it went on being watched. Sight and Sound, the British Film Institute’s magazine, has polled critics once a decade since 1952, and from 1962 through 2002 Kane won all five of them, fifty unbroken years at the top. Hitchcock’s Vertigo took the crown in 2012. In 2022, with more than sixteen hundred voters, Chantal Akerman’s Jeanne Dielman won it, Vertigo came second, Kane third and Ozu’s Tokyo Story fourth; the directors, polled separately, put Kubrick’s 2001 first and Kane second. Third place is a strange kind of compliment, because a film slides down that list once its methods have been absorbed so completely that they stop reading as choices at all. The Library of Congress chose Kane for the first group of films it set aside to preserve, in 1989. Watch it tonight and neither the deep focus nor the low ceilings will startle you, because everyone learned them, and that is the honest measure of what RKO bought with its contract.
