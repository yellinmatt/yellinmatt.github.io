The Rosetta Stone

The key to the hieroglyphs is a priests’ vote of thanks for a tax cut, and it worked because they ordered it carved three times.

In the middle of July 1799, French soldiers rebuilding a ruined fort outside the Delta port of Rashid, which Europeans called Rosetta, pulled a dark slab out of an old wall. The officer in charge, Lieutenant Pierre-François Bouchard, saw that one face was covered in writing and that the writing came in three bands: hieroglyphs at the top, a running script nobody could name in the middle, and at the bottom fifty-four lines of Greek, which any educated officer in Napoleon’s army could read. He stopped the work and sent the slab to the scholars in Cairo.

What Bouchard had found was the lower part of a stela, an upright inscribed slab, cut from granodiorite and standing a little over a metre high. The top is gone, and with it most of the hieroglyphs. Fourteen lines of them survive, none complete, above thirty-two lines of the middle script. The Greek was translated within months and turned out to be routine business. A synod of Egyptian priests, meeting at Memphis on 27 March 196 BC, thanks Ptolemy V, a boy of thirteen from the Macedonian dynasty that had held Egypt since Alexander, for his gifts to the temples, and votes him statues and festivals in return. The interest lies in the last sentence.

One text, then, in two languages and three scripts: sacred letters for the gods, the native hand for the clerks, Greek for the government. Every scholar in Europe understood what that offered, a known text laid beside an unknown one, and copies of the inscription were circulating before the object itself changed hands. When the French army in Alexandria capitulated in 1801 the antiquities went to the victors. The stone sailed on HMS Egyptienne, reached Portsmouth in February 1802, and has been in the British Museum since that summer with a line painted on its side: “Captured in Egypt by the British Army in 1801.”

Twenty years of failure followed, and the cause was an idea inherited from antiquity. A Greek treatise attributed to Horapollo taught that each hieroglyph was a symbol to be interpreted, so that a goose meant “son” because geese were thought to love their young. If that were true the Greek could never help, since pictures of ideas spell nothing. The first cracks appeared in the middle script. In 1802 Silvestre de Sacy and the Swedish diplomat Johan Åkerblad found the Greek names in it, written out sound by sound. Thomas Young, the English physician who had demonstrated the wave nature of light, then turned to the hieroglyphs. He supposed that the oval rings in the top band, the cartouches, enclosed royal names, and he matched the signs inside one of them to the sounds of Ptolemaios. Of his thirteen values six were right. He published them in the Encyclopædia Britannica in 1819 and concluded that the Egyptians had spelled by sound only when a foreign name forced them to.

Jean-François Champollion, born at Figeac in south-west France in 1790, had one tool Young lacked. He had learned Coptic, the church language of Egypt’s Christians, which descends from the speech of the pharaohs and is written in Greek letters. On 14 September 1822, by his biographer’s account, he was studying copies of cartouches from the temple of Abu Simbel, cut some nine centuries before Alexander reached Egypt. One held a sun disc, an unknown sign, and then twice over the sign that stood for s in Ptolemy. The Coptic word for sun is re. Re, something, s, s: Ramesses. A second cartouche opened with the ibis of the god Thoth and continued with the same unknown sign and a single s, which gave Thutmose. The pair in the middle fitted the Coptic mise, birth, and the stone confirmed it, because the same group stands in the hieroglyphs where the Greek speaks of the king’s birthday.

So the pharaohs had spelled their own names by sound, in a language whose descendant he could read. A story first printed in 1856 has him running to his brother’s office, shouting “Je tiens mon affaire!” and fainting on the floor. On 27 September he read his results to the Académie des Inscriptions in Paris as a letter to its secretary, the Lettre à M. Dacier. Young was in the audience.

Two years later his Précis du système hiéroglyphique gave the verdict that still stands: “a script all at once figurative, symbolic and phonetic, in one and the same text, in one and the same sentence, and, I might even venture, one and the same word.” A sign can picture a thing, stand for an idea, or supply a sound, and a scribe moved between the three at will. Horapollo’s goose turns out to be a pun. It writes “son” because the Egyptian words for the bird and the child shared their consonants. Champollion died in 1832 at forty-one with his grammar unfinished, and his brother saw it through the press.

The stone is now the most visited object in the British Museum, and since 2005 Egyptian officials have asked more than once for it back. As a specimen of hieroglyphic writing it is a poor one, fourteen broken lines of formula. They sufficed because the priests of Memphis wanted a boy king’s generosity understood by everyone in Egypt who could read anything at all.
