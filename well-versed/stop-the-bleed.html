Stop the Bleed

A seven-month count in a Baghdad hospital turned the tourniquet from a last resort into the first thing a bystander reaches for.

In 2006, at a combat support hospital in Baghdad, an Army surgeon named John Kragh spent seven months keeping a ledger of limbs. Of 2,838 casualties carried in with major wounds to an arm or a leg, 232 arrived with tourniquets already on: 428 straps on 309 limbs. He wanted to know whether those straps were saving lives or costing legs, since the old fear about the device was that it traded one for the other.

The numbers answered both halves. When the tourniquet went on before shock set in, which the study marked by a weak or absent pulse at the wrist, 90 percent of the casualties survived; once shock had arrived, 10 percent did. Straps applied in the field beat straps applied in the emergency department, with a death rate of 11 percent against 24. Five casualties needed a tourniquet and never got one, and none of them lived. As for the legs, four patients had a nerve palsy that passed, and no limb was lost to a tourniquet alone. The paper ran in the Annals of Surgery in January 2009, and its finding for anyone outside an operating room is about the clock: whether a bleeding person lives is settled in the first few minutes, by whoever happens to be standing nearest.

Civilian medicine took the point after Newtown. Following the shooting at Sandy Hook Elementary School in December 2012, the American College of Surgeons convened trauma surgeons, military medics and the heads of police and fire services in Hartford, Connecticut, under a committee chaired by Lenworth Jacobs. They met there three times and built their recommendations on an acronym, THREAT: threat suppression, hemorrhage control, rapid extrication to safety, assessment by medical providers, and transport to definitive care. The H was the one letter a passer-by could carry out. On 6 October 2015 the White House launched a campaign to teach it to the public and called it Stop the Bleed.

The urgency is plain in the Los Angeles Fire Department’s version of the course, which warns that severe bleeding “can lead to death in as little as 5 minutes.” The whole sequence fits on a card.

ReadStop the Bleed, as the LAFD teaches it

Alert: Call 9-1-1…

Bleeding: Locate the source of the bleeding.

Compress: If the injury is to a leg or an arm you should use a tourniquet.

Do not place the tourniquet onto a joint, go above the joint if necessary.

Wind the windlass until the bleeding stops, then secure the windlass in place.

Los Angeles Fire Department, “Stop the Bleed: Tourniquet,” abridged. The Florida Department of Health card for the same course adds the last step: note the time the tourniquet was applied.

Put the card into your hands and it becomes a short procedure. The strap goes on two to three inches above the wound, on the side nearer the heart, so a gash just below the knee takes the strap on the thigh. Pull the free end as tight as it will go before you touch the rod, because the rod is there for the last few turns. Then twist until the bleeding stops. It will hurt, and the Florida card says so without apology: “A tourniquet will cause pain, yet is necessary to stop life-threatening bleeding.” Lock the rod and write the time on the strap, so the surgeon knows how long the limb has gone without blood. A wound no strap can circle, on the trunk or deep in the groin, gets pressure instead: stuff gauze or a clean cloth into it, then push down with both hands as hard as you can and keep pushing until the medics take over.

Do this Five minutes on the floor, then a real class

Sit down and pick a spot on your calf, a hand’s width below the knee, for a pretend wound. Put your hand where the strap would go and notice that the knee pushes it up onto the thigh. Then fold a towel on the floor, kneel over it with straight arms and your shoulders above your hands, and press with your weight for one full minute: that is the posture you would hold until help arrives. Last, find a hands-on course near you, since the strap is learned by pulling one tight.

When it comes up, here is your line: in Baghdad a tourniquet put on before the pulse faded meant nine in ten survived, and after it faded one in ten, so the person who saves a life from bleeding is usually whoever is already in the room.
