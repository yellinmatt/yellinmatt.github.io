Churchill’s Cigar

The century’s most famous cigar smoker took up the habit in Cuba at twenty, smoked less of each cigar than anyone supposed, and is best remembered from the one second he was made to put it down.

On 30 December 1941 Winston Churchill finished a speech to a joint sitting of the Canadian Parliament in Ottawa and walked into the Speaker’s chamber, where Yousuf Karsh had set up his lights and been promised a few moments with him. The Prime Minister arrived with a cigar in his mouth and no intention of removing it for a photographer. Karsh waited. Then, as he told it afterwards, he “stepped toward him and, without premeditation, but ever so respectfully, I said, ‘Forgive me, sir,’ and plucked the cigar out of his mouth.” The glare he caught an instant later ran on the cover of Saturday Night on 10 January 1942 and as a full page in Life the following month, and it remains the face most people picture at the mention of his name. The best-known portrait of the man records the moment his cigar was gone.

The habit began in Cuba. In the autumn of 1895 the young cavalry officer used five months of army leave to go and watch Spain fight the island’s rebels, travelling by way of New York with an arrangement to send occasional dispatches to the Daily Graphic. He reached Havana in November, and for several days he and a fellow officer lived on little more than oranges and cigars. On 30 November, his twenty-first birthday, he came under fire for the first time, near the village of Arroyo Blanco in the centre of the island. He sailed from Havana on 14 December carrying two lasting tastes, one for the afternoon siesta and one for Havana tobacco, and he planned to stock his mother’s cellar with cigars. From then on, as an associate of one of his biographers put it, “cigar and Cuban were synonymous for Churchill.”

For the next seventy years he bought them by the box. Among his largest suppliers was Robert Lewis at 19 St James’s Street in London, a shop that dates its first cigar sale to 1787 and now trades as James J. Fox, and whose handwritten ledgers record hundreds of thousands of cigars sold to him. His favourite house was Romeo y Julieta, the Havana brand that José “Pepín” Rodríguez Fernández acquired in 1903 and whose factory printed personal bands for rich clients, some two thousand of them at the height of the trade. He liked La Aroma de Cuba as well, a brand that no longer exists. At Chartwell, his house in Kent, a room beside the study held three to four thousand cigars on shelves labelled “large” and “small,” “wrapped” and “naked,” the last pair meaning with or without cellophane. He could buy cheap too: in the 1930s he paid $147 for a thousand American-made Longfellows, under fifteen cents a cigar.

The way he smoked explains the legend better than the count. Those around him put him at eight to ten cigars a day. He seldom bothered with a cutter; he wet the head of the cigar and pierced it with one of the extra-long wooden matches he had sent from Canada, and he wrapped the end in a “bellybando,” a strip of brown gummed paper that stopped it fraying and going soggy as he chewed. He drew the smoke into his mouth and let it out without inhaling, let the cigar go cold so he could keep chewing on it, and often threw it away half-smoked. The International Churchill Society concludes that his smoking, like his drinking, was less than it appeared. The Robert Lewis ledger for 1964, the year before he died at ninety, shows 825 cigars bought over six months, about four and a half a day.

The war turned the habit into a matter for the security services. Cuban admirers sent cigars to the Prime Minister throughout 1941: two boxes from the Maceo Society of Camagüey in January, then large consignments in the spring from the magazine Bohemia and from the Cuban National Tobacco Commission, whose presentation cabinet still stands in the painting studio at Chartwell. His private office feared a poisoner. Roche Lynch, the Home Office’s senior analyst, tested the first boxes, found nothing, and smoked one cigar from each “with no untoward effects.” MI5 later took one cigar from every Cuban box, injected mice with a broth made from them and laid fragments on an observer’s tongue, and reported that “four days elapsed without mishap.” Lord Rothschild, who ran the work, passed the samples while reminding the office that nicotine itself is “very poisonous indeed.” That November, when a secretary proposed exchanging two Brazilian gifts for safer stock or handing them to the staff, Churchill answered in a minute.

· · ·

“… my rule of life prescribed as an absolutely sacred rite smoking cigars and also the drinking of alcohol before, after and if need be during all meals …”

Minute on the Brazilian gift cigars, November 1941, Churchill Archives Centre, CHAR 2/434 (Allen Packwood, “Cigars: Protecting the Premier,” Finest Hour 106). Churchill’s account of his reply to King Ibn Saud of Saudi Arabia at a luncheon in Egypt, February 1945.

The first line is the man in a sentence: he would not hand his staff a risk he had been told to avoid himself. The second came at a luncheon in Egypt in February 1945, after Churchill learned that Ibn Saud permitted no smoking or drinking in his presence. He answered that his own religion required both, at every meal and if need be in between, and the king let him light up.

Romeo y Julieta named its seven-inch cigar with a 47 ring gauge the Churchill in his honour, and the name now serves cigar makers in every country as the standard term for that size. Order one and you will likely finish it more faithfully than he usually did, since he smoked cigars of many lengths and few of them to the end. My own reading is that he understood the cigar as a mark of identity long before anyone spoke of a personal brand. It was legible across a crowded room, in a newsreel and even on a stretcher, after a car knocked him down in New York in 1931, and Karsh showed how much it carried by removing it: the Ottawa portrait holds the eye because every viewer in 1942 knew exactly what was missing from the picture.
