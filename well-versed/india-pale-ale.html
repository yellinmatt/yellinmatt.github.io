India Pale Ale

The beer was not invented to survive the voyage to India, and the archive says so in its own words.

On 25 August 1769, a year out from England and somewhere in the South Pacific, Joseph Banks recorded in his Endeavour journal that the ship’s company had cut a piece of Cheshire cheese saved for the anniversary and tapped a cask of porter, which “provd excellently good, so that we livd like English men and drank the hea[l]ths of our freinds in England.” The beer was ordinary London porter. It had crossed the equator twice and sat twelve months in a hold under Cook’s deck, and it was fine.

Hold on to that cask, because the story everyone tells about India pale ale requires it to be impossible. In the received account, English beer soured on the four-month run to Calcutta, so a London brewer called George Hodgson engineered a stronger and far hoppier ale to survive the passage, and everything hoppy we drink today descends from that solution. You will find it on pump clips, on menus, and in most books published before about 2005. The archive supports almost none of it.

Hodgson brewed at Bow, on the eastern edge of London, and the beer he sold into the India trade already existed. It was October beer, the strong pale stock ale that country gentlemen laid down by the hogshead: Every Man His Own Brewer, published in 1768, prescribes six and a half pounds of hops to every eight bushels of malt for what it calls “a year’s keeping.” Brewers had known how to make beer that lasted for generations. Hodgson’s real advantages were a river and a ledger. The East Indiamen loaded at Blackwall, a short haul down the Lea from his brewhouse, and he let their captains run up to eighteen months’ credit on what they took. The Calcutta Gazette of 20 January 1822 advertised “Hodgson’s warranted prime picked pale ale of the genuine October brewing,” sold under its English name with India nowhere in it.

The voyage did do something, and no one planned it. Four months of heat and motion round the Cape put the beer through a maturation that would have taken two years in a Staffordshire cellar, so it came ashore at Bombay in a condition nobody in England could buy. Hodgson was lucky, and being first to be lucky in a market of a few thousand hogsheads a year, against the two million barrels London brewed annually, made him briefly its master.

The legend endures because its kernel is sound. Hops do preserve. Boiling isomerises the alpha acids in the cone into iso-alpha acids, and at beer’s low pH those behave as ionophores, ferrying protons across the membranes of Gram-positive bacteria and collapsing the gradient that keeps a lactic-acid spoilage organism alive. Every brewer in Georgian England understood the effect in practice, which is precisely the reason nobody needed to invent a beer around it.

Burton is where the story becomes about a place. Russia’s tariff wall had just shut Samuel Allsopp out of the Baltic trade his town lived on, and at a London dinner early in 1822 an East India Company director named Campbell Marjoribanks sent his butler for a bottle of Hodgson’s ale that had been out to India and back. The scene that follows was written down thirty years later by a doctor commissioned to explain Burton to the reading public.

IIt happened at this time, that dining with an East Indian Director, Mr. Marjoribanks, in the course of conversation he happened to make some remark on the late occurrences in Russia, the neglect of his by the government, and the gloomy prospects of his house from the loss of their European trade.

II“But why not, Allsopp,” observed Mr. Marjoribanks, “leave the cold climates, and try the warmer regions of the earth? Why do you not make an attempt on the Indian market?”
“I never heard of it.”
“There are 5,000 of English beer sent to Madras and Bengal every year; and, what is more to your purpose, it is a trade that can never be lost… We are all now dependent upon Hodgson, who has given offence to most of our merchants in India. But your Burton ale, so strong and sweet, will not suit our market.”

IIIHere Mr. Marjoribanks rang the bell, and directed his butler to bring in a bottle of Hodgson’s ale, that had been to India and back. The butler poured out a glass for Mr. Allsopp, who held it up to the light, and then, tasting it, exclaimed, “Is this the Indian Beer? I can brew it.”

IVOn Mr. Allsopp’s return to Burton-on-Trent, sitting one morning in his … he took up a bottle on which was written “Hodgson’s Indian beer”… so, setting aside the sample of malt which he was then carefully examining, he directed “Job Goodhead” to be summoned. Now this Job Goodhead was the of the house…

V“Job,” said Mr. Allsopp, holding out to him a glass of the Indian beer, “can you dry your malt that colour?”
“Yes, sir,” answered Job, at the same time tasting the beer, and sputtering it out fast, for Job was of the old school, and preferred his ale strong and sweet. “But, sir,”—
“Never mind the taste, Job. Can you dry your malt to this colour?—are you sure?”
“Quite sure, sir.”
“Then do so.” The great brewer and his hereditary maltster set to work at once; and the first specimen of the great pale ale and bitter beer trade of Burton, now extending to so many thousand hogsheads, was brewed in a teapot.

John Stevenson Bushnan, Burton and its Bitter Beer (1853), pp. 100–102. Ron Pattinson, who put the passage back into circulation, accepts the meeting and the sample and treats the spitting and the teapot as later polish on a company story.

Allsopp’s beer came out better than the London original it copied, and the reason sits under the town. Burton stands on Keuper marl, and its well water carries calcium sulfate at six to eight hundred parts per million. Sulfate sharpens the perception of bitterness and dries the finish, so the same grist and the same hops read cleaner and more hop-forward brewed there than on the Thames. Within a generation brewers everywhere were dosing soft water with gypsum to imitate it, a practice still called burtonising and still the default under a West Coast IPA.

The second thing everybody knows has a shipwreck in it. Walter Molyneaux wrote in 1869 that around 1827 a vessel carrying three hundred hogsheads went down in the Irish Channel, that the salvage was sold in Liverpool, and that “by this means, in a remarkably rapid manner, the fame of the new India ale spread throughout Great Britain.” Historians hunted that wreck for a century and found nothing, until Martyn Cornell established that Molyneaux had missed the year by twelve. The East Indiaman Crusader cleared Liverpool for Bombay on 6 January 1839 with hogsheads of Bass and Allsopp ale aboard, straight into the storm the Irish still call the Night of the Big Wind. She struck a bank off Blackpool that is marked Crusader Bank on the charts to this day, and on 7 February her salvaged cargo went under the hammer in Liverpool: silk scarves, tin plates, and “India ale, Bass and Alsop’s brands.” Whether that sale made the beer’s English name is a separate question, and Cornell’s verdict on it is “not proven.”

The beer did come home. Bass opened a Liverpool store for pale India ale in 1841, announcing that it was “almost superseding every other sort of malt liquor” in the country, and put its red triangle on the trade-mark register the year the register opened, in 1876.

Then it almost vanished. British IPA thinned through the twentieth century until the name sat on bitters of three and a half per cent, surviving at full strength mainly in Worthington’s White Shield. Anchor Liberty Ale, first brewed in California in 1975 with whole Cascade hops, is generally taken as the first modern American treatment, and what it wanted out of a hop was smell rather than shelf life: grapefruit, pine, resin, thrown in late in the boil and again after fermentation, when the aromatic oils survive instead of boiling away. The old beer was hopped to last. The new one is hopped to be opened young, and a can four months old is past its best, which inverts the very thing the name commemorates.

What the numbers mean on a menu

Ranges from the BJCP 2021 guidelines, the document homebrew competitions actually judge against. IBU counts bitter compounds in parts per million and stops meaning much above about seventy, since the tongue saturates. Note the hazy: fewer bitterness units than an English IPA and more alcohol, because its hops go in cold and late for aroma. Bitterness and hoppiness are separate axes, and the menu rarely says which one it is selling you.

Watch Martyn Cornell on The Craft Beer Channel

The historian who did the archival work, taking the myth apart in person. Listen for how gently he handles it: he keeps conceding what the legend gets right before naming where the documents stop supporting it, which is the tone to borrow if you ever have to correct someone about this at a bar.

Carry away the shape of it rather than the correction. The name records a trade route instead of a recipe: an English keeping ale that happened to travel well, that one Staffordshire town’s water improved, that came home famous and then nearly died, and that California brought back around the opposite virtue. Ask a bar which IPA it means before you order, because at this point the three letters cover a bitter English pale ale, a resinous West Coast one, and a soft cloudy thing that tastes of orange juice.
