The Meiji Restoration

A warrior class carried out the revolution that abolished it, and the bonds it took in exchange became the country’s banking system.

A photographer in San Francisco sat five Japanese officials down in the spring of 1872. Four of them wear wool frock coats and silk cravats with top hats balanced on the knee, and the fifth, seated in the middle, wears court robes and a chonmage, the shaved crown and oiled topknot that had marked a samurai for three centuries. The man in the middle was Iwakura Tomomi, the ranking figure of the party, and he would cut his hair before the tour was out. The four around him, Kido Takayoshi, Yamaguchi Masuka, Itō Hirobumi and Ōkubo Toshimichi, amounted to most of the working government of Japan, and they were four years into the business of taking apart the order that had produced all five of them.

The event that put them there had been a small one. On 3 January 1868 a coalition from the outer domains seized the palace gates in Kyōto, announced the ouster of Tokugawa Yoshinobu and proclaimed the fifteen-year-old Mutsuhito ruler of Japan. Yoshinobu had already given up his powers the previous November on the calculation that he would keep a place in whatever came next. He was wrong, fought a short civil war over it, and surrendered in June 1869. A shogunate of two hundred and sixty-five years ended inside eighteen months at a cost light enough that the affair still travels under the label of a bloodless revolution. The two decades that followed were another matter.

Most of the winners were samurai of middling rank in their thirties, from domains that had backed the losing side at Sekigahara in 1600 and been kept outside the Tokugawa inner circle ever since. They invoked the emperor, but the argument that moved them was China, where the Opium Wars had shown an old civilization opened by gunboat and parcelled out into concessions. The lesson was legible from Edo Bay, into which Commodore Perry had sailed in 1853 to demand the same terms at the same range. Fukoku kyōhei, enrich the country and strengthen the army, was a programme for not becoming China.

In April 1868, with the fighting still running in the north, the new government issued five sentences in the emperor’s name.

Article four was Kido’s contribution and the most useful of the five. Read narrowly it disowns the expulsion politics that half the coalition had been shouting five years earlier. Read broadly it licenses the abolition of anything, and the government took the broad reading. The capital moved from Kyōto to Edo, renamed Tokyo, before the year was out. The daimyo were induced to hand back their land registers in 1869, and in August 1871 the domains went altogether, some two hundred and seventy of them replaced by prefectures under governors appointed from Tokyo. A conscription law in 1873 put commoners under arms. An edict in 1876 forbade the wearing of swords in public.

Every one of those measures took from the samurai something they had held by birth, and every one was drafted by samurai. The heaviest was fiscal. Each man of the class drew a hereditary stipend, and the new regime had inherited the entire payroll; through the early 1870s those stipends ran between a quarter and a third of all state spending, owed to about five per cent of the population. In 1876 the government stopped paying cash and issued kinroku bonds instead, 173.9 million yen of them, thirty-year maturity, nothing saleable for five years, and the wealthiest recipients came out on a quarter of their former income. Then the bonds did something nobody had legislated. Chartered national banks were permitted to count them as capital, and the number of such banks went from six in 1876 to a hundred and fifty-three inside three years, with ex-samurai subscribing three times the capital of every other class combined. The stipend that had kept a man ready to fight turned into the reserve behind a country’s credit.

Resistance came from the men who had made the revolution. Saigō Takamori of Satsuma, who commanded the imperial armies in 1868 and then resigned over his colleagues’ refusal to invade Korea, became the reluctant figurehead of some thirty thousand shizoku who took the field in Kyūshū in February 1877. Seven months of fighting ended with Saigō dead at Shiroyama above Kagoshima that September, beaten by an army of conscripted farmers and shopkeepers. Everyone drew the same conclusion. Bearing arms had been the class’s whole justification, and men who had carried a sword since childhood had just lost to men issued rifles three years before.

Itō Hirobumi, the youngest man in the San Francisco photograph, went to Berlin and Vienna looking for a model and came home with a Prussian one. The constitution he drafted was promulgated on 11 February 1889 and presented as a gift from the throne, amendable only on the emperor’s initiative, seating a bicameral Diet on a franchise narrow enough to exclude almost everybody. The first Diet met in 1890.

By then Japan had a parliament, a conscript army, a national school system, a banking sector and better than two thousand kilometres of railway, and it had assembled all of it inside a single working lifetime. The bill for that speed came due where the anniversary speeches do not go: in Hokkaidō, where the settlement of Ainu land began in 1869 and the language and the fishing rights went with it; in the Watarase valley below the Ashio copper mine, poisoned by tailings from the 1880s; in the tenements of the new industrial cities. Tristan Grunow’s observation about the Meiji sesquicentennial is that a success story stays one only by being edited.
