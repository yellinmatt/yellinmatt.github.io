Pandora’s Box

The oldest telling gives Pandora a storage jar as tall as a man, Erasmus handed her a box in 1508, and the one thing the lid kept in has been argued over ever since.

Waterhouse painted her in 1896 on her knees in a dark wood, one hand raising the lid of a gilded chest, her face turned close to the gap as a thread of vapour slips out. That is the picture most of us carry when someone warns against “opening Pandora’s box”: a curious woman, a forbidden casket, every trouble in the world let loose. It is a fine painting of the wrong object. The earliest full telling of the myth, in the Greek poet Hesiod’s Works and Days, has no box in it at all, and the detail most people forget, the one thing left inside, is where the story keeps its sting.

Hesiod frames it as a bill for stolen fire. Prometheus has smuggled fire to mortals in a hollow fennel stalk, and Zeus promises them in exchange “an evil thing in which they may all be glad of heart while they embrace their own destruction.” Hephaestus moulds a maiden from earth and water, Athena teaches her weaving, Aphrodite sheds grace on her head, and Hermes puts in her “lies and crafty words and a deceitful nature.” Because every god on Olympus “gave each a gift,” she is called Pandora, the all-gifted. Hermes delivers her to Prometheus’s brother Epimetheus, who had been told never to accept a gift from Zeus. In the poem’s driest line, “he took the gift, and afterwards, when the evil thing was already his, he understood.”

For ere this the tribes of men lived on earth remote and free from ills and hard toil and heavy sickness which bring upon men; for in misery men grow old quickly. But the woman took off the great lid of the jar with her hands and scattered all these and her thought caused sorrow and mischief to men. Only remained there in an unbreakable home within under the rim of the great jar, and did not fly out at the door; for ere that, the lid of the jar stopped her, by the will of Zeus who gathers the clouds. But the rest, countless plagues, wander amongst men; for earth is full of evils and the sea is full. Of themselves diseases come upon men continually by day and by night, bringing mischief to mortals silently; for wise Zeus took away speech from them. So is there no way to escape the will of Zeus.

Hesiod, Works and Days 90–105, translated by Hugh G. Evelyn-White (Loeb Classical Library, 1914).

The word Hesiod uses for that jar is pithos, which the classicist William Hansen describes as a storage vessel five or six feet high. Picture the household’s larder in clay: the kind of jar Hesiod tells his farmer, a few hundred lines later, to fill with winnowed grain. A woman lifting the lid of one is lifting the lid on the stores of a house, and in this jar the stores are diseases that travel in silence.

The box arrived in 1508. Retelling the myth in Latin under the proverb that the gifts of enemies are no gifts, Erasmus of Rotterdam rendered pithos as pyxis, a box. Robert Graves, reviewing Dora and Erwin Panofsky’s study of the myth in the Spectator in 1956, guessed that Erasmus had “somehow confused her jar with the deadly casket which foolish Psyche opened in The Golden Ass,” and noted that the jar has stayed a box in popular speech ever since, except in Italian, where it is still a vaso. Waterhouse’s chest descends from that slip of a humanist’s pen.

Now do the reading yourself. Say the passage above aloud once, slowly, and stop at the lid closing on Hope. Then make the call every reader of Hesiod has to make: is Hope kept for us, a last provision stored in the house, or kept from us, one more thing Zeus holds back while the plagues roam free? The grammar will not settle it, and Britannica records a later version of the story in which the jar held blessings, lost to the world when curiosity opened it. My reading leans dark: the passage closes on “no way to escape the will of Zeus,” and after five lines of diseases that come in silence, a Hope locked under the rim reads as one more thing withheld.

The habit worth keeping is the vessel test. Whenever an old story turns up in an argument, ask three things: what the oldest text actually says, when the familiar detail came in, and what the original leaves inside. For Pandora the answers are a jar, 1508 and hope, and the third is the one that changes the meaning of the phrase. Someone who warns against opening Pandora’s box usually means a cascade of trouble; Hesiod’s version adds that the lid came down in time to keep the one thing that might have helped.

When the phrase comes up at dinner, here is your line: Hesiod gave Pandora a storage jar five or six feet tall, Erasmus turned it into a box in 1508, and the only thing that stayed inside was hope.
