Literature · The Birth of the Novel

Don Quixote

A broken soldier published a joke about a man ruined by reading, then wrote a sequel in which that man discovers he has become a book.

On a plain in La Mancha a thin man in salvaged armour counts thirty or forty windmills and informs his squire that they are giants. Sancho Panza, a farmer with no imagination and excellent eyesight, tells him flatly that they are windmills and that the arms are sails. Don Quixote spurs his horse anyway. The sail catches the lance, snaps it, and flings horse and rider across the field, and when Sancho reaches him the knight cannot move. Four centuries of readers have taken that scene for the whole book, which is a pity, because Cervantes was still warming up.

The volume came out of Juan de la Cuesta’s press in Madrid on 16 January 1605 as El ingenioso hidalgo don Quixote de la Mancha, opening on a sentence every Spanish schoolchild still knows by heart: En un lugar de la Mancha, de cuyo nombre no quiero acordarme, in a village of La Mancha, the name of which I have no wish to remember. Cervantes had sold the rights the previous July to the bookseller Francisco de Robles for a sum nobody wrote down, and the first edition ran to four hundred copies, most of them shipped to the Americas where Robles expected a better price. He had misjudged his market. Pirated editions were coming off Lisbon presses within weeks, and by that August six printings were alive in three countries.

The author was fifty-seven, which in Habsburg Spain was old, and he had spent the working part of his life being unlucky in interesting ways. At Lepanto in October 1571 he took three gunshot wounds, two in the chest and one that left his left hand useless ever after; Spain remembered him as el manco de Lepanto, the one-handed man of Lepanto. Sailing home in 1575 he was seized by Barbary corsairs and sold in Algiers, where he spent five years as a slave and tried four times to escape before Trinitarian friars raised his ransom. Back in Spain he requisitioned grain for the Armada, collected taxes, was excommunicated once for helping himself to church stores, and did time in a Seville jail over his accounts. The story that he first imagined Don Quixote in that cell has never been proved and has never needed to be.

What makes the book the first modern novel is a piece of paperwork. Eight chapters in, Cervantes halts the story mid-swordfight and admits his source has run dry. He recovered the rest, he says, in the Alcaná market in Toledo, where a boy was selling old papers for scrap: an Arabic history by a Moorish chronicler called Cide Hamete Benengeli, bought for half a real and turned into Castilian by a hired man paid in raisins and wheat. Everything after that reaches the reader through an author who denies authorship, a chronicler whose truthfulness the text keeps questioning because he is a Moor, and a translator working for groceries. The reader is handed the problem of whether any of it happened and left alone with it. Novels have run on that problem ever since.

At this point they came in sight of thirty or forty windmills that there are on that plain, and as soon as Don Quixote saw them he said to his squire, “Fortune is arranging matters for us better than we could have shaped our desires ourselves, for look there, friend Sancho Panza, where thirty or more monstrous giants present themselves, all of whom I mean to engage in battle and slay.”

“What giants?” said Sancho Panza.

“Those thou seest there,” answered his master, “with the long arms, and some have them nearly two long.”

“Look, ,” said Sancho; “what we see there are not giants but windmills, and what seem to be their arms are the sails that turned by the wind make the millstone go.”

“It is easy to see,” replied Don Quixote, “that thou art not used to this business of adventures; those are giants; and if thou art afraid, away with thee out of this and betake thyself to prayer while I engage them in fierce and unequal combat.”

A slight breeze at this moment sprang up, and the great sails began to move, seeing which Don Quixote exclaimed, “Though ye flourish more arms than the giant , ye have to reckon with me.”

So saying, and commending himself with all his heart to his lady Dulcinea, imploring her to support him in such a peril, with and covered by his , he charged at Rocinante’s fullest gallop and fell upon the first mill that stood in front of him; but as he drove his lance-point into the sail the wind whirled it round with such force that it shivered the lance to pieces, sweeping with it horse and rider, who went rolling over on the plain, in a sorry condition.

Cervantes, Don Quixote I.8, translated by John Ormsby (1885).

Watch what the scene actually does. Sancho is correct, the windmills win, and Don Quixote climbs up with his explanation ready: the magician Frestón, who once stole his library, has turned the giants into windmills to cheat him of the glory of killing them. The delusion takes the fall and survives it. That pattern runs the whole first part, and it is why quixotic was never meant as praise. The word names a mind that has stopped letting the world vote.

In September 1614, with Cervantes about fifty-nine chapters into a sequel he had been slow to write, a rival book surfaced in Tarragona: Second Volume of the Ingenious Gentleman Don Quixote of La Mancha, by one Alonso Fernández de Avellaneda of Tordesillas, a name that belonged to nobody then and belongs to nobody now. Its preface called Cervantes old, crippled, and jealous. He finished his own Part Two inside a year and wrote the theft into the plot.

His Don Quixote, riding toward the tournament at Zaragoza, stops at an inn and overhears two gentlemen in the next room reading a book about him. He gathers that the Don Quixote in their pages is bound for Zaragoza and has fallen out of love with Dulcinea, and turns for Barcelona instead, so that a printed lie can be caught out by a fact. Further on he meets Don Álvaro Tarfe, a character lifted straight from Avellaneda, and makes him swear before a notary that he has never laid eyes on this Don Quixote. At the end Cervantes kills the knight and has a notary certify the death, so no third man can raise him again. He was dead himself by 22 April 1616, five months after the book appeared.

That is the condition of the second volume throughout: everyone Don Quixote meets in 1615 has already read the 1605 book, so he spends his last road being recognised, quoted, flattered and set up by people who know his story better than he does. A duke and duchess stage weeks of elaborate cruelty on him purely because they enjoyed Part One. A character who reads the book he is living in, and alters his route to correct it, is the whole trick of the modern novel, performed once and completely, three hundred years before anyone had a name for it. When the Norwegian Nobel Institute polled writers in fifty-five countries in 2002 for the greatest work of fiction ever written, they chose the book about the madman who reads.
