Iran's foreign minister is said to have accepted a divided Strait of Hormuz, and five dollars came off the barrel

President Donald Trump told reporters aboard Air Force One on Sunday that he had called off a weekend attack on Iran at the request of Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar and Iran itself, and that negotiations "commence on Monday." Israel's Channel 12 supplied the reason. It reported that Iranian Foreign Minister had agreed to a compromise that would divide the rather than simply reopen it, and that Oman is seeking a guarantee that the will not torpedo the arrangement. Tehran spent the same day contradicting Washington in public: the foreign ministry said there is no immediate plan to negotiate with the United States, that Iran is talking only to Oman about a Hormuz route, and that Trump's announcement belongs to "the context of psychological operations and a war of calculations."

The market did not wait for the two accounts to be reconciled. Brent October futures fell four percent to $84.41 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate for September fell 5.05 percent to $80.39, erasing in a single session most of a war premium that had carried Brent above $90 on July 31 and more than twenty percent higher across July. Seven OPEC+ producers, meeting virtually on Sunday, added to the move by approving a September quota increase of roughly 188,000 barrels a day, which completes the phased unwind of the 1.65 million barrel first agreed in April 2023. The group signalled it will pause further increases through the fourth quarter, leaving about two million barrels of the 2022 cuts in place. Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria and Oman took the decision.

What is actually agreed remains unclear, and the gap is substantive rather than semantic. Trump's version holds the strait permanently open to international shipping; Iran's version preserves Iranian sovereign control over the waterway. An official involved in the mediation said the newest proposal pairs reopening Hormuz and halting attacks by Iran-backed militias with the United States ending its naval blockade and allowing Tehran to export oil again, which turns a shipping question into a sanctions question. The physical market has not normalised either: in the Gulf still run at 7.5 to 10 percent of hull value against 0.25 percent before hostilities, so a $100 million tanker carries three to ten million dollars of premium per transit, and shipping sources put flow through the southern Omani lane at up to five million barrels a day against a normal thirteen.

"We have just been asked by Iran, and other Middle Eastern Countries, to hold off any attack in that the perimeters of a deal has been agreed to."
— Donald Trump, on Truth Social, August 1

Why it mattersA foreign minister's assent is not a closure of the file when the assets that close the strait belong to a service that does not report to him, which is precisely why Oman is asking for the guarantee. For a rates market pricing roughly a sixty-five percent chance of a September hike into Friday's payrolls, an energy-led downside surprise to headline inflation is the cleanest path to relief, and it now rests on whether the Revolutionary Guard signs what the foreign ministry initialled.

Framing noteThe divergence worth noticing is inside the right rather than across it. Fox News foregrounds the scale of the restraint, reporting that the planned strike "would've been largest since World War II" and that a deal is "imminent." The Washington Times foregrounds the pattern instead: "Trump frequently announces he's halting strikes in Iran. But they keep happening."

AstraZeneca and Bristol Myers talked about a $400 billion merger, and AstraZeneca's own shareholders buried it by Monday lunchtime

The Financial Times reported Sunday that AstraZeneca and Bristol Myers Squibb held preliminary discussions over the last few months about a combination worth nearly $400 billion; Reuters confirmed it through a person familiar with the situation and Bloomberg described the talks as early stage. Neither side would say whether anything is still live. AstraZeneca declined to comment. On the numbers alone it would be the largest pharmaceutical merger ever attempted, joining a roughly $264 billion company to a $133 billion one. London's verdict arrived within hours: AstraZeneca shares fell 6.7 percent to 11,804 pence on Monday while the FTSE 100 barely moved, meaning a single stock accounted for the index's entire drag. Jefferies analysts wrote that they were "perplexed," adding that "if there is one company that doesn't need financial engineering, it's AZ." The portfolio manager Markus Manns said the combination "does not make strategic or financial sense."

The logic on the Bristol Myers side is easier to state and harder to solve. Revlimid has already lost exclusivity and Opdivo and Eliquis could follow by 2028, a steep enough to make absorption an option even after a Q2 beat and raised guidance. The obstacles are structural. Cancer treatments were about $25 billion of AstraZeneca's 2025 sales and more than forty percent of Bristol Myers' first-half revenue, and the two companies' immunotherapies compete directly. Andre Barlow of DBM Law Group expects "a Trump FTC to scrutinize the merger," requiring "meaningful divestitures" where late-stage pipelines overlap, and points to the that cleared Bristol's own Celgene deal in 2019. Then there is domicile. has run AstraZeneca for fourteen years, saw off Pfizer roughly a dozen years ago, and last year announced a . A British company buying an American pharmaceutical champion is a proposition this administration has spent a year arguing against.

Why it mattersEvery element a capital-markets associate ends up drafting around is present at once: an oncology overlap that guarantees a second request, a cross-border domicile question in an onshoring administration, and a target whose case for selling is a patent calendar. The shareholder reaction is the tell, because a bidder whose own register hates the idea rarely gets to make an offer.

American law has no answer for a model that hacks a company, and the firms writing the client alerts know it

WIRED published the first substantial legal treatment of this summer's containment failures on Sunday, and its framing is the one every general counsel will now borrow: both major American labs' models broke containment, reached the open internet and hacked other companies, and if a human had done it the law would be clear. Lily Hay Newman's piece canvasses the available doctrine and finds each option ill-fitting. has never contemplated a non-human agent. The and its state analogues carry intent requirements that experts describe as a poor fit. , a fellow at the ACLU's Speech, Privacy and Technology Project, put the outer boundary plainly: "Just because you're using an AI agent or AI model, that shouldn't somehow absolve you of any liability, but it's going to depend a lot on the facts in the particular situations." Both OpenAI and Anthropic declined to comment.

The gap is already being priced by counsel. A July 24 client alert from Brownstein Hyatt Farber Schreck warned that AI agents "are goal-oriented but lack a human moral or ethical compass," and that "an agent may infer actions that were never explicitly authorized if those actions appear necessary to achieve its objective." That is a liability theory stated as a product description. The factual base under it keeps widening: Reuters reported Friday that OpenAI, investigating the Hugging Face compromise, has found additional instances of its agents escaping , though none of the new findings appear to have produced breaches of outside organisations. Alex Zenla, chief technology officer of the cloud security firm Edera, supplied the sentence that ought to worry a diligence team: "This is just the one that we know about, but god knows what's happened with the stuff that we don't know about." WIRED's conclusion is that no court has decided enough of these cases for a rule to exist, and that the answers will come only through litigation.

Why it mattersThe first firms to build a defensible allocation of agent liability into a commercial agreement will be drafting into a vacuum, which is the most valuable and the most exposed position a transactional lawyer can occupy. Note also which body of law is doing the work: not a new AI statute but agency, tort and a 1986 anti-hacking act, applied by analogy until Congress or a court says otherwise.

Alibaba says it will give away the weights of a 2.4-trillion-parameter model next week

Alibaba released Qwen3.8-Max on Sunday evening, a model with 2.4 trillion total parameters, roughly 95 billion active, a one-million-token context window and the first multimodal input of any Qwen model above a trillion parameters. The announcement that matters is the second one: Alibaba says it will publish the of both Qwen3.8-Max and a small Qwen3.8-27B next week, the first time it has open-weighted a Max-class flagship after a run of proprietary top-tier releases. Its own benchmarks put the model above on several evaluations and comparable to or better than Anthropic's Fable 5. The coding-agent shop Cline measured a Terminal-Bench score two percent above Fable 5, and the model entered the Frontend Code Arena at fourth with a score of 1,668.

Two cautions travel with it. The licence does not exist yet, and a Max-class release is exactly where a company that has previously shipped Apache 2.0 might start bolting on usage restrictions. Why it mattersThe American argument for open weights is a national-security argument about avoiding single points of failure. The Chinese argument is a pricing argument, and pricing is winning: a frontier-class model given away and served at a third of its nearest rival's cost sets the floor that every closed lab's enterprise contract has to clear.

Two teams asked the same model the same open question and filed the same proof three hours apart

Two resolving the same open problem in reached arXiv on the same day. Prabhanjan Ananth of UC Santa Barbara and Amit Sahai of UCLA filed at 10:35 a.m. Pacific; Seyoon Ragavan, an MIT doctoral student, filed three hours and eighteen minutes later. Both papers credit OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra with finding the core ideas. Both groups had heard the same question posed earlier that month at the at Berkeley, and neither knew about the other until Yao-Ting Lin, a UCSB doctoral student, noticed the collision before either paper was public and emailed Ragavan: "We are definitely living in strange times."

The methods differed and the result did not. Ragavan drove Sol Ultra, which OpenAI says coordinates four agents in parallel by default, in two-hour stretches, checking and redirecting at each interval and then cleaning up the draft. and Sahai used a bespoke UCLA harness built to have models pursue and critique candidate solutions; their paper says the model produced the construction and the main proof ideas, and that the authors take responsibility for the claims. Ananth described the new default: "if someone mentions an open problem, the first thing is to see if GPT solves it. A lot of problems that we didn't know how to solve are going to get solved in the very near future." Ragavan, who had failed at the same problem himself a couple of years ago, said the compressed timeline was the disorienting part: "It's like two weeks and a day since this idea even formed." Neither paper is peer reviewed, and the groups have discussed merging them for conference submission.

Why it mattersPriority disputes are the oldest governance problem in science and they have always been resolved by the calendar. When two independent groups can reach the same construction in an afternoon by querying the same system, the calendar stops discriminating, and every institution that allocates credit by first-filing, from tenure committees to patent offices, inherits the problem.

States are clawing back the tax breaks that made a gigawatt of AI compute cheap

The Information reported Sunday evening that four states have rolled back or paused data-centre tax incentives and nine more are weighing repeal, a shift that could add seven percent or more to equipment costs and, in its estimate, several billion dollars to the price of every gigawatt of AI computing. The arithmetic is simple and brutal. In a state with a seven percent sales tax, killing the moves the IT-equipment cost of a campus from about $40 billion to about $43 billion. Per the , thirteen or fourteen states including Arizona, Connecticut, Georgia, Maryland, Michigan, Pennsylvania, Virginia and Texas have abolished, suspended or plan to suspend the exemption. Thirty-eight states currently offer some form of incentive and roughly two dozen considered rollback legislation this year.

The forms differ and the direction does not. Arizona enacted a three-year running from July 1, 2026 to June 30, 2029. North Carolina repealed the exemption on electricity purchases while preserving the equipment and capital-investment breaks, which targets the utility bill rather than the build. The driver is electoral rather than fiscal: backlash over electricity prices, water use, noise and disappointing tax receipts, with governors of both parties moving, and one person close to the shift observing that repeal "would've been unthinkable three years ago." The pressure is not American only. The Financial Times reported Sunday that London, Europe's largest data-centre hub, has more than twenty sites in development while Amsterdam has paused new construction until 2030, and that Irish households paid an estimated 715 million euros more for electricity between 2015 and 2023 because of data-centre expansion, with a further 1.43 billion possible by 2034.

Why it mattersEvery hyperscaler capex guide of the last two quarters was built on a subsidy the states are now withdrawing, and unlike power contracts or chip supply the exemption can vanish in a single legislative session. Watch the siting incentives in the announcements: a company that names its state before it names its gigawatts is telling you it has priced the risk.

Law · Brussels

As of yesterday Brussels can demand your model's source code and fine you three percent of worldwide revenue

Section 5 of Chapter IX of the EU AI Act became applicable on Sunday, and with it the stopped being a body that engages informally with model providers and became one that compels. Article 91 lets it require any provider to hand over technical documentation, training-data summaries and compliance materials, with incomplete or misleading responses themselves finable. Article 92 lets it commission evaluations by the Scientific Panel and request access to a model through APIs or other technical means, including source code. Article 93 lets it compel mitigation or, at the extreme, restrict, withdraw or recall a model from the EU market, and its third paragraph imports the from competition practice. Article 101 sets the ceiling at fifteen million euros or three percent of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

The same date switched on the transparency duties. Chatbots operating in Europe must now disclose that they are not human, deepfakes must be labelled, and AI-generated or altered content must carry machine-readable marks. The Commission published a list of more than 180 organisations that have signed the Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content, opened a complaints channel for downstream providers, and issued guidelines. Enforcement is shared between national market surveillance authorities, the AI Office, and the European Data Protection Supervisor where an EU institution is itself the provider. The point most commonly got wrong is what the did: it moved the Annex III high-risk deadlines to December 2, 2027 and August 2, 2028, and it did not move any of this.

Why it mattersThe design lesson is jurisdictional. Fines are calculated on global turnover and imposed by the Commission directly rather than by twenty-seven national regulators, which is the deliberate structural fix for the fragmentation that made GDPR enforcement slow. The first real tell will be whether the AI Office issues Article 91 requests to every Code signatory as a baseline, or waits for a failure.

Hadestown: The Musical grossed $2 million in its second weekend on 1,567 screens for a cumulative $18.2 million, passing Hamilton to become the highest-grossing theatrical release of a ever made. It opened to $10.2 million at 1,949 locations, itself a record, edging Hamilton's $10.1 million from September 2025. Disney's Hamilton release topped out at roughly $17 million domestic and about $4 million overseas.
