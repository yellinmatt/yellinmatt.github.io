Talks collapsed at midnight, and fifty percent tariffs went live on Saturday

Negotiations between the United States and Canada broke down late Friday, minutes before a midnight deadline and hours after President Trump told reporters the two sides had "pretty much" reached a deal. Tariffs of 50 percent took effect Saturday. The scope is reported two different ways by credible outlets and this brief prints both rather than choosing: Al Jazeera, France 24 and NPR size it near $20 billion of Canadian exports, while Bloomberg, Fox Business and Yahoo Finance report roughly $28 billion. The covered goods run to dairy, alcohol, wine, cement and hockey equipment. Energy, and critical minerals were carved out, which is the tell.

Prime Minister suspended negotiations and announced effective September 8, aimed at American steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics. At a Saturday press conference he accused Washington of using economic integration as a weapon, called the US demands "unfair, uneconomic," and said last-minute changes had "called into question the reliability of any deal."

"You're at war when you get attacked. We got attacked."
— Mark Carney, press conference, August 22

Trump's first public comment came Sunday: "Canada wants the benefits of being a State, without being one!!!" US Trade Representative said Washington had offered to cut steel, aluminum and auto tariffs and scrap a recent lumber duty, a package he described as the most preferential treatment of any trading partner, and said the administration was moving forward with measures responding to Canadian retaliation. The rupture lands as the mandatory joint review opens, on an agreement that currently moves more than 85 percent of Canada-US trade duty-free.

Why it mattersTariff risk stops being disclosure boilerplate the moment a review clause and a retaliation calendar collide on the same page. Every cross-border issuer with a Canadian supply chain now has a September 8 date to write into its risk factors, and the USMCA review that was supposed to be housekeeping has become the leverage.

Framing noteThe Washington Post headlined the collapse as showing "the limits of Trump's aggressive trade strategy"; Fox Business carried Greer's line that the rejected offer would have given Canada "the most preferential treatment of any trading partner." Identical timeline, opposite party at fault.

The bear case on the largest offering ever attempted arrived the same day as the number

Corporate spending on , Anthropic's largest and priciest model, has plateaued at roughly 11 percent of total outlay on the company's tools, the Financial Times reported Sunday, citing data from across a sample of about 70,000 companies. Opus 5, the cheaper tier released July 24, has passed Fable 5 in spending share. The FT frames this as breaking a pattern of corporate users defaulting to the most powerful model, and notes that if it holds it "could radically alter the business model of frontier labs."

Two pieces of corroboration and one live dispute. Anthropic made Sonnet 5's introductory pricing permanent on August 10, abandoning a planned September 1 increase, which is what price pressure looks like from the seller's side; Gavin Baker of Atreides posted Vercel data showing token share moving from 28 to 62 percent in two months. Against that, Jeremiah Dillon publicly disputed the FT's read, arguing Ramp's model-mix figures come only from its token spend-management product rather than the full customer sample. The story landed hours after the New York Times reported bankers floating a raise above $100 billion at up to a $2 trillion valuation, which this brief led with yesterday.

Why it mattersThis is the diligence question on the largest IPO ever attempted, published in the same news cycle as the price. If customers are routing to the cheapest adequate model rather than the best one, the revenue line that supports a $2 trillion mark is a commodity line, and the risk factors in a prospectus filed within weeks will have to say so.

Shein finally came to market, at roughly a quarter of what it was once worth

Shein Global Holdings launched its Hong Kong offering Monday, marketing about 280 million Class B shares at HK$47.60 to HK$49.50 to raise up to HK$13.9 billion, or roughly $1.8 billion. At the top of the range the equity is valued near $27 billion, against the $98.2 billion at which the company was privately marked in 2022. That is a decline of about 72 percent, and it is the price of a four-year venue migration: a New York listing abandoned under supply-chain and US-China scrutiny, a London attempt stalled on regulatory and political grounds, a confidential Hong Kong filing in July 2025, and clearance in July of this year.

Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and JPMorgan are . Roughly $400 million was reserved for reported to include Boyu Capital, Tiger Global, General Atlantic, Tencent, Taikang Life and UBS Asset Management Singapore. Pricing is set for August 31 and trading for September 1, a debut the South China Morning Post reports was pushed into September because investor orders came in slower than planned.

Why it mattersA 72 percent haircut between the last private mark and the first public one is the clearest available statement that private valuations are marketing and public valuations are underwriting. For anyone who will spend a summer papering offerings, this is the deal to read: sponsor liability, disclosed anchors, and a venue chosen for what would clear rather than what would price best.

Corporate · Hangzhou

Alibaba launched a $10.2 billion placement on a Sunday and the stock fell 9 percent

Alibaba launched a HK$80 billion, or $10.2 billion, on Sunday, expressly to fund artificial-intelligence development, offering 710 million shares at a 3.6 percent discount to Friday's close. Launching a marketed equity deal on a Sunday is a deliberate structural choice: it front-runs the Monday Asian open and compresses the book-building window. Livemint reports it as the third-largest such offering, after Alphabet and Intel. The stock fell about 9 percent.

The reaction is the lesson. A 3.6 percent discount is tight, which is underwriters signaling they expected strong demand, and the market answered with a drop nearly three times the concession. criticized the raise publicly, and Fortune tied his objection to a simultaneous purchase of JD.com. The deal lands in the same 48 hours as SoftBank's record retail bond and the Anthropic listing chatter, three distinct capital-formation structures all funding the same compute.

Why it mattersDilution is the honest price of a capex arms race, and Sunday's tape put a number on it. When a company has to sell 9 percent of its market value to raise 3 percent of it, the equity market is telling you it does not believe the return on the spend.

Corporate · Tokyo

SoftBank turns to Japanese households for a record trillion-yen bond

SoftBank Group plans a one trillion yen sale, roughly $6.3 billion, Bloomberg reported Monday. It is the largest retail bond offering by any issuer in Japan, and SoftBank's third bond issue this year. The stated purpose is funding the group's OpenAI commitments, which include a twenty-year, ten-gigawatt Ohio data center deal announced August 17 through SB Energy, on which Nvidia agreed to a portion of the completed facility's value.

The channel is the story. Institutional buyers price covenants, negotiate terms and can refuse; Japanese retail savers, facing decades of near-zero deposit yields, are the demand pool most likely to fund a leveraged bet on compute at a coupon the issuer chooses. It is being raised into a global long end that has not cooperated all month: the US 30-year touched 5.323 percent on August 18, its highest since 2007, and closed the week near 5.27 with two-year-to-thirty-year at roughly 104 basis points.

Why it mattersFollow the funding sequence and you can see the risk being pushed outward. The compute obligation sits with OpenAI, the balance sheet with SoftBank, the loss backstop with Nvidia, and the cash with Japanese households. Each hop is legitimate; the aggregate is a chain in which the party bearing the funding risk is the one furthest from the underwriting.

Law · South Florida · Miami

A Miami-Dade circuit seat is still unresolved this morning, by 572 votes

Florida's August 18 primary decided most of the state's judicial races, and Miami-Dade's bench came out rearranged. Incumbent Circuit Judge Mavel Ruiz was defeated outright by Destiny Goede Alvarez, a 30-year-old attorney. Incumbent Circuit Judge Luis Perez-Medina trails challenger Maribel Diaz by 572 votes, a margin of 0.26 percent, which triggers a mandatory under Fla. Stat. section 102.141. Diaz is an attorney with the Miami-Dade County Tax Collector's Office. A county court runoff between Rita Baez and Bonita Jones-Peabody goes to November, one of seven judicial runoffs statewide.

The pattern is the one local bar leaders have complained about for years: sitting circuit judges displaced by low-profile challengers in a summer primary that almost nobody votes in. It runs alongside the appointment side of the same pipeline, where on August 7 the Florida Supreme Court denied retired Judge Kevin Emas's mandamus petition against Governor DeSantis as moot after a July 10 appointment, with two justices writing separately over whether the 60-day appointment deadline in is judicially enforceable at all.

Why it mattersThese are the judges who will hear Miami commercial litigation for the next six years, decided by a few hundred votes in August. In a market this small, knowing which circuit seats just turned over is worth more than knowing the national docket, and today the answer is still pending.

Bessent calls it an economic D-Day, and oil sells the news

Treasury Secretary published a Financial Times op-ed over the weekend declaring that "at dawn begins an economic D-Day, the single greatest financial offensive ever marshaled against an adversary," and scheduled a 2 p.m. Eastern press conference Monday to release specifics. Trump posted "ECONOMIC D-DAY," warning that any country doing business with Iran faces tremendous economic consequences. The architecture is rather than new primary designations: exposure for foreign purchasers of Iranian crude, banks, shipping companies, ship registries and third-country government entities.

The pressure point is China, which buys more than 80 percent of Iran's seaborne crude on data, and Beijing has said it rejects unilateral sanctions. Tehran answered Monday: ships violating transit rules face detention or confiscation, and security chief Mohsen Rezaei threatened to halt all Gulf oil flow if neighboring states join the crackdown. Markets went the other way. WTI fell about 1.62 percent to $85.65 and Brent 1.38 percent to $93.09, which is what happens when the announcement is already in the price.

Why it mattersSanctions design is a first-order transactional workstream, not a foreign-policy footnote. Every cross-border offering document carries a sanctions representation, and a secondary-sanctions regime aimed at Chinese refiners and their banks changes what diligence has to establish before a deal can price.

Framing noteThe Washington Post ran it as a crushing campaign aimed at "a country that has so far withstood nearly five decades of punishing American sanctions"; Fox News carried Bessent's "greatest coordinated economic isolation in the history of the world" without hedge. The delta is efficacy, not fact.

Congo's outbreak is now the deadliest on record there, and the fatality rate has doubled since June

The first 16,250 doses of arrived at Kinshasa's N'djili airport, the opening tranche of roughly 70,000 allocated by the WHO and partners. Twenty thousand are earmarked for a Phase 3 trial to establish whether the vaccine protects against at all, a question with no human answer; the other 50,000 go to frontline and health workers. As of August 19 the outbreak stood at 5,290 confirmed cases and 2,516 deaths, a near 47.5 percent, up from roughly 20 percent in June.

It has passed the 2,299 deaths of the 2018 to 2020 epidemic to become Congo's deadliest, spread across 54 health zones in six provinces, and the WHO says it is on track to surpass the 2014 to 2016 West Africa outbreak. Sunday's reporting identified the obstacle: artisanal gold miners in , highly mobile, working insecure and hard-to-reach terrain with no hand-washing stations, who make contact tracing nearly impossible. Epidemiologists now believe the virus was circulating in the mining town of Mongbwalu as early as February, three months before the outbreak was declared.

Why it mattersA fatality rate that doubles mid-outbreak is not a fact about the pathogen; it is a measurement of a response losing control. And the vaccine arriving is not a solution but an experiment, which is its own live problem in research ethics conducted under emergency conditions.

Bankers have a new pitch for sports franchises: they are a hedge against AI

CNN's Chris Isidore reports that the franchise boom now has a stated rationale from the bankers selling into it: teams are being marketed as AI-proof assets. , who has banked franchise sales for thirty years, put it plainly: teams used to be bought "like fine art," for scarcity and ego, and "now it's a hedge against technology disruption." The ladder underneath the claim is real. Bob Iger and Josh Kushner agreed this month to buy control of the Lakers at $12.5 billion; the NFL moved closer to approving a $9.6 billion Seahawks sale; MLB approved the Padres at $3.9 billion; the Timberwolves and Lynx sold Friday at $4.5 billion; the Yankees took $2.6 billion from Apollo; and Fenway Sports Group reportedly sold 40 percent of Liverpool to a consortium including Jeff Bezos.

Economist Victor Matheson supplies the counterweight, and it is the oldest one there is: "You can afford to overpay as long as you believe that there will be people in the future willing to overpay by at least as much or more." Two structural drivers are named. Streaming entrants are bidding up rights for the last live content audiences will still watch advertising against, and legal wagering since now routes as much as a billion dollars a year to teams and leagues.

Why it mattersAn asset class does not get repriced because its cash flows changed; it gets repriced because the story about its cash flows changed. Calling a franchise AI-proof is a claim about scarcity of attention, and it is being made at the exact moment institutional capital needs somewhere to sit that is not compute.

Sony's disc-less endgame drew its first organized boycott on Sunday night

The PSBlackout protest began Sunday at 7 p.m. local time and runs a full week, asking participants to withhold logins, play sessions and purchases across all Sony platforms. It was organized by , a game-preservation group, in response to Sony's July 1 announcement that it will end physical disc production for all new PlayStation games from January 2028. The related petition passed 345,000 signatures, up from about 120,000 in early July. Sony is separately closing the PS3 store in some markets this year, with global PS3 and Vita closures in 2027.

The legal stake is , and the timing did Sony no favors: on August 22, days into the backlash, the company pushed terms-of-service reminders to PSN users stating that they do not own their digital games. DoesItPlay's grievance list runs wider than discs, naming the closure of Bluepoint Games, a live-service strategy it calls misguided, and the abandonment of PS VR2. Sony has declined to respond across its social channels.

Why it mattersThis is the consumer-protection front of the license-versus-ownership fight, and it is now organized rather than ambient. California's AB 2426 already bars advertising a revocable license as "buying"; a coordinated seven-day boycott is the market testing whether the doctrine has any commercial teeth behind it.

Culture · Los Angeles

The film Warner Bros. buried for a $30 million write-down opens on Friday

Deadline published cast interviews Sunday with Will Forte and Lana Condor, who describe having held out hope after a "freakin' awesome" funeral screening for a picture nobody expected to see released. Warner Bros. shelved the completed Coyote vs. Acme in November 2023 to claim an estimated $30 million , the same maneuver used on Batgirl. After public backlash the studio let the filmmakers shop it, entered talks in March 2025 and bought it for a reported $50 million, then raised a $100 million to release it wide.

It premiered at San Diego Comic-Con on July 25 and opens Friday, August 28, directed by Dave Green and written by , from Ian Frazier's 1990 New Yorker piece structured as a product-liability complaint by the Coyote against Acme. It is reported at 94 percent on Rotten Tomatoes off first reactions.

Why it mattersFriday is the live test of whether shelving a finished film for tax benefit destroys recoverable value. If a rescued write-off opens well on an independent's borrowed marketing money, the accounting treatment that produced it starts to look less like prudence and more like an impairment of the asset itself.
