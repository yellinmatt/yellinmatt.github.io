The Splash · News · Washington and Tehran · October 24–25 · cross-spectrum LCR

Trump says a strike on Iran could come “maybe tomorrow,” and Tehran says no American warship will enter the Gulf

President Trump told reporters on Saturday that a renewed attack on Iran could come “maybe tomorrow,” and in the same remarks floated “a nice, big, beautiful agreement,” the Indian outlet The Federal reported after he voted early in Florida. Al Jazeera’s live coverage on Sunday carried both an attack as soon as Sunday and a major deal as live options. As of 7:13 a.m. Eastern on Sunday, CBS News reported no American strike, and noted that Trump has also said strikes would wait until after the Nov. 3 midterms.

Tehran answered in kind. “We will not allow U.S. military ships in the Persian Gulf under any circumstances and at any time,” said Ali Abdollahi, chief of staff of the armed forces. said Sunday that a renewed war would not change Iran’s positions. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton, in a to Secretary of State Marco Rubio reported by CBS late Friday, wrote that Iran has “substantially increased efforts to target U.S. personnel.”

The price is already on the screen. Brent settled at $116.72 on Friday after briefly topping $117, its highest since March, and the energy sector closed at a record. The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a late Saturday, and the updated its regional warnings. An IRGC spokesman called the American threats “largely for media consumption.”

Whether there is war or not, our policies remain the same.
Abbas Araghchi, via CBS News, October 25

Why it mattersOil near $117 already prices a war that has not restarted, so the weekend is a binary trade: a strike lifts the premium again, and a deal unwinds the one that carried energy stocks to a record on Friday.

Elvis’s rightsholders sue Anthropic over 274 songs, saying Claude still reproduces lyrics past its guardrails

A subsidiary of , which controls much of Elvis Presley’s intellectual property, sued Anthropic on Friday with other holders of rights in his music, claiming the company trained its models on 274 Presley compositions and lyrics without permission. The songs include “Can’t Help Falling in Love” and “Don’t Be Cruel.” The plaintiffs seek of up to $150,000 per song, about $41.1 million, NME reported.

The output claim is the sharper one. The complaint says Claude kept reproducing lyrics after Anthropic , and that asked to recreate songs “for a modern audience” it produced near-copies. Lead counsel said Anthropic built its business “based on copyright infringement at a scale never seen before.” Anthropic has not responded and has argued that training is fair use. Conflict disclosed: this paper is compiled with Anthropic’s model.

Why it mattersFair use may protect what a model learned, but a model that still recites lyrics on request hands plaintiffs a cleaner infringement case with a per-song multiplier attached.

Law · San Francisco · October 24–25 · cross-spectrum LLC

The Ninth Circuit sits on a Sunday to hear the bid to set aside 20,000 Los Angeles mail ballots

A of Judges Mark Bennett, Patrick Bumatay and Gabriel Sanchez ordered argument “via Zoom on Sunday, October 25, 2026, at 1 p.m. PDT” in United States v. California, the suit to segregate ballots from about 20,000 voters as likely noncitizens. The department appealed after Judge David O. Carter did not rule by its noon Oct. 22 deadline: “There is no un-ringing the bell,” its motion said.

California says there is nothing to appeal, and that carries no civil remedy. On Friday the department wrote to at least 18 counties; San Bernardino’s letter listed 1,133 people. “Any election official who knowingly processes the ballot of a noncitizen is opening themselves to legal jeopardy,” wrote Bill Essayli, the first assistant U.S. attorney. Carter’s own hearing is Tuesday.

Why it mattersWhether a federal court will stop a state count on the strength of an agency list, nine days before an election, will be cited in every county that got a letter.

AI · October 24 · single-tier coverage: C

Forced to pick, Grok chose the Republican in 92 percent of midterm races and GPT the Democrat in 97 percent

Researchers at Convovo, a studio led by Cornell professor Tapan Parikh, asked and GPT-6.1 Sol, with search on, in nearly 500 House, Senate and governor races, telling them not to “hedge, waffle, or list both sides,” TechCrunch reported Saturday. They agreed in fewer than 10 percent of races. “For GPT, it has mostly decided already…it’s in the weights. For Grok, it really is the search pattern,” Parikh said. OpenAI said ChatGPT “is designed to be objective by default”; did not respond. The study is not independently replicated.

Why it mattersIt separates two biases a regulator would treat differently, one trained into the weights and one imported live from search, and neither is visible to the voter asking.
