A $700 million bid for a 500-lawyer firm routes around the rule that says nonlawyers cannot own one

Wood Smith Henning & Berman, a Los Angeles-founded insurance defense firm with more than 500 lawyers across 43 offices in 35 states plus London, has signed a letter of intent to sell part of itself to private equity. The buyer is , which manages roughly $22 billion and was spun out of Harvard's endowment operation in 1998. The reported enterprise value is about $700 million, which works out to roughly eighteen times the firm's adjusted EBITDA of $38.2 million. Wood Smith ran an auction for outside investment in the non-legal side of the business before landing on Charlesbank, and a definitive agreement is expected within weeks. If it closes it is the largest private-equity investment in an American law firm to date, and the first completed deal after months of reported flirtation between Am Law 100 firms and outside money.

The vehicle is a . The investor owns and operates billing, technology, human resources and back-office administration; the lawyers retain formal ownership and control of the entity that practices law. That does not repeal , which bars fee-sharing with nonlawyers and nonlawyer ownership of an entity practicing law, and which every US jurisdiction has adopted in some form except Arizona and, narrowly, the District of Columbia. It routes around it, on the assertion that the investor owns nothing that practices law. The structure is lifted almost verbatim from healthcare, where management companies have coexisted with corporate-practice-of-medicine bans for thirty years. The live ethics question is Rule 5.4(c) and Rule 1.8(f): who actually directs professional judgment when a third party owns the profit and loss, the client-intake software, the matter-management system and the compensation infrastructure. Owning the billing system is a short distance from owning the billing decisions.

The first concrete result of the flirtation the country's richest firms have been carrying on with outside money.
— Above the Law, August 20

Insurance defense is not an accident as the first target. Panel-counsel rates, commoditized volume work, thin margins and standardized work product describe the practice area generative AI compresses fastest, so Charlesbank is buying the operational layer of the segment with the most cost left to squeeze. The number that travels furthest, though, is the multiple. Eighteen times EBITDA prices a law firm the way a buyer prices a services roll-up, and partnership equity has never carried a public multiple before. It has one now, and every managing partner who has said no to outside capital has just been handed a comparable to argue against.

Why it mattersThe ethics fight will be fought over Rule 5.4 and it will probably be won on the paperwork, because the paperwork was designed for that. The consequential change is the comparable. Once a partnership has a number, every subsequent conversation about outside capital starts from a valuation rather than a principle.

Anthropic will let enterprises keep the thirty days of retained data on their own cloud

Bloomberg reported on Thursday, and Reuters confirmed the same afternoon, that Anthropic will give enterprise customers materially more control over their data on its frontier models. The thirty-day retention requirement stays, but business customers get the option to hold the data on their own cloud infrastructure rather than Anthropic's, delivered through a new safety system the company expects to ship later this year. The changes have been in development for months, built with more than a hundred customers including Salesforce, the only one named. The policy being softened dates to June, when Anthropic said it would require thirty-day retention of all enterprise traffic on its models and future frontier systems, to guard against cyberattacks staged through its technology. Reuters named the competitive trigger without hedging: on Wednesday OpenAI announced a safety system that avoids retaining customer data while still identifying misuse.

The legal mechanism is what makes this more than a privacy nicety. Federal courts spent the first quarter of 2026 writing zero retention directly into protective orders. In Morgan v. V2X, decided in the District of Colorado on March 30, the court barred parties from putting confidential material into any AI platform unless the provider is contractually prohibited from storing or training on inputs and from disclosing them, and must permit deletion on request. In , five days earlier in the District of Kansas, the court banned public AI tools for all discovery material, confidential or not, and required closed enterprise systems. Under that case law a mandatory thirty-day retention sitting on the vendor's own infrastructure is close to disqualifying for any regulated or in-litigation enterprise, and is the compliance workaround rather than a favour. named as a design partner is the tell: this was negotiated by the customers who could not sign under the June terms.

Why it mattersCustody is not the same thing as protection. Moving the logs onto a client's own tenancy answers the protective-order problem and quietly creates a new one, because a log the client controls is a log the client can be ordered to produce.

Santander closed on Webster for $12.3 billion using a statutory device Delaware does not have

Webster Financial filed an 8-K on Thursday announcing completion of its acquisition by Banco Santander under a transaction agreement dated February 3. The aggregate value is about $12.3 billion measured on Santander's February 2 closing price, with Webster holders receiving $48.75 in cash plus 2.0548 Santander per share. It is Santander's first American retail bank acquisition in roughly seventeen years and the third-largest US bank deal since 2010. The approval sequence ran Webster stockholders on May 26, the OCC on June 12, the European Central Bank on July 21 and the Federal Reserve Board on August 4, four regulators across two jurisdictions in six months.

The structure is the part worth copying. Webster first merged into a wholly owned Virginia subsidiary in a , and Santander then acquired every share of that entity through a . A share exchange is a Model Business Corporation Act creature Delaware does not offer: it binds all shareholders to exchange by operation of law without merging the entity out of existence, so the bank charters, licences, contracts and deposit franchise survive intact and no change-of-control or assignment clause fires. The reincorporation is a means rather than an end, existing only to unlock the statute. What the record does not disclose is whether Webster holders had any collar or walk-away right against six months of Spanish equity and exchange-rate risk, which is the first question a student should ask about a mixed-consideration deal that took half a year to close. Webster ran a substantial communications programme through the pendency, covering consumer and commercial customers as well as investors.

Why it mattersThe ECB signed off two weeks before the Fed did. Cross-border bank deals live or die on that sequencing, and this one is now the working template for the next European bank that wants an American deposit base.

Law & the Courts · Atlanta · August 20

The Eleventh Circuit vacates the order that broke up the Delta and Aeroméxico joint venture

In Delta Air Lines and Aerovías de México v. U.S. Department of Transportation, No. 25-13546, a published opinion issued Thursday, the Eleventh Circuit held that DOT's September 2025 final order terminating approval of the carriers' and withdrawing its was on two independent grounds, and vacated it. Judge Branch wrote for the court, joined in full by Judge Tjoflat, with Judge Rosenbaum concurring in the judgment only. On the first ground, DOT rested the order on competitive conditions at Mexico City's Benito Juárez alone: "The final order analyzed none of the 1,687 city-pairs in the U.S.-Mexico market, let alone engaged in a comprehensive share shift analysis like DOT did in 2016," and that airport is only 21 percent of US-Mexico flights. On the second, DOT treated Mexican compliance with the as a necessary precondition while having "approved two Japanese joint ventures despite the lack of an open skies agreement that included Haneda," and it "does not contest that it held the petitioners to a higher standard than other joint venture applicants."

The doctrine is the consistency line running from State Farm: an agency may change course but not abruptly and without explanation, and it must treat like cases alike. Rosenbaum's concurrence is the more interesting document. She would hold that granting a new joint venture and withdrawing an existing one "are not both apples," so the majority is comparing the wrong universe and identifies "no such precedent" from which DOT departed. She reaches the same result on the Haneda inconsistency alone, because DOT's claim that open-skies compliance was necessary "was wrong," and she pins the concession to the transcript, noting DOT admitted the Haneda carveout at oral argument. The practical effect is that a decade-old revenue-shared alliance survives, the court having already stayed the order pending review, and DOT would have to start again with the country-pair analysis it skipped.

Why it mattersThis is an anti-deference outcome under a standard designed to defer, and the reason is procedural rather than substantive. The cheapest way for an agency to lose is still to fail to explain why this applicant is different from the last one it waved through.
