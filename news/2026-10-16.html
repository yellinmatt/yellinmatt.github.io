The FTC subpoenas OpenAI and Anthropic over safety incidents, and plans to put their chiefs under oath

The sent to OpenAI and Anthropic on Wednesday, Axios reported, each running more than 50 pages. They seek documents on security incidents and safeguarding practices, plus marketing materials and the documents that describe each company’s privacy policies. The commission plans to compel the labs’ top executives to testify, and demands to third-party AI organizations will follow.

“We will engage constructively with the FTC.”
OpenAI spokesperson, to Axios, October 15

The probe is a consumer-protection case: the agency is testing whether the labs’ conduct was . Chair co-chairs the White House , which was set to hold its first meeting on Thursday after ordering industry-wide notification and remediation of incidents. That order followed Anthropic’s disclosure that its agents had made “fraudulent” use of government websites during testing. Ferguson has argued that AI systems should not be released if they are not safe. The demands themselves are not a finding of wrongdoing.

OpenAI said it would “engage constructively.” Anthropic gave Axios no comment, but on the same day it said six evaluators from METR, the Berkeley testing nonprofit, now work inside the company with employee-level access, and that “Anthropic can’t veto publication” of what they find. Redactions are limited to narrow categories such as customer data and model architecture, SecureBio is reviewing the chemical and biological chapter of its August risk report, and Anthropic said “every frontier AI company should do the same.” The same Wednesday, a group of state attorneys general sued OpenAI under their consumer-protection laws.

Why it mattersThis is the first federal compulsory process against the frontier labs, landing as Anthropic courts investors for a November listing. Whatever the demands surface becomes risk-factor disclosure, and sworn executive testimony is the record securities plaintiffs later quote.

Law · Brooklyn

A federal judge throws out the Trump administration’s suit against New York City’s sanctuary laws

Judge Ramón Reyes Jr. dismissed United States v. New York City on Thursday, holding that the government lacked standing and that, “even if the United States has standing, and it does not,” its preemption claims fail. Its theory that the city’s laws impede enforcement is “nothing more than a backdoor around the Tenth Amendment,” he wrote, invoking the principle.

The city’s limits on holds were already required by state law, Reuters reported, and no federal statute obliges localities to share release dates. The policies date to Mayor Ed Koch in 1989; the suit followed the July 2025 shooting of an off-duty customs officer in a Manhattan park. Mayor Zohran Mamdani praised the ruling, no appeal has been announced, and the Justice Department did not immediately respond.

Why it mattersPreemption was the government’s main tool against sanctuary cities, and this ruling gives the Tenth Amendment priority over it. Expect an appeal to the Second Circuit.
