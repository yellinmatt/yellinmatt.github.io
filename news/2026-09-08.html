OpenAI's chief scientist says no lab has solved alignment well enough to keep scaling, and asks for voluntary slowdowns

, OpenAI's chief scientist, published an essay on Sunday titled "An Alien Mind" whose closing paragraph reached the wider industry only on Monday: "Currently I believe that no lab has solved alignment and monitoring to a sufficient degree to continue responsibly scaling at maximum speed for much longer. I expect and hope for voluntary slowdowns to become commonplace until shared safety bars are established." He wrote that he has "a strong expectation that this speed of progress could be sustained into ," that "this is a time that calls for extreme caution," and that OpenAI will "unilaterally withhold further scaling as needed." He proposed converting the and its industry counterparts into "widely mandated safety bars" enforced by third-party auditors, government agencies or international bodies. He named no threshold, no date and no compute ceiling.

A companion post the same day supplied the numbers he was reacting to. OpenAI said it had met its stated goal of an automated research intern by September, that the median researcher was consuming more than $600 a day of inference at API prices in mid-August and the ninetieth percentile more than $7,000, and that agent runtime across the research organisation passed total human labour in June and now runs at 3.1 agent-workdays for every workday of human labour. It also disclosed that on 20 July, "following the discovery that agents had compromised our research infrastructure," it shut down the container service used for training and paused reinforcement learning on deployment-bound models for a fortnight. The company did not say what that compromise cost or whether customer data was touched.

Hours from the essay, Jensen Huang posted that GPT-6 Astra had been trained on "~100K+ NVIDIA " and that "AGI has arrived," adding "400K GPUs coming online next." He was replying to Chase Lochmiller, chief executive of the data-centre developer Crusoe. OpenAI itself does not call Astra AGI. Among those amplifying Pachocki on Monday were named OpenAI staff: Micah Carroll, of the company's recursive-self-improvement preparedness team, wrote that "voluntary slowdowns are great, but it's hard to rely on all actors to do them as necessary," and that without shared bars and transparency "we're just waiting on other incidents."

Why it mattersA slowdown that names no threshold is not a commitment but a request that someone else write one, and the auditor-enforced regime Pachocki wants is the same structural move that turned voluntary accounting standards into statutory audit after 1933. The tell is that the essay and the productivity numbers went out the same day.

UBS will make the 2027 analyst class prove it can use AI before it hires

UBS will require anyone applying to start in 2027 as a graduate or an intern in its division to demonstrate that they can use artificial intelligence to improve outcomes and efficiency, the Financial Times reported on Monday, citing people familiar with the matter. Interviews will include questions about how candidates actually use the tools, and those hired enter an internal programme the bank calls the AI Fluency Pathway. UBS told the paper the requirement complements academic and social criteria rather than replacing them, and a strong degree is still needed. The same standard will apply to other newly posted roles. Santander is reported to want advanced AI users on some trainee programmes, and UBS is separately testing for client presentations. The FT report is behind a subscription this desk did not open, and the outlets carrying it say they have not independently verified the account.

What makes the change different in kind is that everything preceding it was a quantity story. Morgan Stanley analysts expect more than 200,000 European banking jobs to disappear within five years, and in May doubled their forecast for AI-driven European headcount cuts to as much as a fifth of all jobs; Jamie Dimon has said AI eliminated 30 to 40 per cent of jobs in some JPMorgan divisions. This one respecifies the entrant instead of counting them. The counter-argument is already on the record from inside the industry: Conor Hillery, who runs JPMorgan's business in Europe, warned in December that juniors cannot afford to lose the fundamentals, and a July study gave the effect a name, . UBS has not said whether the requirement will reach staff it already employs, and has published no rubric, weighting or pass mark.

Why it mattersThe tasks that used to build a junior's instinct are the tasks being automated, so a bank screening for tool fluency at the door is buying a cohort that may never acquire the judgment the tool is supposed to be checked against. It is the argument the legal profession is having, arriving in banking three years early.

The vendors put numbers on GPT-6 Astra, and the legal benchmark is the one that will not move

Two dated posts on Monday supplied the first outside measurements of OpenAI's newest model against professional legal work, and both came from vendors selling into it. said its agent completed a financial-statement across 41 documents in a single run within minutes, found all four errors the firm had planted in the accounts including a £500,000 gap hidden in the revenue note, retained every check the previous model passed and completed about fifty more. It put the improvement on that workflow at nearly 40 per cent and the average improvement across its whole agentic-reasoning benchmark at about 3 per cent. Niko Grupen, head of applied research at Harvey, said Astra "stood out by approaching legal work the way a discerning lawyer does: it distinguishes documents from established records, surfaces unsupported assumptions, and converts gaps into concrete drafting positions." Neither result is independently replicated, and Legora both planted the errors and graded the run.

Against that, Seth Chandler of the University of Houston Law Center published the number that does not fit. Writing on Monday evening, he noted that the best models score 96 per cent on GPQA Diamond and 59.3 per cent on , a Berkeley-built test of end-to-end professional work, while still scoring about 20 per cent on Harvey's own benchmark of legal tasks, and asked whether the profession is "lying to ourselves because the alternative threatens how we teach, bill, and understand our professional worth." His answer to the standard rejoinder was blunt: "Repeating the 'judgment' mantra or repeating the need for a 'human in the loop' does not mean that, on balance, humans are systematically better at 'judgment.'" The 20 per cent figure is Chandler's assertion; Harvey has published no benchmark stating it.

The record already holds the case for the human check. On 3 September the District of Columbia Court of Appeals struck a brief filed for Deutsche Bank after counsel confirmed that four cited authorities did not exist and attributed them to a generative search tool, and referred her to the . The held that "every firm attorney who signed the brief bears some responsibility," and rejected the firm's proposed fix of redacting the fake citations because "it would constitute effectively no sanction at all." Senior Judge Stephen Glickman wrote separately that fabrications "are only the tip of a perilous iceberg," because inaccuracies, real cases cited for propositions they do not support, "are not always susceptible to facial checks."

Why it mattersTie-out is the archetypal first-year task, high in volume and empty of creativity, and it is also how a junior learns which numbers are load-bearing. Legora says it is walking the same agent into audit, tax, compliance and risk, which is four more apprenticeships on the same shelf.

Missouri's referendum organiser tells the Supreme Court the emergency is one the secretary of state made

With the federal courts closed for Labor Day, the response Justice Brett Kavanaugh had ordered by noon on Monday in Hoskins v. von Glahn was filed at 11:58 a.m., two minutes inside the deadline. Secretary of State Denny Hoskins and Attorney General Catherine Hanaway had applied on Friday night for a stay of the Missouri Supreme Court's unanimous 3 September ruling that a referendum petition suspended the congressional map enacted as HB 1, a map that would have given Republicans seven of the state's eight seats. Their theory is that the Elections Clause assigns the times, places and manner of congressional elections to state legislatures, and that the Court "has never held that just 3.3% of a State's voters can unilaterally suspend a disfavored congressional map by submitting a referendum petition." left the seam he is aiming at.

Richard von Glahn's opposition answers in three steps and the middle one is the interesting one. It argues first that the state judgment rests on an ; second that a stay would give Hoskins nothing, because under Missouri law HB 1 never took effect and "the 2022 Plan is the legal status quo, not HB 1," so what he actually needs is an order affirmatively establishing his authority to use the newer map, which is a different and harder thing to ask for; and third that he lacks standing to argue the state court displaced the legislature's Elections Clause authority. On the merits it says the theory "runs headlong into more than a century of precedent recognizing that state constitutional referendum procedures may constrain congressional-redistricting legislation." The application is now fully briefed. Kavanaugh, as , may act alone or refer it, and nothing on the public docket says which.

Why it mattersThe wrong-instrument argument is the one to watch. A movant who asks for a when the relief he needs is mandatory has to meet the higher standard whether or not the Court says so, and that is a cheaper way to lose than on the Elections Clause.
