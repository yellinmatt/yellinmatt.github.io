The Splash · The World · Washington and Riyadh · cross-spectrum LLLC

Trump calls off the Iran strikes after a phone call from Riyadh, and Tehran calls the deal a lie

President Trump said late Saturday that the United States would hold off on new strikes against Iran, hours after Saudi Crown Prince telephoned him to argue against them. In a post carrying the usual maximalism, Trump wrote that the country "is locked and loaded" to strike with "military terror, strength, and power not seen since World War II," and then, in the same breath, that Washington had "just been asked by Iran, and other Middle Eastern countries, to hold off any attack." He said the pause was "subject to being able to rapidly make a deal," described its terms as the immediate and total opening of the and an end to Iran's nuclear threat, and claimed Israel had joined the commitment.

The Saudi Press Agency confirmed the call, and a White House official confirmed it to the Associated Press without describing what was said. A source told Axios that the crown prince "urged Trump to de-escalate and refrain from launching the strikes." The Saudi logic is not sentimental. Tehran has been explicit about where it would answer an attack on its own energy infrastructure: Nournews, tied to Iran's , said on Saturday that American strikes would draw Iranian fire on oilfields in Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates and gasfields in Qatar and Israel, and Fars News published a target list covering six countries. A Gulf client state, in other words, exercised a veto over its patron's war plan by pointing at its own refineries.

Whether any of it holds is a separate question. Iranian military officials told state media that Trump's claim Tehran had asked for a halt was a "lie," accused him of trying to extort Gulf leaders, and said forces were at their highest readiness. Israel's Channel 12 reported that Foreign Minister had accepted a Qatari and American compromise under which ships would enter the Gulf on the Iranian-controlled side and leave by the Omani one, with Oman waiting on guarantees the Revolutionary Guard will not sink the arrangement, and Iranian officials denied that too. The violence that framed the weekend was not hypothetical. Kuwaiti air defences intercepted Iranian drones on Saturday, Iran said it had struck aircraft shelters and satellite equipment at Ahmad al-Jaber Air Base in answer to an American strike on a house on Qeshm Island that killed three members of one family, and logged the Bermuda-flagged tanker GASLOG SHANGHAI taking a projectile in its engine room eleven nautical miles off Limah, Oman.

"We have just been asked by Iran, and other Middle Eastern countries, to hold off any attack."

Why it mattersThe five months since February have taught the oil market to price Hormuz as a political variable rather than a physical one, and this weekend it moved on a phone call rather than a fleet. Note who made the call: the Senate could not pass a war powers resolution on Thursday, failing 49 to 50, and the constraint that finally bit came from a foreign sovereign protecting its own balance sheet. That is worth remembering the next time anyone models sanctions risk as a function of American politics alone.

Framing noteThe same facts split cleanly. The New York Times ran "In Iran, the U.S. Appears Headed for a Strategic Defeat, Allies Fear" and the Guardian called the halt Trump's "latest climbdown from extravagant threats," while Fox News led its weekend file with a former CENTCOM deputy describing Iran as "desperate" as the United States dismantles its drone and missile network. Nobody disputes the call, the post or the drones.

AI · Brussels and Sacramento

Brussels can start fining the AI labs this morning, and California's labelling law switches on the same day

Two regulators acquire teeth today. As of August 2 the European Commission may investigate general-purpose AI model providers, demand their documentation, run its own technical evaluations, order corrective measures, restrict or withdraw a model from the European market, and fine. The obligations themselves have applied since August 2025; what arrives now is the enforcement machinery behind them, at €15 million or 3 percent of worldwide annual turnover for breaches of the general-purpose and transparency rules, €35 million or 7 percent for prohibited practices, and €7.5 million or 1 percent for supplying misleading information to authorities. The same date turns on : chatbots must say they are not human at the moment of contact, deepfakes must be labelled whether or not the maker meant to deceive, and providers must embed machine-readable marks in synthetic text, image, audio and video. Systems already on the market have until December 2 to comply with the marking requirement.

Two carve-outs are worth the reader's attention, because both will be litigated. Obligations do not attach where the AI interaction is "obvious," a standard one Brussels academic traced to the average-consumer test in EU consumer law and called "vague and very relative," and they do not attach to deepfakes forming part of an "evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous" work. The enforcer is thin: the has 145 staff across six teams, only 34 of them on regulation and compliance. The industry has split on the voluntary code that demonstrates compliance: Amazon, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, Mistral and OpenAI signed the general-purpose code, X signed only its safety and security chapter, and Meta did not sign at all. Note also what did not arrive, since Monday's client alerts will get this wrong: the high-risk obligations under Annex III were pushed to December 2027 by the digital omnibus signed on July 8. Across the Atlantic and deliberately synchronised, becomes operative today, binding generative AI providers with more than a million monthly California users, enforced by the attorney general, a city attorney or county counsel at $5,000 a violation.

Why it mattersThis is the first regime anywhere that can price a model provider's disclosure failure as a share of global revenue, and the artistic and satirical exemption is a genuine entertainment-law question dressed as a technical footnote. Whoever draws the line between a labelled deepfake and an unlabelled parody will be doing EASL work, in Brussels, for years.

Entertainment & EASL · Zurich

FIFA kills its $4.2 billion sale of the World Cup, and UEFA declares no confidence in Infantino

FIFA abandoned the largest private-capital transaction ever attempted in sport on Friday night, four days after it was forced to confirm the plan existed. The vehicle was , a $20 billion holding company that would have folded FIFA's media rights, sponsorships, ticketing, licensing and tournament operations into one entity, with roughly a fifth of it sold for $4.2 billion to a group led by , the holding company of Joshua Kushner. FIFA insisted the investors would take minority stakes with no operational role. The inducement to the membership was cash: $20 million per national association in the coming World Cup cycle, and another $20 million for those who signed on, with a September 19 deadline to accept.

The arithmetic killed it before any ballot. UEFA's 55 federations voted Thursday to boycott all FIFA tournaments while the proposal stood, CONCACAF's members rejected it outright, and the Asian confederation registered serious concern. Those two confederations alone accounted for 90 votes against, and needed 106 to pass. He withdrew it Friday, saying the project "has created divisions of a nature that, regardless of the level of support, are no longer in the interest of the objective set out in the first place." That did not settle it. UEFA came back Saturday morning with a statement of no confidence, noting that FIFA already holds more than $5 billion in cash reserves and declaring that "we cannot keep going on like this with secret schemes on fast track timescales, cooked up by faceless individuals and of dubious benefit to the game." CONCACAF was blunter, calling for "a comprehensive reckoning with this presidency."

Why it mattersA members' association tried to sell a fifth of its commercial future to private capital and its own members valued the offer below the price of a revolt, which is the clearest read yet on where governance power in world football actually sits. For anyone drafting downstream rights agreements, note what nearly happened: media, ticketing and licensing were about to be severed from the governing body and placed inside a company with outside shareholders.

Law & the Courts · Denver

A Colorado judge certified a nationwide class in three weeks and handed the class of 2022 a fifth year

Judge Charlotte N. Sweeney of the District of Colorado issued two orders late Friday in Wisne v. NCAA. She certified a nationwide class of Division I athletes who began college play in 2022-23 and exhausted four years of eligibility by this spring, and in a 50-page opinion she enjoined the NCAA from stopping them playing a fifth season. The suit was filed on July 8. therefore took about three weeks, against the one to four years it consumed in O'Bannon, Alston and House. The grievance is a drafting artefact: the NCAA cabinet approved an age-based rule in June granting up to five years to complete five seasons, with the clock starting at a player's nineteenth birthday or enrolment, and declined to make it retroactive to the athletes who graduated high school in 2022, citing roster chaos. The plaintiffs, led by former Northern Colorado basketball player Brock Wisne and eleven others, pleaded that as a horizontal agreement among the association and its members not to compete for athletic services, costing them name, image and likeness income and revenue-share payments.

The NCAA's chief legal officer, Scott Bearby, called the rulings "egregiously wrong" and made "with virtually no evidence, and without witness testimony or even so much as a hearing," which previews the Tenth Circuit appeal: speed, the leap past a temporary restraining order straight to a , and commonality across athletes with wildly different NIL economics. Note also what Sweeney did not enjoin: the NCAA's survives, so anyone who takes the fifth year does so with vacatur risk attached. The counterattack was already moving the same evening. The SEC and Big Ten, which had objected to the over its media-rights language, announced they now support it. The bill would give the NCAA antitrust immunity on eligibility and moot the class outright, and John Thune could bring it up before the Senate leaves on August 7.

Why it mattersFront Office Sports puts the reach at tens of thousands of athletes, but the interesting fight is the race between a court and a statute. Plaintiffs' counsel Rob Shelquist said the exclusion of his clients looked like "political expediency to publicize the need for legislative action," and if the Senate moves before the Tenth Circuit does, a class certified in three weeks is extinguished in five. That is the shape of modern sports antitrust: win the injunction, lose the immunity.

Markets & Deals · New York

SpaceX reports earnings Tuesday, and two days later 911 million insider shares come free

SpaceX reports second-quarter results after the close on Tuesday, its first as a public company, and the release is not merely an earnings event. It is the trigger on a . Rather than a single 180-day cliff, SpaceX drafted a staggered structure with sixteen separate release dates, and the first substantial one keys off the earnings release rather than the calendar: beginning Thursday, insiders may sell up to 20 percent of restricted holdings, as much as 911.5 million shares, worth roughly $123 billion at recent prices. That is more stock than currently trades. SpaceX listed with a of under 5 percent of shares outstanding, and reckons Thursday will more than double the tradable float.

The price history explains why anyone cares. SpaceX priced on June 11 at $135.00 for 555,555,555 Class A shares, raising $75 billion, the largest initial public offering ever completed and roughly three times Saudi Aramco's $24.9 billion in 2019. It opened at $150 on June 12 and closed its first day at $160.95, up 19 percent, ran to an all-time high near $225, and then traded below its issue price in mid-July. It fell 4 percent on July 27 as the unlock came into view. A word of caution on the arithmetic circulating in secondary coverage: at least one syndicated item puts the June raise at $85.7 billion, while SpaceX's own pricing announcement and the contemporaneous wire reporting all say $75 billion at $135.

Why it mattersThe structure is the lesson, not the ticker. When you float under 5 percent of a company, a conventional 180-day cliff hands the market a single detonation, so you buy sixteen smaller ones and tie the first to a disclosure event so the selling happens into a fully informed tape. Anyone who ends up drafting an underwriting agreement for a scarce-float listing will be reading this document.

Markets & Deals · South Florida · Dania Beach

A Boston fund bid $88 million for the headquarters Spirit Airlines built for $275 million

Hill City Capital has offered $88 million in cash for Spirit Airlines' former corporate campus in Dania Beach, entering the bankruptcy auction as through an affiliate, DPC HoldCo LLC. The asset is eight and a third acres at 1731 Radiant Drive holding roughly 615,600 square feet across four buildings: a six-storey, 180,000-square-foot office built for more than a thousand employees, an amenity building, an in-flight and flight-operations training facility, and housing for up to 400 visiting workers. It opened in 2024 and cost $275 million to build. Spirit's own bankruptcy schedules carry the land at $33 million and the buildings at $288 million. The Broward County Property Appraiser puts market value near $133 million. The bid is roughly a 73 percent discount to what the debtor says the buildings are worth.

The terms are worth reading as a template. If Hill City is outbid it takes a break fee of 3 percent of the purchase price plus expense reimbursement capped at $500,000, and it would let Spirit keep a small office in the building to wind itself down through the end of October. The auction has already slipped twice, from July 22 to August 4 and now to August 11, with a sale approval hearing set for September 16. Spirit filed for Chapter 11 in November 2024 and shut down in May, putting 17,000 people out of work; Broward County commissioners weighed bidding to move county offices in and abandoned the idea on June 16 over zoning uncertainty. The local footnote is the good one: , founded in 2019 and run by Chip Frazier out of Boston, discloses a $693 million position in Coral Gables-based MasTec as its single largest holding in its most recent .

Why it mattersThis is what a purpose-built corporate campus is worth once the tenant no longer exists, marked to an actual bid rather than a schedule: about a third of construction cost. Every South Florida developer selling single-tenant office on a credit story should read the August 11 result, and the Hill City name is worth filing, because the same fund's biggest disclosed position is a Coral Gables public company.

Artificial Intelligence

Washington's frontier-AI framework was due Saturday and, as of the weekend, had not appeared. Executive Order 14409, signed June 2, gave agencies sixty days to publish a classified benchmarking process defining which systems count as covered frontier models, a voluntary disclosure framework giving government up to thirty days of pre-release access, and a federal cyber workforce plan. Forkast reported Friday night that none had issued. The absence matters because of what is waiting behind it: Bloomberg reported on July 17 that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent helped develop a proposal for an independent frontier-AI regulator modelled on FINRA and reporting to the SEC, screening models for deception, bioweapon uplift and hacking capability. Law & the Courts

Justice Jackson set a Monday afternoon deadline in the mail-ballot fight, and the Supreme Court's courtroom goes dark the same day. The administration applied on July 27 in Trump v. California, No. 26A124, to implement Executive Order 14399 in full after Judge Indira Talwani enjoined parts of it and the First Circuit upheld her on July 25; twelve states led by Alabama filed in parallel the next day. Ketanji Brown Jackson, circuit justice for the First Circuit, declined to grant immediate relief and instead ordered the challenging states to respond by 4 p.m. Monday. The Court has meanwhile posted that its courtroom is closed from Monday to August 31. · SCOTUSblog, Bloomberg Law, The Epoch Times

A Springfield judge told the Justice Department it has no auditing power over state elections, and made it permanent. Judge Colleen Lawless of the Central District of Illinois dismissed the government's suit against the Illinois State Board of Elections with prejudice on Friday in a 14-page opinion, holding that a demand for the unredacted voter file of more than eight million residents "to ascertain Illinois's compliance" with federal law "does not relate to any suspected violation of voting." Her structural line is the one that will be quoted: "Congress did not confer a general auditing power to the DOJ over state election officials." Were it otherwise, she wrote, the statutory requirement that the attorney general state his purpose "would be pointless." Illinois is the nineteenth consecutive state to defeat one of these suits in the past year. · Chicago Sun-Times, Bloomberg Law, Newsweek

The ABA's House of Delegates sits Monday with 45 resolutions and an accreditation monopoly coming apart underneath it. The annual conference has been running in Chicago since July 29; delegates take up policy on discrimination, independence of the profession, habeas corpus and immigration enforcement. The structural story is quieter. The legal education council suspended Standard 206 in May 2025, extended the suspension to August 2027 in February and sent a repeal out for comment, then appointed a committee to review the accreditation standards wholesale, over the objection of 52 law school deans who asked it to slow down. In January the Texas and Florida supreme courts stopped requiring bar applicants to have graduated from an ABA-accredited school, Texas after four decades. The body that licenses the licensers is being quietly demoted. · ABA Journal, ABA Section of Legal Education

The World

Italy suspended Schengen free movement with Spain, and Madrid called it a breach of European law. After the Ceuta crossings, in which at least 67 people died, some drowned and others crushed against a breakwater, and between 50,000 and 60,000 crossed before the Spanish army intervened, Rome suspended passport-free travel with a fellow member state. Pedro Sánchez wrote to Ursula von der Leyen, António Costa and Micheal Martin demanding an emergency meeting of all 27 interior ministers, arguing that Ceuta is not in the Schengen Area at all, that virtually everyone who crossed has been returned to Morocco, and that Frontex rates Spain among the least porous external borders in the Union. The meeting was called unanimously on Saturday and sits Tuesday. Giorgia Meloni meanwhile announced a letter signed by 22 member states calling for stronger borders. The coverage split cleanly: Fox led on a socialist prime minister attacking a neighbour who restored border controls, the Times on a far right that now sets the agenda, and the BBC on a political storm whipped up by social media. · The Guardian, Associated Press, Fox News, The New York Times

Ukraine stopped one ballistic missile out of 27 over Kyiv, and Trump walked back the Patriot licence. Russia fired 35 missiles and 185 attack drones before dawn Saturday, killing nine and wounding 28 including four children, according to Mayor Vitali Klitschko. Zelensky's arithmetic is the story: of the 27 ballistic missiles, exactly one was intercepted, because the Patriot stock is the only thing Ukraine has that can touch them and it is nearly gone. At a cabinet meeting at Camp David on Friday, Trump was asked whether Ukraine would be allowed to manufacture Patriots and said "we have not agreed to that," adding that "it's a hard thing to give away that kind of technology." Zelensky had left the White House on July 28 expecting a licence and had met Lockheed Martin executives. The Institute for the Study of War assesses that Russia fired more missiles in July than it manufactures in a month. · NPR, Associated Press, Kyiv Post

The device went off around 8 p.m. Saturday at Balzi Rossi, which had been closed to the public for a private function. Iraq and Turkey reopened the Kirkuk pipeline to move 750,000 barrels a day around Hormuz. The two signed on Saturday to raise exports through the line to Ceyhan on the Turkish Mediterranean, with a minimum of 750,000 barrels a day and a one-year term while a wider framework covering oil, electricity and water is negotiated. The pipeline has been largely idle since 2023 after legal and commercial disputes halted exports from the Kurdish region. Before the war Iraq shipped roughly 3.5 million barrels a day, most of it out of southern terminals through the strait. Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi called it "an important strategic milestone to ensure the uninterrupted flow of our oil exports." This is the quiet consequence of the splash above: while Washington and Tehran argue about reopening Hormuz, the region's second-largest OPEC producer is paying to stop needing it. · Associated Press
