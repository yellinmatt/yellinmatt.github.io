A bankruptcy judge lets LIV Golf reject every player contract except Jon Rahm’s

A bankruptcy judge in New Jersey on Wednesday let LIV Golf reject every player contract but one. Judge approved the league’s motion and carved out Jon Rahm, whose contract was adjourned to a November 5 hearing, Front Office Sports reported. Rahm’s lawyer, John Beck of Dentons, told the court his client will not play in the relaunched league, known as LIV 2.0.

Mr Rahm has independently reviewed the proposed terms of LIV 2.0 and has determined that those terms are unacceptable to him.
— John Beck of Dentons, counsel to Jon Rahm, in court, October 7

Beck said the two sides are in “advanced discussions for a consensual separation agreement,” to be filed by October 15 under . If the talks fail, Rahm will press his objection. He is reported to be owed more than $100 million in future guarantees, and LIV’s schedules list him at $7.47 million, the largest unsecured claim in the case, according to Sportico. Sergio Garcia did not oppose his release but asked that be defined as a termination.

The rescue now turns on who stays. signed an amended on Monday offering $300 million of exit financing and a $4 million loan for the case. The deadline for players to commit moved to October 25 from October 13, and the number required is now at the firm’s sole discretion. “The next 22 days is going to be about signing up the players,” said Ted Goldthorpe of BC Partners Credit. LIV filed on September 8 with $15 million of cash.

Why it mattersRejection is a court-approved breach, so most players become unsecured creditors of an estate with little to pay them. Rahm kept his objection alive and is trading it for a negotiated exit, while the sponsor can now walk whatever the head count.

A judge weighs whether a Polymarket bet is a swap in the first insider case of its kind

A federal judge in Manhattan heard argument Wednesday on whether to dismiss the first insider-trading prosecution built on a prediction market. Master Sgt. Gannon Ken Van Dyke of the Army Special Forces is accused of placing about $34,000 of Polymarket bets in the days before the capture of Nicolás Maduro. The positions were worth more than $400,000 when the markets resolved. He has pleaded not guilty to five counts, including wire and commodities fraud.

The defense says an event contract is not a and that prosecutors have adopted a “limitless definition of what a swap is,” Courthouse News reported. Judge asked whether the defense had to “convince me that no prediction market bet is a swap under the meaning of the CEA.” On the fraud counts, defense lawyer Zach Intrater argued that secret military plans are not : “How is the government injured?” Garnett did not rule.

Why it mattersIf an event contract is a swap, insider-trading law follows prediction markets wherever they list, and every trading desk’s compliance manual needs a chapter. If not, Congress has a gap.

Miami chairman of a failed Puerto Rican bank gets 62 months for looting it

Juan Francisco Ramirez, 60, of Miami, was sentenced Wednesday in the Southern District of Florida to 62 months for a wire fraud conspiracy at Nodus International Bank, a Puerto Rican he chaired and co-owned. The Justice Department put the loss at $23.6 million. The bank bought at least 47 promissory notes, about $25.3 million, from a Miami finance company he co-owned. Why it mattersThe last step came after the regulator had called time. Insiders swapped their own bad paper into a dying bank to cancel what they owed it.

The Copyright Office opens an inquiry into streaming fraud and its link to AI music

The U.S. Copyright Office on Wednesday published a into music streaming fraud, asking how bot farms, click farms and playlist manipulation work and what they cost. Comments are due November 23 and replies December 21. The inquiry follows a May 21 letter from Representative Scott Fitzgerald of the House Judiciary Committee asking the office to “examine the prevalence of streaming fraud across digital platforms.”

Ten questions cover the connection between fraud and AI-generated music, the role of distributors, clawbacks, a shared database, criminal enforcement, and whether “no-fault injunctive relief” could help. The notice calls the primary harm “the dilution of the royalty revenue pool.” Its footnotes cite Universal’s suit against , Music Business Worldwide reported. A 2025 inquiry into , also prompted by Fitzgerald, ended without policy recommendations.

Why it mattersNo-fault injunctions are the blocking remedy rights holders have sought for years, now reframed as fraud control. The comment record is where labels, distributors and services set their positions before any bill.
