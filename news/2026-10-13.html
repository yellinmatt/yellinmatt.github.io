Character.AI files for bankruptcy, and Disney opens the bidding at $20 million for a license to what is left

Character Technologies, the company behind the chatbot app Character.AI, filed for Chapter 11 in Delaware on Monday to run a court-supervised of “all or substantially all” of its assets. Its is Disney, which has agreed to pay $20 million for a non-exclusive, perpetual license to certain of its intellectual property and claims, plus help from former Character.AI staff it has already hired. Disney hired its former chief executive, Karandeep Anand, as technology chief last month. The company expects the sale to close by early December; the app stays up for now but “will be wound down” if no viable bid emerges.

The first filings show the estate. Google is the largest unsecured creditor, owed about $4.1 million in trade payables, Business Insider reported, and and Pennsylvania are listed as litigation claimants. A Tuesday motion counts “approximately 163 million” current and past users and says none is a creditor owed . They get a blog post.

The papers disagree on the ending: one motion speaks of “value-maximizing wind-down activities,” another of “successfully emerging from chapter 11.” The motion caps such payments at $2.5 million, less than Google’s claim, and warns the company “risks losing all of the data, code, intellectual property” if cloud providers cut it off.

Why it mattersThis is the acqui-hire run through a bankruptcy court: Disney took the people, then buys a license under a judge’s order, the cleanest shield from the litigation that sank the company.

Albertsons asks a Delaware court for $9.3 billion from Kroger over the merger the FTC blocked

Albertsons told the Delaware Court of Chancery on Monday that Kroger owes it “more than $9.3 billion,” Bloomberg reported: the premium its shareholders would have received had their $24.6 billion merger closed, a $600 million termination fee, and $75 million in interest and costs. A federal court blocked the deal for the FTC in December 2024. A two-week trial before Vice Chancellor Lori Will is set for later this month. Kroger did not immediately comment.

The case turns on one . Kroger agreed to remove “any antitrust impediment to the merger,” Albertsons says, “with no limit except that Kroger did not have to divest more than 650 stores.” “Kroger breached these maximalist contractual commitments,” the filing says. Kroger says Albertsons secretly worked with to push regulators toward bigger divestitures. Cerberus, the private equity firm, owns more than 30 percent of Albertsons and stands to collect most of any award.

Why it mattersA willful-breach finding is the prize: it turns a merger agreement’s fee from a ceiling into a floor.
