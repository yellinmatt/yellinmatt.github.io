Paramount buys its way past twelve attorneys general with a Miramax hostage and $30 million a film

California Attorney General and eleven counterparts filed a Monday resolving their July suit against Paramount Skydance, clearing the last obstacle to a roughly $111 billion acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery. The states had alleged the merger would lower output and raise prices; trial was set for March 2027 before Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín in Oakland. Chief executive David Ellison told staff he expects to close in "approximately two weeks." The settlement is pending court approval, and Bonta opened his press conference by refusing to call it an endorsement.

The remedy is unusually specific for a state settlement, and the enforcement clauses are the part a deal lawyer reads first. Paramount commits to release 30 films a year including 20 wide releases in years one and two, rising to 32 and 21 in years three through five, with at least four independent films annually. Miss the output floor and the company pays $30 million per missed film into the WGA, IATSE, DGA and Teamsters health and retirement trusts and to the National Association of Attorneys General, and must divest Studios. Domestic production spending rises by at least $1.5 billion over five years against 2025 levels; roughly 5 percent of Paramount's production is currently domestic, and a federal film credit of 20 percent or more would ratchet the requirement to 20 percent of output in years one and two and 30 percent thereafter. A separate obligation to negotiate Paramount and Warner Bros. basic cable carriage independently is backed by the threat of divesting BET, VH1 and Comedy Central.

What forced it was arithmetic rather than law. The merger agreement gave Warner Bros. Discovery holders an additional 25 cents a share each quarter after September 30, a worth an estimated $650 million a quarter, and the litigation would have run into mid-2027. Paramount also settled separately with the Writers Guild, agreeing to no writer layoffs at CBS News Broadcast for five years plus $17.5 million to the guild health fund. The package adds a $47.5 million workforce fund over five years, a $5 million annual independent film acquisition fund, a News Editorial Independence Board covering CNN and CBS News, and an independent . Warner Bros. Discovery shares rose 10.7 percent; Paramount fell about 3 percent after Bonta spoke.

"This settlement is not a vote of support for this merger."

Why it mattersThis is the template for the post-federal era of merger review: twelve state enforcers extracting a five-year output covenant, a penalty schedule payable to union trust funds, and two divestiture triggers on a deal Washington had already let through. For anyone heading into capital markets, the lesson is that the ticking fee did the work the antitrust brief could not, and that is now a negotiating fact.

The Netherlands is told to plan for an International Criminal Court that cannot pay its own staff

Dutch officials have been formally notified that entity-wide American sanctions on the International Criminal Court are imminent, and the host state is examining how to keep the tribunal running by paying staff itself, protecting witnesses and maintaining the detention facility, the Associated Press reported Monday. Two sources said US officials warned the measures could land as early as Monday and encouraged the Netherlands to withdraw from the institution. Two US officials told the AP that more sanctions may be coming but that the scope is not final and no announcement is expected this week. The Wall Street Journal reported Sunday that the measures would prohibit most transactions with the court after a grace period of six to seven months.

An entity designation would bar US persons and companies from supplying funds, goods or services to the court absent a licence. Washington has already sanctioned more than a dozen current and former staff, including nine of the eighteen sitting judges, both deputy prosecutors and the former chief prosecutor, who lost his Microsoft email, had bank accounts frozen and was barred from entry. The court has been hardening for months, migrating off Microsoft products to a German provider and switching insurance and financial providers to firms without a US footprint. Dutch and court officials are pressing for six months to sever from American vendors. The can shield European staff by barring EU companies from complying, but it is unclear whether it covers an international body rather than a European one. The court, seated in since 2002, has 125 member states; Venezuela and Chad have announced withdrawals in recent months.

Framing noteThe AP describes an institution being dismantled, reporting that the sanctions could "prevent the court from accessing financial and IT services, and even paying its U.S. employees." Secretary of State Marco Rubio, quoted across tiers, describes a rogue tribunal being checked: the court is "a corrupt and fatally politicized supranational court that has maliciously abused its authority and exceeded its mandate." Neither side disputes the mechanics.

Why it mattersThis is sanctions law used against an institution rather than a person, and the unresolved question is whether an entity designation can reach a treaty body that 125 states fund. Watch the licensing structure: a six-month wind-down would tell you Treasury intends a vendor severance rather than a shutdown, and that distinction is the whole compliance problem for every European bank and insurer touching the court.

OpenAI asks Washington to write the world's rules for self-improving AI, hours before the General Assembly opens

OpenAI published a policy document Monday, timed to the opening of the high-level week, asking that "the United States should lead an effort to work together with countries around the world to develop global technical standards for frontier AI, including for RSI." is defined in the post as AI systems taking over successive generations of AI development, and the company states that fully autonomous RSI "is not happening today, and we should not pursue it unless and until it can be done safely." The failure it names is humans "unable to provide oversight on research processes they no longer understand."

Two mechanisms are proposed. Standards would route through the existing safety-institute network, naming Australia, Canada, Germany, France, Kenya, Japan, Korea, Singapore, India and the United Kingdom, coordinated through ; and common measurement and incident-reporting protocols would set triggers for mandatory human review of automated research plus "common incident severity levels and reporting thresholds." The carve-out is doing the real work: these "would not be licenses, mandatory prerelease review, or approval requirements," and national governments decide whether to adopt them. The analogy offered is aviation and financial stability. Named counterparties include ISO, the and the Agentic AI Foundation. Sam Altman briefs the Security Council on Wednesday in a session convened by France, chaired by Foreign Minister ; the Council last took up AI in 2023.

Why it mattersRead the ask as a compliance architecture rather than a safety statement. Incident severity levels and reporting thresholds are the raw material of a disclosure regime, and disclosure regimes are what firms build practice groups around. The aviation and financial-stability analogy is not accidental: it is the argument that measurement standards can bind without a licensing statute behind them.

AI-adopting firms are hiring, and the new jobs are going to people who already had one

A study released Monday by Bharat Chandar of the and Bouke Klein Teeselink of King's College London finds that at firms adopting AI, senior headcount rose 6.7 percent over five years while junior employment fell 3 percent. The junior share of headcount at those firms dropped 1.9 percentage points even as total hiring rose. The dataset is 1.25 billion job postings and 154 million employment records across 41 countries, January 2021 through March 2026. The authors' formulation is that AI "is labor saving for junior workers and labor expanding for seniors in exposed occupations."

The pattern replicates in economies as unlike each other as Brazil, Saudi Arabia and the United Kingdom, which is the paper's argument against a US-specific or business-cycle explanation. One line deserves attention from anyone entering a rich-country professional market: junior employment losses "run somewhat deeper in richer and more digitized economies." The aggregate finding is genuinely positive, since adopting firms added more jobs overall than non-adopters. The damage is compositional. is an empirical finance economist, and the framing shows: this is a study about how firms allocate work internally, not about whether machines can do it. Standard measures put legal work near the top of the exposed set.

Why it mattersA 1.9-point compression in the junior share is not a staffing detail in a law firm, it is the denominator of the . This is the first multi-country measurement of the thing everyone has been asserting, and it lands the same day OpenAI told the UN that automated research is the capability worth governing.

Amazon locks Meta's shopping agent out of its store, and Meta closes up eleven percent anyway

Amazon blocked Meta's Muse agent from Amazon.com, notifying users late Sunday with a popup reading that "continued access by an unauthorized AI agent violates Amazon's Conditions of Use, to which our customers have agreed." The technical grievance is specific: Amazon's terms require agents to self-identify by embedding a text snippet in HTTP requests, and Muse does not. The data grievance is that Muse can read a user's account pages and purchase history on prompt, which Amazon characterises as an undisclosed third party moving through customer accounts. Amazon says it asked Meta to exclude Amazon.com from Muse's scope voluntarily and was refused.

Muse launched September 8 and passed 2.5 million downloads by Monday, overtaking ChatGPT as the top free iPhone app on Friday. Each instance runs in an isolated virtual machine with a second agent reviewing sensitive actions and blocking access to card numbers and credentials. The precedent is instructive: Amazon sued Perplexity in 2025 over its agentic browser and an appeals court dismissed the case in August, while expressly declining to bar Amazon from blocking agents that breach its terms. Amazon is exercising the reserved self-help remedy rather than litigating again, and the reason it fights on contract rather than computer-fraud grounds is . Meta closed up more than 11 percent; Intel, AMD and Arm all posted double-digit gains on the read-through that agents burn processor cycles. Meta Connect opens Wednesday.

Why it mattersAmazon's Conditions of Use are a agreement, historically the flimsiest thing in contract law, and it is now the load-bearing instrument in the fight over who may transact on whose platform. Nobody has answered the prior question either: in , who is the buyer.

Eighth Circuit orders Missouri onto the map its own supreme court voided, then pauses seven days for Washington

A divided panel held Monday in Onder v. Missouri, No. 26-2797, that the state's plan to use its 2022 congressional map in November violates the Constitution, and that only the 2025 map complies. Judges James Loken and Justin Smith joined a per curiam opinion; concurred in part and dissented in part, arguing the intervenors lacked standing. The constitutional hook is Article I, Section 2: "the right to participate in the procedure of choosing a member of Congress requires continuity in the constituency throughout all stages of a single election." The panel declined to reach the Equal Protection and Elections Clause claims while calling the district court's Equal Protection analysis compelling.

The permanent injunction is stayed to September 28, a window built for the Supreme Court. Mootness failed because Missouri's absentee balloting begins Tuesday and had not yet arrived. The was rejected on the ground that the 2025 map is the status quo, since candidates filed under it and more than 1.2 million voters cast August primary ballots in those districts; the panel blamed any confusion on "the last-minute decision by a state court to switch the congressional map." Roughly 1.4 million Missourians sit in different districts under the two maps, and about 284,000 August primary voters would receive a general-election ballot for a different seat. The order then invokes the Supremacy Clause at a state high court directly, saying it trusts the Missouri Supreme Court will respect the decision. The defendant is Secretary of State ; People Not Politicians, represented by the , will seek emergency review. Justice Kavanaugh denied Missouri's emergency application on September 8 before the district court issued the order now affirmed.

Why it mattersA federal panel has told a state supreme court it may not change a congressional map mid-election, and given the Supreme Court exactly one week to disagree. The doctrinal move worth watching is the recharacterisation of the status quo: whichever map the court calls the baseline decides who bears the Purcell burden, and that framing did all the work here.
