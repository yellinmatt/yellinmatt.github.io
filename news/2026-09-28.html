Seventeen basis points: the two-year has almost caught the ten-year, and the bond market is changing the subject from inflation to recession

The gap between the two- and ten-year Treasury note narrowed to as little as last week, the slimmest since early 2025 and a whisker from inverting. In Monday’s Asian session the two-year yielded about 4.90 percent against a ten-year near 5.21, the thirty-year at 5.53, highest since 2004. What changed is the direction of travel. A month ago the curve steepened as the long end sold off on doubts about the inflation-fighting credibility of Chairman . Since the September 16 increase the short maturities have led, and traders price three more quarter-point rises inside a year.

“It’s raising rates too much, and it’s going to have to cut them hard in the future.”
— Jamie Patton, co-head of global rates, TCW Group, September 28

The spread carries weight because of its record: an inverted two-year to ten-year curve has preceded each of the last eight American recessions since the 1960s, at an average lag of about fifteen months. Against that sits 2022, when it inverted and no downturn came. The , which the Fed’s own economists prefer, is still steep. Zach Griffiths at said flattening this sharp “calls into question the idea that the economy is very strong.”

Equity transmission is visible: the fell into a technical correction last week, down about ten percent, as the compressed spread squeezed banks that borrow short and lend long. Odds of an October hike sat near 70 percent against 8.8 percent a month ago. The regime change is dated: before the Iran war began in February, traders priced cuts.

Why it mattersThis is the discount rate under every model on the desk, and it has moved further in a month than most deal assumptions move in a year. The Fed says it is fighting inflation; the two-year says it will overdo it and reverse.

The tariff lists arrive, and soybeans are not on them

Washington and Beijing published the product lists on Monday, turning the summit framework into tariff lines. China’s commerce ministry called it “a reciprocal tariff reduction framework of $30 billion for $30 billion,” which most wires rendered as $60 billion two-way. The Chinese list names 77 categories, the American one more than 1,600 products. Read the contents and the ambition shrinks. China will take poultry, dairy, eggs, peanuts, silk and pure-breed breeding horses; the United States, microwave ovens, fish hooks, artificial flowers and Christmas decorations. were left off; corn, wheat, meat and dairy made it.

USTR said it would “improve market access for about 30 percent of US exports to China.” Deborah Elms of the Hinrich Foundation: “both sides have largely listed goods that do not move the needle.” Next contacts are in November and the G20 in Miami in December.

Why it mattersA summit produces a communique; a list produces customs classifications, and only one is tradable. The soybean omission answers whether decoupling is reversing or merely being decorated.

Switzerland declines to freeze its own neutrality, 70.1 percent against and not one canton in favour

Swiss voters rejected the “Safeguard Neutrality” on Sunday by 70.1 percent, on turnout near 47 percent, carrying no canton at all. The text would have written a “perpetual and armed” neutrality into the constitution, barred NATO cooperation, and confined Switzerland to sanctions approved by the UN Security Council. That last clause was the operative one: it would have stripped Bern of the power to adopt EU sanctions on Russia by its own authority, which is what it did after 2022 and what started the campaign.

Behind it stood , with members of the . The real innovation was never the word “armed” but fixing the sanctions definition where parliament could not adjust it. Two figures circulate and should not be blended: 70.1 percent is final, and the earlier GFS Bern projection had it near 71.

Why it mattersSwiss sanctions discretion is load-bearing for commodity trading, private banking and Russian-asset compliance, and the country that clears much of that trade just kept the instrument.

Eight Marines wounded on September 14, and the Pentagon never said so

An Iranian struck a US-contracted vessel in the Strait of Hormuz on September 14, injuring eight Marines. The Pentagon never announced it. Fox reported it Sunday; NBC then got three officials to describe seven enlisted among them, smoke inhalation and possible traumatic brain injuries, all eight back on duty. The vessel was not a Navy ship, and declined to discuss how it defends Gulf shipping. The figure that gives the disclosure its weight is in the Pentagon’s own database: more than 800 American servicemembers injured in this conflict this year.

Why it mattersA campaign framed as low-cost has produced eight hundred recorded injuries and a strike the department did not announce. That is a disclosure question before it is a war-powers one.
