OpenAI points ChatGPT at the junior banker, with Morgan Stanley and Evercore as design partners

OpenAI released ChatGPT for Financial Services on Thursday, a vertical build of its enterprise product designed with and and running on . It bundles licensed feeds from , PitchBook, LSEG News and Crunchbase, plus hooks into a bank's own entitlement systems, and ships with workflows for valuation analysis, , buyer screening, earnings analysis and pitchbook preparation. It generates PowerPoint decks in the client firm's own template.

Nick Turley, OpenAI's vice president of product, told CNBC the company is "effectively teaching ChatGPT to research like an analyst and back up its conclusions like an analyst as well," and called the product "the canonical product we are hoping the industry adopts." He reached for the hours directly, noting that analysts and bankers "are working 100-hour weeks," and compared the intended effect to the way Microsoft Excel transformed the industry. OpenAI named no bank that has actually signed a commercial agreement, only the two design partners, and Turley declined to identify further customers beyond saying there was "a ton of demand."

Pricing was not disclosed. The detail that matters more is structural: the product is built on rather than shipping as a standalone application, so it reaches an analyst through a licence the firm has already bought and an administrator already controls. That makes adoption a procurement decision made above the desk rather than a tool a first-year chooses. It lands three days after UBS told its incoming 2027 analyst class that fluency with these systems is now part of the hiring bar.

"We're effectively teaching ChatGPT to research like an analyst."
— Nick Turley, OpenAI, to CNBC, September 10

Why it mattersThe tasks named in the launch are precisely the ones a first-year analyst is paid to perform and a second-year associate is paid to check. Sold through an enterprise licence rather than per seat, it is not a tool anyone at the bottom of the pyramid gets to decline. The open question for the class of 2027 is what the first two years are now for.

The SEC's own advisory committee asks the Commission for rules on AI-written disclosure

The SEC's met at headquarters on Thursday morning for a public session on artificial intelligence and the public-markets information ecosystem, paired with a second panel on . Commissioner Mark Uyeda told the committee that downloads of from the Commission's own website have risen sharply, and attributed part of the increase to participants now able to process filings at a scale that was not previously available.

Commissioner put the harder question to the panel: how extensively issuers already use these systems to draft or check their disclosure, and whether doing so improves accuracy or simply yields more inaccurate boilerplate faster. Supreme Court blocks Missouri's new map a second time in three days, with no noted dissents

In People Not Politicians v. Onder, No. 26A326, the Supreme Court on Thursday morning stayed the September 8 temporary restraining order entered by Chief Judge Stephen Clark of the Eastern District of Missouri, holding it pending disposition of the Eighth Circuit appeal and any petition for certiorari. The order was unsigned, brief, and carried no public dissents. The application reached Justice Kavanaugh as for the Eighth and he referred it to the full Court, two days after denying a parallel application on his own.

The sequence that produced it took a week. The Missouri Supreme Court unanimously voided the new HB1 map on September 3, after a referendum petition carrying more than 300,000 signatures suspended the statute; under the state constitution, a sufficient petition suspends a law on filing rather than on a vote, which is why could argue the map was void rather than merely challenged. Kavanaugh denied Secretary of State 's stay bid on the 8th; under an hour later Clark ordered the state to use HB1 anyway; the Eighth Circuit declined to disturb him on the 9th. Missouri will now run on its 2022 map, with the deadline to mail military and overseas ballots nine days away. Clark, a Trump appointee, had treated HB1 as the status quo five days after the state's highest court held that it "is not the law and has never been the law."

Framing noteFox News leads on the president calling the underlying Missouri ruling "ridiculous," putting the political fight first; the Washington Post leads on the pattern, that this is the second time in three days the Court has turned Missouri Republicans away. Neither disputes a word of the order.

Why it mattersThe question underneath is whether a federal district judge can reinstate a map a state's highest court has held void on . The answer came back in the shortest form the Court has, and unanimously enough that nobody wanted their name on a dissent.

First Circuit keeps the Postal Service's mail-ballot rule blocked through November

A panel of Judges Gustavo Gelpí, Julie Rikelman and Seth Aframe unanimously denied the administration's motion for a on Thursday, leaving Judge 's preliminary injunction intact. The rule at issue imposes barcode design mandates on mail ballots, registration through a Postal Service portal, and ballot-mail verification. The panel wrote that the appellants "have not made a strong showing that the district court erred in determining that the Final Rule is likely unlawful," and agreed it "is likely a regulation of the manner of holding elections for members of Congress."

The panel further agreed with Talwani that implementation "will likely result in the disenfranchisement of millions of voters across the country while providing minimal, if any, gains in combating voter fraud." States remain free to comply voluntarily and the Service may keep building infrastructure for use after November. This is the administration's third trip to the Supreme Court on the rule; the emergency application there, 26A305, is still pending before Justice Jackson, who set a briefing schedule that ran through Wednesday afternoon.

Framing noteFox News foregrounds Republican criticism that Talwani, an Obama appointee, is running out the clock so the rule cannot take effect before the election. CBS and Democracy Docket treat the ruling as a straight holding. The dispute is about the judge's motive, not the reasoning.

Why it mattersThis is the same structural move the Missouri courts made from the opposite direction, and the pairing is the week's real story: two federal courts have now told the executive that administering a congressional election is not an agency function it can assume without Congress saying so.

Thomson Reuters tells the Third Circuit that Washington's fair-use filing does not rescue Ross

In Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v. Ross Intelligence, No. 25-2153, Ross filed a flagging the United States government's in separate copyright litigation against OpenAI, in which Washington said it "has a strong interest in the question whether training AI models on written works constitutes fair use." Thomson Reuters answered on Thursday that a general governmental interest in the question is not an endorsement of Ross's answer to it.

The appeal is the first case about training an AI system on copyrighted material to reach a federal court of appeals. It was argued on June 11 in Philadelphia before Judges Restrepo, Montgomery-Reeves and Bove, who pressed hardest on the first and fourth fair-use factors. Below, the district court granted summary judgment to Thomson Reuters over Ross's use of thousands of to train a legal research tool. The same panel decided a related fair-use case, American Society for Testing v. UpCodes, shortly before hearing argument here, and practitioners have been reading it for tells ever since.

Why it mattersEvery pending training suit against the major laboratories is waiting on an appellate holding, and this is the one that lands first. Because the underlying material is editorial summary of public court opinions, the panel has to draw the line between what the law says, which nobody owns, and how a publisher arranged it, which is the thinnest copyright in the building. Whichever way it falls, the reasoning travels.

Saudi Arabia weighs folding EA into Savvy Games, six weeks after the $55 billion take-private closed

Saudi Arabia's is weighing a merger of Electronic Arts with , Bloomberg's Cecilia D'Anastasio and Dinesh Nair reported on Thursday. PIF holds roughly 93 per cent of EA after the $55 billion buyout that closed on August 5, funded with about $36 billion of its own equity and $20 billion of new debt arranged by JPMorgan; Brussels cleared that deal on competition and grounds about six weeks ago. A combination would sit Battlefield, The Sims and Madden alongside Savvy's portfolio, which reaches Pokémon Go and Mobile Legends: Bang Bang.

Any move is expected to wait on Savvy's separate $6 billion purchase of from ByteDance, announced in March. The structuring point is the one worth sitting with: because PIF already owns both companies outright, combining them may be treated as an internal reorganisation rather than a notifiable merger, which would mean no fresh filing in Brussels or at CFIUS for an entity on the scale that drew years of scrutiny to Microsoft's Activision deal. No decision has been taken. PIF and EA declined to comment; Savvy did not respond to Bloomberg.

Why it mattersCommon ownership is doing the work that a merger review would otherwise have to do. Riyadh bought the pieces one at a time, each cleared on its own terms, and the assembly may never face a regulator at all. For anyone who will spend a career structuring around clearance risk, that is the sequence to understand, not the franchises.
