Chapter Three

The cost of capital

Money is never free, and the price of it is the single number that decides which projects a company is allowed to want.

Every business runs on money it did not have to begin with, borrowed from a bank or raised from someone willing to own a piece of the outcome, and each of those two sources charges a different price for the same favour. The lender wants a fixed return and gets paid first; the owner wants an uncertain one and gets paid last, which is exactly why the owner demands more.

Why the blend matters

Because a company usually carries both at once, the price it truly pays is a weighted average of the two, and that blended figure becomes the hurdle every proposal has to clear. A project returning nine percent looks handsome until you learn the money funding it costs eleven, at which point the company is quietly destroying value while reporting a profit.

The useful reframing is that the hurdle rate is not an accounting fact but a statement about opportunity: it is what the same money would have earned somewhere else at the same risk.

Where the intuition breaks

The blend shifts as the mix shifts, so loading up on cheap debt appears to lower the cost of capital right until the added fragility makes the owners demand more, and the saving evaporates. That tension is the whole reason capital structure is argued about rather than calculated.
